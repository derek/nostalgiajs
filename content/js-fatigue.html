The post that gave "JavaScript fatigue" its name was a feature request for React, and seven months later the React team shipped the feature with the author in the credits.

Saul: "How's it going?"
Me: "Fatigued."
Saul: "Family?"
Me: "No, JavaScript."

Eric Clemmons opened a post called "Javascript Fatigue" with that exchange, from a coffee with a friend a few days earlier. The line after it takes the title back: "More accurately, I meant React and the JavaScript ecosystem that comes with it." The post was submitted to Hacker News at 05:44 UTC on December 27, 2015, and the correction did not travel as well as the title.

Clemmons was chief technology officer of HigherEducation.com at the time, going by the CV on Clemmons's own site, and the grievance was a working one. The team had started three new projects that quarter, and each had needed its tooling decided before anybody could build anything.

Hello World in late 2015

React asked for a build step sooner than most libraries did. Its components were written in JSX, which no browser runs, so in practice a React project started with a compiler, and the compiler was Babel. On October 29, 2015 Babel 6 came out under a note from James Kyle calling it "a very disruptive major". Babel had decided to be a platform for tools and no longer just a transpiler, so a fresh install now transformed nothing until you picked plugins or a preset such as es2015 or react. Clemmons was writing two months into that.

Babel was one tool of several. A project also wanted a bundler, a linter and a development server, and Dan Abramov's later description of how they got along is fair to all of them: "Many of those tools are plugin platforms and don't directly acknowledge each other's existence. They leave it up to the users to wire them together." One Hacker News reader of Clemmons's post reported spending a couple of days on "the Node.js gulp-vinyl-browserify-babelify nonsense" before trying Vue.

The people who made React were spared all of this, and I think that is one reason it lasted. Abramov's post says Facebook's practice was to release only code it used itself, and that inside Facebook the linting, transpiling and packaging were done by remote development servers that product engineers never had to configure. A starter kit for outsiders was the one thing that practice ruled out. By the same account, several "React CLI" projects had been started inside Facebook and had failed.

Over Christmas 2015 the complaint surfaced on Twitter. Clemmons's post links to what Christopher Chedeau, who writes as Vjeux, posted the day after the argument: a challenge. The best setup Chedeau had ever used, the post says, was the one from the age of 14. Connect to a server with WinSCP, make a file called test.php, and type:

<?php echo 'Hello World';

Save, wait half a second for the upload, click the file in a browser. Chedeau wanted that for JavaScript and React, and listed the conditions: nothing to set up for a new project, a single file ("No package.json, no .babelrc, no Procfile"), a URL to share, and a page that keeps working six months later.

What Clemmons asked for

The diagnosis comes under the heading "Too Many Tools." and fits in a sentence:

Ultimately, the problem is that by choosing React (and inherently JSX), you've unwittingly opted into a confusing nest of build tools, boilerplate, linters, & time-sinks to deal with before you ever get to create anything.

Then the post turns down the usual remedy. Teams coped by copying a boilerplate project or running a generator such as Yeoman, and Clemmons, who had written a generator, argued that neither held up across many projects. Generated code is a copy. When the setup improves, the improvement has to be carried by hand into every project made from it, because the common code "is not abstracted into an independently updatable dependency."

The rest is a list of things to build instead. Library authors should offer an opinionated, "author-endorsed" wrapper for the 90 percent of cases that look alike. Tools should ship presets, as Babel had just done. File and folder naming conventions should stand in for configuration. And there should be tooling that gets a prototype running first and leaves the decisions for later, which is where the post cites Chedeau's challenge and names five projects already trying: budō, nwb, prot, rackt-cli and rwb.

The forecast is cheerful. Clemmons wrote that the pendulum had swung from restrictive frameworks to modular libraries buried in boilerplate, and would soon settle in between. The last line is "Here's to an exciting 2016! :)". Read from the second heading on, the post is a specification with a complaint on top.

The title leaves the post behind

Hacker News took the post twice inside a day. The first submission used its title. Whoever made the second put the missing word back, as "React/JavaScript fatigue", and that thread ran to 183 comments.

The first objection I can find is to the name. Someone from the same React community, who posts as chantastic, had reacted to the post, Clemmons wrote to them on Twitter, and on December 29 chantastic answered at length in a gist: "Articles titled like yours have a tendency to take a life of their own. The bulk of my reaction was to the title itself and the resulting conversation." The gist agrees that building React apps was painful and disputes the rest: that React's trouble was JavaScript's, that enough React apps resembled one another for a common wrapper to exist yet, and that a wrapper would shield anyone from the churn underneath it.

Clemmons replied underneath the same day with a one-sentence version of the post. It was "not advocating a one-size-fits-all"; the idea was to take the effort then going into yet another boilerplate and spend it on abstractions for the most common cases. Two days after publishing, the author was explaining what the post had said to a reader who agreed with most of it.

By January 18, 2016 the phrase had come loose enough for Ben Halpern to warn against letting it become a buzzword. Halpern still defined it narrowly, as the setup that frameworks, "namely ReactJs", demanded before a first screen. The worry was that "as the phrase diffuses into buzzword-land it is easy for the original context to be lost and for people to infer their own meaning to it."

I think the phrase got away for plain reasons. A title is the one part of a post that circulates without a click, and this title said JavaScript where the body said React. It also arrived when anyone who had upgraded Babel, chosen a bundler or inherited somebody else's Gulp file had a fatigue of their own to file under it. Nothing in the two words told them that the author's was more specific, or that it came with a plan.

One dependency, version 0.1.0

On July 22, 2016 the React blog carried Abramov's "Create Apps with No Configuration". Abramov had been to EmberCamp the week before and come back impressed by Ember's command-line tool, and had agreed with Christopher over Messenger to spend a hackathon week on one for React. The link on that name goes to Vjeux's Twitter account: the author of the Christmas challenge. The npm registry shows the name create-react-app first published on Sunday, July 17, which fits the post's account of a rough proof of concept built over a weekend, and its companion package reaching 0.1.0 on the Friday. Shipping it meant setting aside the practice of releasing only what Facebook used. Abramov called that an exception, and called the tool an experiment: "only time will tell if it becomes a popular way of creating and building React apps, or fades into obscurity."

A new app's package.json looked like this:

{
  "name": "hello-world",
  "dependencies": {
    "react": "^15.2.1",
    "react-dom": "^15.2.1"
  },
  "devDependencies": {
    "react-scripts": "0.1.0"
  },
  "scripts": {
    "start": "react-scripts start",
    "build": "react-scripts build",
    "eject": "react-scripts eject"
  }
}

The project has no webpack config, no .babelrc and no lint file. All of that lives inside react-scripts, which is why the post can promise that "you can update a single dependency to get them all." Clemmons's objection to generators had its answer: the common code was now an independently updatable dependency. The price was stated as plainly. The build settings were fixed and could not be changed, testing was missing, and the way out was eject, an idea credited to Ean Platter's Enclave, which copies the whole configuration into your project and so turns it back into a boilerplate.

The post's "Backstory" section says where the diagnosis came from. "Eric Clemmons called this phenomenon the 'JavaScript Fatigue'", Abramov wrote, and quoted the sentence about the confusing nest of build tools. The closing paragraph thanks six people for "early feedback, ideas, and contributions". One is Clemmons. Another is Jonny Buchanan, whom the registry lists as the author of nwb, one of the five tools on Clemmons's December list. The post does not say Create React App was built in answer to "Javascript Fatigue", and I won't either. It does show that the person who set the challenge co-wrote the tool, and that the person who wrote the complaint was among those thanked for early feedback on it.

A dialogue borrowed from Docker

The year kept supplying material, including the March afternoon when a seventeen-line package vanished from npm and took Babel's install with it. Then on October 3, 2016, ten weeks after Create React App, Jose Aguinaga published "How it feels to learn JavaScript in 2016" and submitted it to Hacker News.

The piece is a conversation. Someone who has not built for the web in a few years needs a page that fetches user activity from a REST endpoint and shows it in a filterable table, and proposes jQuery. The other speaker, who prefers the title "Front End engineer", says no one uses jQuery anymore and prescribes React, which comes with React DOM and JSX, which need Babel, which wants a bundler, and on through Browserify, Webpack, SystemJS, TypeScript, Flow, Fetch and a roll-call of template engines, before conceding that a page which only displays data never needed React. The newcomer gives up on the web altogether: "The JavaScript community is insane if it thinks anyone can keep up with this." Hackernoon's copy showed 739,529 reads when I looked.

Set beside Clemmons's post, the complaint has changed shape. Clemmons objected to the decisions a React project demanded before work could start, which a good default can remove. Aguinaga's newcomer objects to how much there is to know and how fast it is replaced, across the whole language, which no default removes. The word "fatigue" appears nowhere in the dialogue, and in the two Hacker News threads about it, some 420 comments, I count it once. The label was attached afterwards. A year on, in an es-discuss thread that carries Aguinaga's title, one reply offers as the best response an article whose address ends javascript-fatigue-fatigue.

Create React App appears nowhere in the dialogue either. It came up in the comments, where one reader said there was no need to know the toolchains "if you just use create-react-app" and another answered: "Yes, you DO need to know the toolchain." The same doubt is in chantastic's gist from December.

The form was second-hand, and said so. A note at the top credits "It's the future" from CircleCI's blog, a parody by Paul Biggar that Hacker News picked up on June 9, 2015. I could not open the original, which now redirects to the blog's front page. Lines quoted in that thread show the same question-and-answer form, and Biggar's follow-up ten days later describes it as a piece that "satirized the container ecosystem, lightly mocking Docker and Google and CoreOS and a bunch of other technologies". The follow-up is titled "It really is the future". Many readers had taken the parody as the plain truth about a pointless fashion, Biggar wrote, and they were wrong: containers were how applications were going to be built. It also records that readers had compared the parody to the state of front-end development, six months before Clemmons's coffee with Saul.

So Biggar had met Clemmons's problem first. Each wrote about the cost of a toolchain they believed in, and each was read as saying the toolchain was nonsense.

Where the fix and the joke went

Create React App became a category of tool and then was retired. On February 14, 2025 the React blog published "Sunsetting Create React App", by Matt Carroll and Ricky Hanlon, deprecating it for new apps. The post's history of 2016 is Clemmons's argument in the past tense: people had wired the tools together by hand, the community's boilerplates "were difficult to update", and a single recommended configuration had solved that. Its verdict on the design is "This model became so popular that there's an entire category of tools working this way today." The reasons for stopping were that the tool had no active maintainers and that frameworks now covered the same ground. The registry bears out the first: the latest react-scripts, 5.0.1, dates from April 2022. Abramov had offered two outcomes, popular or obscure, and the experiment delivered both, in that order.

The shortest reply to the satire came from the library that became htmx. Two days after Aguinaga's piece, Carson Gross posted a version of the dialogue on the blog of intercooler.js, a library built on jQuery that makes AJAX requests from HTML attributes. It reuses Aguinaga's opening lines and changes the expert, who answers "Nope. I'm just an old web dev guy" and recommends returning bits of HTML from the server. The whole conversation is over in eleven exchanges. Intercooler's home page now carries an announcement that its successor is out, under the name "htmx 1.x (intercooler 2.0)".

The takeaway

"JavaScript fatigue" began as a specific complaint from someone who meant to do something about it. A React project made you assemble a build before you could write a line, and copies of that build could not be upgraded. The remedy the post asked for, an opinionated default endorsed by the library's authors and updated as one dependency, is what Create React App turned out to be, and by the React team's own account that design is now a category of tool. The phrase had a different career. Its title cut it loose from React within weeks, and it became the name for something no package can fix: the sense that there is more of the ecosystem than one person can follow. The piece that best expresses that sense never uses the word.

Clemmons's post is on the author's own site now, coffee with Saul and all. The date above it reads December 27, 2016, a year later than Hacker News recorded it, which gives the last line, "Here's to an exciting 2016! :)", four days to come true.
