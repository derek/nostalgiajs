# Power struggles over shared internet infrastructure (naming, trust, crawling)

How to read the evidence tags on each finding:

- **[summary]**: a search tool or page-summariser reported it; I did not see the page text. Reopen before use.

## Postel: what was asked, when, how many servers, how long, the surrounding fight, Magaziner, the word "test", eyewitnesses, and the ending

### Takeaway
A real story with a primary document at its centre. Postel's PGP-signed directive of 28 January 1998 never uses the word "test"; it calls the change "a small step" in a transition, and "test" appears only in the press statement and in the 3 February message ending it. The server count is unresolved (five, six, eight, "more than half" and "most" all appear), and the famous Magaziner threat traces to secondhand accounts.

### Cited Findings

**The directive itself**
- [opened] Postel's message is headed "Date: Wed, 28 Jan 1998 17:04:11 -0800", subject "root zone secondary service", PGP-signed by "iana <iana at iana.org>". Key lines: "The time has come to take a small step in one of those transitions. At some point on down the road it will be appropriate for the root domain to be edited and published directly by the IANA." and "we would like to have the secondaries for the root domain pull the root zone (by zone transfer) directly from IANA's own name server. This is "DNSROOT.IANA.ORG" with address 198.32.1.98." — [internet-history, Craig Simon, 12 Nov 2002](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November/000373.html)
- [opened] The directive promised no change of content: "The data in this root zone will be an exact copy of the root zone currently available on the A.ROOT-SERVERS.NET machine. There is no change being made at this time in the policies or procedures for making changes to the root zone." — [same message](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November/000373.html)
- [opened] The ending message is headed "Date: Tue, 3 Feb 1998 22:31:13 -0800 (PST)", subject "root zone transfer verification completed": "The root zone transfer verification test has been completed. Please return to the previous method of obtaining the root zone information from the A.ROOT-SE[RVERS.NET]…" So by Postel's own messages the redirection ran about six days, 28 January to 3 February 1998. — [same message](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November/000373.html)
- [opened] Postel's statement to the press: "As a verification that such a transfer can be accomplished smoothly and without interruption to the operational service, a test is being performed to rearrange the flow of root zone information. Once this test is completed the arrangements may revert to the previous arrangements." — [same message](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November/000373.html)
- [opened] Postel sent all three texts to Craig Simon on "Thu, 5 Feb 1998 23:27:50 -0800 (PST)" under the subject "Re: The famous test", so the documents come from Postel directly, via Simon. Simon notes "apparently there was an earlier email which included the same text, but without a PGP signature". — [same message](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November/000373.html)

**The word "test"**
- [opened] Craig Simon: "the directive initiating the action … does not refer to a test. Instead, Jon announced a "small step" in the "transitions" of the Internet's "management arrangements." Nothing was said about testing or an expected reversion back to the original arrangement." — [Simon, 2002](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November/000373.html)
- [opened] The face-saving claim, in Simon's words: "I have a taped interview in which Ira Magaziner says that during their famous phone conversation, Jon Postel proposed the idea of using the word "test" as a face saving gesture." This is one researcher's report of their own unpublished tape; I found no transcript. — [Simon, 2002](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November/000373.html)
- [opened] The opposite view, from Dave Crocker: "Jon needed to test the ability to switch to a different master DNS root, to make sure that there were ways to "route around" this concern over NSI. That's all the test was. Jon was clear about the need for this, weeks before the test." — [quoted in Simon, 2002](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November/000373.html)
- [summary] In the same thread Simon paraphrases Tom Postel as saying the test had ended before Magaziner called, which conflicts with the "ordered to stop" version. — [internet-history, November 2002 archive](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November.txt)

**How many servers (sources disagree; not resolved)**
- [summary] Six: Wired News, 5 Feb 1998, said Postel redirected six of the Net's twelve root servers and that they were still redirected at publication. Read through a mirror of Wired's archive, via a summariser. — [Wired News mirror](https://wirednews.pages.dev/1998/02/fallout-over-unsanctioned-dns-test)
- [summary] Five: Tech Monitor (Computergram) reported five of the twelve other root servers, and a window of 23 January to 4 February that does not match Postel's own message dates. — [Tech Monitor](https://www.techmonitor.ai/?p=90208)
- [pointer] Eight: Wikipedia says Postel "emailed eight of the twelve operators", citing Singer and Friedman's 2013 book, and that four US government roots (NASA, DoD, BRL) stayed with NSI. Search results attribute the same "eight of the twelve" wording, plus an anecdote that Paul Vixie arranged childcare in case of arrest, to Goldsmith and Wu; I did not open the book. — [Wikipedia: Jon Postel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Postel)
- [opened] "More than half": John Gilmore, 2025: Postel "had successfully converted more than half of the root servers to direct replication from IANA. When he asked the next root server (I think it was the one run by the Army), they told NSI about the request." — [internet-history, Gilmore, 18 Jul 2025](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2025-July/010553.html)
- [opened] "Most": Michael Froomkin's law review article says Postel asked "the root servers not controlled by NSI or the U.S. government" and "Most of the other root servers complied." Froomkin says they pointed to "his server "B""; Simon corrects this, saying they pulled from "a separate machine at ISI -- DNSROOT.IANA.ORG -- not B." — [Froomkin, "Wrong Turn in Cyberspace", 2000](https://osaka.law.miami.edu/~froomkin/articles/icann-body.htm); [internet-history, November 2002](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November.txt)
- [summary] A 2002 list post gives 27 January and says the switch was to F root (Vixie's); the signed directive contradicts both details. — [internet-history, November 2002](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November.txt)

**Magaziner**
- [summary] On the record at the time, Magaziner was mild. Wired quotes: "We thought the timing was a bit dicey" and "I'd give him a bit of slack", and has Magaziner recalling Postel saying "Yeah, that wasn't the right time to do it." NSI spokesperson Chris Clough: "It's a concern about who can authorize changes over the infrastructure and traffic patterns of the Internet." — [Wired News mirror, 5 Feb 1998](https://wirednews.pages.dev/1998/02/fallout-over-unsanctioned-dns-test)
- [opened] The threat, in Gilmore's 2025 wording: "On 1998-01-30 or so, there was a fractious phone call from Ira Magaziner to Jon Postel and some USC-ISI lawyers. Magaziner basically told Jon "Put those back or you'll never work on the Internet again"." Note "basically" and "or so": this is a paraphrase 27 years later, and Gilmore does not say they were on the call. Wikipedia sources the same quote to a 2002 Salon interview with Gilmore. — [Gilmore, 2025](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2025-July/010553.html); [Wikipedia: Jon Postel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Postel)
- [opened] Froomkin: "Magaziner was later quoted as saying that he told Postel that redirection could result in criminal charges, although it is unclear what statute would apply." I could not extract the footnote naming where Magaziner was quoted. — [Froomkin, 2000](https://osaka.law.miami.edu/~froomkin/articles/icann-body.htm)
- [opened] Froomkin's verdict: "the late Jon Postel, apparently tried to redirect the root from the "A" server and was intimidated into withdrawing the attempt." — [Froomkin, 2000](https://osaka.law.miami.edu/~froomkin/articles/icann-body.htm)

**People who were there**
- [opened] Joe Touch (ISI colleague), replying to Gilmore on 19 Jul 2025: "I have a very different recollection" and "My recollection is that this step was intended to prove how *easy* it would be to move the root zones, thus that no single organization should assert control over the root zones simply because of the inertia and effort required to change them." Also: "I view it as he discussed it with me - as a way to prove that the role was mutable." — [internet-history, Touch, 19 Jul 2025](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2025-July/010569.html)
- [opened] Touch on Postel's papers: "it is part of the USC archive of his office effects, and key portions of it with potential legal content would be USC confidential and not subject to public disclosure." — [Touch, 2025](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2025-July/010569.html)
- [opened] Gilmore's motive account: Postel "was (reasonably) concerned that if he added seven competing TLDs to the root zone, then a corrupt NSI would refuse to accept the update at the "A" root server". Gilmore was in CORE, the registrar council that had built a registry for seven new TLDs and "couldn't go into real operation without those new TLDs getting into the domain name systems' root zone." — [Gilmore, 2025](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2025-July/010553.html)
- [opened] Brian Carpenter (then IAB chair): "the IETF met in D.C. in December 1997, and Magaziner invited a number of people to see him in his office in the Old Executive Office Building, certainly including Jon." Then: "Of course, he ignored us." — [internet-history, Carpenter, July 2025](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2025-July/010561.html)
- [summary] 2002 thread: Randy Bush: "it proved to the folk in dc and the big companies that the engineers could not be trusted." Crocker's reply: "It demonstrated that those folk in dc were clueless about operations." Simon: "John Gilmore was evidently an important agent leading the call for the redirection" and the operators "went along, even with raised eyebrows, but they trusted Postel's judgment". Crocker (2009): operators "simply did what Jon told them to do"; Postel "didn't do bravado". — [internet-history, November 2002](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November.txt); [Crocker, 31 Aug 2009](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2009-August/000899.html)
- I found no first-person account by Paul Vixie of the January 1998 request.

**The surrounding fight**
- [opened] Gilmore on NSI: "NSI was bought in 1995 by beltway bandit SAIC for $4.7M. SAIC immediately politicked NSF, which then allowed NSI to charge every domain holder $50/year for their formerly free domain names." One participant's partisan account; figures not cross-checked. — [Gilmore, 2025](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2025-July/010553.html)
- [opened] Froomkin: "On January 30, 1998, Magaziner and DoC published their proposal for the reform of DNS administration" (the Green Paper), and "On January 28, 1998, Jon Postel -- who may have been aware of the likely content of the Green Paper -- sent an e-mail". So the directive came two days before the Green Paper. — [Froomkin, 2000](https://osaka.law.miami.edu/~froomkin/articles/icann-body.htm)
- [opened] Froomkin on the outcome: "by 1998 the U.S. government had defeated an attempt to redirect the root and amended its contract with NSI to make the government's supremacy clear." The later White Paper called for a new non-profit ("NewCo") to "[o]versee operation of the authoritative Internet root server system". — [Froomkin, 2000](https://osaka.law.miami.edu/~froomkin/articles/icann-body.htm)
- [summary] Kashpureff: the DOJ press release says the InterNIC hijack ran 10 to 14 July and again 21 to 24 July 1997; Wikipedia says 11 July, stopped 14 July, restarted 18 July. Arrested in Toronto on 31 October 1997; handed to US authorities 24 December 1997; pleaded guilty in March 1998 to one computer-fraud count. — [DOJ release, archived](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20081204191746/http:/www.cybercrime.gov/kashpurepr.htm); [Wired News mirror, "Domain Guerrilla Pleads Guilty"](https://wirednews.pages.dev/1998/03/domain-guerrilla-pleads-guilty); [Wikipedia: AlterNIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlterNIC)
- [pointer] Wikipedia: Kashpureff, Jason Hendeles and Diane Boling created AlterNIC in 1995; the hijack was DNS cache poisoning against BIND before 4.9.6; sentence was a $100 fine and two years' probation. — [Wikipedia: AlterNIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlterNIC)

**The ending**
- [pointer] Postel died on 16 October 1998 in Los Angeles of complications after heart-valve surgery (Wikipedia citing the Washington Post obituary). Vint Cerf's memorial is RFC 2468. — [Wikipedia: Jon Postel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Postel)
- [opened] Gilmore's ending: "Jon Postel died of a leaking heart valve later that year, which left a void that the corrupt, bloated and self-serving ICANN (which was created based on Magaziner's model) was happy to fill" and "In short, the fix was in, and the beltway bandits won." — [Gilmore, 2025](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2025-July/010553.html)
- [opened] A strange coda from Gilmore alone: "on 1998-02-15 … the data center was broken into by thieves, the chain-link fencing around the servers was cut, and two entire Sun Enterprise 450 servers, worth about $70,000, were stolen. Nothing else in the whole multi-tenant data center was stolen." Gilmore blames SAIC by rhetorical question only ("who could have wanted to target CORE except SAIC?") and says the theft "was never resolved". — [Gilmore, 2025](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2025-July/010553.html)

### Inferences
- The six days and the 17:04 PST timestamp are the firmest facts; the press counts (five, six) and book counts (eight) probably reflect different days or different ways of counting, since Gilmore says Postel asked operators one at a time.
- The strongest fresh angle for this audience is the document: a PGP-signed email with an IP address in it, which you can quote as the hook, and which undercuts the label everyone uses.
- The honest framing is three rival readings from people who knew Postel: a contingency drill against NSI (Crocker), a demonstration that the root was movable (Touch), and groundwork for adding CORE's seven TLDs (Gilmore). Simon's "test of power" sits between them.
- Two-sentence test: Postel wanted the root to be publishable by IANA and not hostage to NSI's "A" server, but the White House read a quiet zone-transfer change as a seizure, so he reversed it within a week and the label "test" covered the retreat. The reader learns that the directive never said "test", and that the people closest to Postel still disagree about what it was.

### Gaps
- No source reconciles the server count, and none I opened lists which letters switched.
- The Magaziner tape (Simon) is not public as far as I could find. Simon's dissertation on the DNS war is the obvious next source [memory]; not opened.
- Not opened: Milton Mueller's "Ruling the Root", Goldsmith and Wu's "Who Controls the Internet?", the Washington Post story of 31 Jan 1998 (HTTP 403), Network World's "Taking the wrong root?" (4 Feb 1998), the AP story by Ted Bridis (5 Feb 1998), Salon's 2002 Gilmore interview, and Carpenter's book pp. 119-122.
- Unverified in this session [memory]: White Paper date (June 1998), ICANN's incorporation date (autumn 1998), the IAHC and gTLD-MoU dates (1996-97), NSI's September 1995 fee start. Check against ICANN and NTIA pages before use.
- Kashpureff's hijack dates differ between the DOJ release and Wikipedia; neither was opened directly.

## DigiNotar: timeline, Fox-IT findings, Comodohacker, what changed, and alibo

### Takeaway
A complete story with a clean arc: a forum post by a pseudonymous user in Iran on 27 or 28 August 2011 led within 24 days to a certificate authority's bankruptcy. It is the best documented of the three and the most told (Slate feature, Darknet Diaries episode); nothing is known about alibo beyond the post.

### Cited Findings

**Timeline**
- [pointer] VASCO acquired DigiNotar on 10 January 2011. — [Wikipedia: DigiNotar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DigiNotar)
- [opened] Slate: an intruder got in through unpatched web-server software "in June 2011"; "On July 10, the intruder successfully issued his first rogue certificate"; "On July 19, a routine check by DigiNotar revealed that some of the certificates it had ostensibly signed were not listed in the company's logs". DigiNotar revoked them and "by the end of July, the company believed the problem had been dealt with." — [Josephine Wolff, Slate, 21 Dec 2016](https://slate.com/technology/2016/12/how-the-2011-hack-of-diginotar-changed-the-internets-infrastructure.html)
- [summary] Start of the breach disagrees: The Register reports Fox-IT putting it on or around 6 June; CSO reports a possible initial compromise on 17 June. — [The Register, 6 Sep 2011](https://www.theregister.com/2011/09/06/diginotar_audit_damning_fail/); [CSO](https://www.csoonline.com/article/532492/data-protection-nearly-300-000-iranian-ip-addresses-likely-compromised.html)
- [opened] The post date disagrees by a day. Slate: "On Saturday, Aug. 27, 2011, an Iranian man who went by the online alias alibo tried to check his email". The Register (29 Aug 2011): "According to a post published on Sunday by a user calling himself alibo". 27 August 2011 was a Saturday, so the gap may be time zones. — [Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2016/12/how-the-2011-hack-of-diginotar-changed-the-internets-infrastructure.html); [The Register, 29 Aug 2011](https://www.theregister.com/2011/08/29/fraudulent_google_ssl_certificate/)
- [opened] alibo's words as quoted by The Register: "I think my ISP or my government did this attack (because I live in Iran and you may hear something about the story of Comodo hacker!)". The Register adds that alibo's claims about ISPs "including ParsOnline" "couldn't be independently confirmed", and that the certificate was "issued on July 10" and valid for "*.google.com". — [The Register, 29 Aug 2011](https://www.theregister.com/2011/08/29/fraudulent_google_ssl_certificate/)
- [opened] Gervase Markham of Mozilla: "On Monday August 29th at 6.30pm BST Mozilla was informed by Google about a misissued certificate for *.google.com which was being used in active attacks on users in Iran." And: "DigiNotar discovered evidence of compromise of their systems on the 19th of July, but decided not to inform embedders of their root, including Mozilla." — [Markham, 3 Sep 2011](https://blog.gerv.net/2011/09/diginotar-compromise/)
- [opened] Google's post is dated August 29, 2011; the body did not load for me. A reader comment quotes it: "Google Chrome users were protected from this attack because Chrome was able to detect the fraudulent certificate." Wikipedia credits the post to Heather Adkins. — [Google Security Blog](https://security.googleblog.com/2011/08/update-on-attempted-man-in-middle.html)
- [opened] The Register, same day: browsers were "taking the highly unusual step of blocking all certificates issued by DigiNotar". It quotes an unnamed researcher: "This CA should receive an internet death sentence as their carelessness may have resulted in deaths in Iran". — [The Register, 29 Aug 2011](https://www.theregister.com/2011/08/29/fraudulent_google_ssl_certificate/)
- [pointer] 3 September 2011: the Dutch government took over operational management and withdrew its statement that government certificates were unaffected. 20 September 2011: VASCO announced DigiNotar had been declared bankrupt by the Haarlem court. — [Wikipedia: DigiNotar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DigiNotar)

**Fox-IT**
- [opened] Slate: the intruder "would go on to issue 531 rogue certificates for domains ranging from aol.com and microsoft.com to mossad.gov.il and cia.gov." — [Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2016/12/how-the-2011-hack-of-diginotar-changed-the-internets-infrastructure.html)
- [summary] Fox-IT counted about 300,000 unique IP addresses requesting google.com with the rogue certificate, over 99% of them in Iran. Note the unit: IP addresses, though Slate and Wikipedia write "300,000 Gmail accounts/users". — [CSO](https://www.csoonline.com/article/532492/data-protection-nearly-300-000-iranian-ip-addresses-likely-compromised.html); [Dark Reading](https://www.darkreading.com/cyberattacks-data-breaches/stolen-digital-certificates-compromised-cia-mi6-tor)
- [opened] Mozilla's earlier count was 247: "These 247 certificates were all short-life, and expired by the 19th of August." Markham adds it was "standard DigiNotar practice, for all their certificates except for their EV certificates, not to put any revocation information in their certs". — [Markham, 2011](https://blog.gerv.net/2011/09/diginotar-compromise/)
- [opened] Slate on physical security: "the investigators' best guess was that the keycards for a few computers were left permanently in place." — [Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2016/12/how-the-2011-hack-of-diginotar-changed-the-internets-infrastructure.html)
- [opened] Slate quotes the lead investigator: "The realization that the … security of a small company in Holland [may have] played a part in the killing or torture of people really shocked me." Slate's name for the investigator fell outside my extract (Hans Hoogstraaten [memory]; confirm on the page). — [Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2016/12/how-the-2011-hack-of-diginotar-changed-the-internets-infrastructure.html)
- [summary] Report dates: interim report 5 September 2011; the final "Black Tulip" report is filed by the Dutch government under 13 August 2012, while press coverage of it is dated late October 2012. — [Darknet Diaries ep. 3 references](https://darknetdiaries.com/episode/3/); [Threatpost](https://threatpost.com/final-report-diginotar-hack-shows-total-compromise-ca-servers-103112)

**Comodohacker**
- [summary] On 5 September 2011 a Pastebin user "Comodohacker" claimed the breach, self-described as a 21-year-old Iranian student (unconfirmed), and claimed access to four more CAs including GlobalSign. Fox-IT said fingerprints "left on purpose" in a script matched the March 2011 Comodo breach. — [InfoWorld](https://www.infoworld.com/article/2304583/comodo-hacker-claims-credit-for-breaching-diginotar.html); [The Register, 7 Sep 2011](https://www.theregister.com/2011/09/07/globalsign_suspends_ssl_cert_biz/)
- [pointer] Nobody had been charged as of 2013; Bruce Schneier suggested NSA involvement or exploitation, which others dispute. — [Wikipedia: DigiNotar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DigiNotar)

**What changed**
- [opened] Slate: "Certificate pinning—what Google did with its certificates in Chrome, which resulted in the DigiNotar compromise first being detected—has become more common". On Certificate Transparency: "Google announced in December 2014 that, beginning in 2015, it would no longer display the green address bar in Chrome" for EV certificates that were not logged. — [Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2016/12/how-the-2011-hack-of-diginotar-changed-the-internets-infrastructure.html)

**alibo**
- No source identifies alibo. Everything known comes from the post: a Chrome user in Iran, reached Gmail fine through a VPN, named ParsOnline, attached the certificate. The thread was at `productforums.google.com/forum/#!topic/gmail/3J3r2JqFNTw` (listed in Darknet Diaries' references); I did not open it and do not know whether it survives. — [Darknet Diaries ep. 3](https://darknetdiaries.com/episode/3/)

### Inferences
- The protagonist problem: alibo is a pseudonym who posts once and vanishes, and the attacker is an unverified persona. The fullest human arc belongs to DigiNotar (kept the breach from the browsers for about 40 days, 19 July to 29 August) or to the browser people (Markham; Adkins and Google's pinning).
- The dot most web developers have not connected: a pin Google shipped in Chrome for its own domains turned one user's warning into the detection of a state-scale interception, and the fix that followed (CT logs) is why every certificate a developer gets today is public. It also sets up the WoSign story below.
- Two-sentence test: DigiNotar wanted its July break-in to stay quiet, but one Chrome user in Iran posted a warning that Chrome showed only because Google had pinned its own certificates, so within 24 days every browser had deleted the company and it was bankrupt. The reader learns that the alarm came from a help-forum post.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Fox-IT reports (the Dutch government link returned an error page, the SEC copy was blocked), so 531, 300,000 and 99% rest on press summaries. Password and antivirus details that circulate about the report are not verified here.
- The alibo post itself was not opened; the one-day date disagreement is unresolved.
- VASCO's purchase price was not found.
- Wolff's later book has a DigiNotar chapter [memory]; not checked.

## robots.txt: the 1994 record, Stross's claim, Koster's answer, AI crawlers and RFC 9309

### Takeaway
The archive supports a better story than the one usually told. Koster's own 2019 timeline dates Stross's emails to 7 March 1994, ten days after the proposal was posted on 25 February 1994, and names an earlier September 1993 incident as the likelier spur. The file was first called `/RobotsNotWanted.txt` and got its name in a June 1994 vote in which Roy Fielding argued for `/spiders.txt`.

### Cited Findings

**The 1994 record**
- [opened] First public mention, www-talk, "Date: Fri, 25 Feb 1994 13:18:03", subject "Important: Spiders, Robots and Web Wanderers", from Martijn Koster at Nexor: "Don't worry, this is not a debate about "are robots good or bad"." And: "a proposed standard that will allow WWW server maintainers to indicate if they want robots to access their server, and if so which parts." And: "Robots are one of the few aspects of the web that cause operational problems and cause people grief." And: "The major robot writers are in favour of this idea, so I don't see any fundamental problems." PS: "I do hope this gets out; www-talk as been empty for days..." (The Message-ID begins 9402240935, suggesting it was written on the 24th.) — [www-talk archive, Calgary mirror](https://ksi.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/archives/WWW-TALK/www-talk-1994q1.messages/712.html)
- [opened] Announcement, www-talk, "Sun, 3 Jul 1994 18:54:53 +0200", "ANNOUNCE: A Standard for Robot Exclusion": "Some of you may remember a proposed standard for robot exclusion I posted to this forum last year." (It was that February; "last year" is Koster's wording.) "Most of the robots in operation either use it already, or have promised support soon." — [www-talk archive](https://ksi.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/archives/WWW-TALK/www-talk-1994q3/0007.html)
- [opened] The standard's status text: "This document represents a consensus on 30 June 1994 on the robots mailing list". "It is not an official standard backed by a standards body, or owned by any commercial organisation. It is not enforced by anybody, and there no guarantee that all current and future robots will use it." Its introduction: "In 1993 and 1994 there have been occasions where robots have visited WWW servers where they weren't welcome", including robots that "swamped servers with rapid-fire requests". — [robotstxt.org, "A Standard for Robot Exclusion"](https://www.robotstxt.org/orig.html)
- [opened] Koster, 2019, on the first version: "unfortunately no copy of that version survives." And: "Back then it used /RobotsNotWanted.txt". — [Koster, "Robots.txt is 25 years old", 1 Jul 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)
- [opened] Same day, Usenet: "there was a discussion in comp.infosystems.www started by "PG" (Paul Ginsparg, then at Los Alamos National Lab) on 25 Feb 1994 "WHAT TO DO ABOUT MISERABLE AUTOMATED DOWNLOADS???", after a robot visit from Imperial College London". — [Koster, 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)
- [opened] The naming vote. Koster, 6 Jun 1994: "The only outstanding issue is the name of the file; it is too long for DOS-based servers. Is there any problem with changing the filename to robotsp.txt (for robots policy) ?" Roy Fielding, 15 Jun 1994: "I propose that the name be: /spiders.txt", because "It fits within the 8.3 filename restrictions for PCs" and "It is more web-ish than /robots.txt"; "I need to have a decision within the next 24 hours in order to get my paper done on time ;-)". Fielding also joked: "we could always call it "robots.pl" and require the format to be in Perl4. ;-)". Candidates tallied: spiders.txt, robots.txt, robots.lmt, racl.txt, agents.pol, agents.txt, avoidURL.txt; voters included Guido van Rossum and Michael L. Mauldin. Settled 17 Jun 1994 when Koster answered "Yes" to ""robots.txt"? empty file = all robots permitted? '#' = comment character?" — [Koster, 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)
- [opened] Early implementations Koster found: Fielding's MOMspider paper (May 1994) says it "looks for and obeys any restrictions found in a site's /RobotsNotWanted.txt document"; "Lycos Perl code from Jun 1994"; and "a norobots.py implementation from Guido.van.Rossum@cwi.nl on 21 Jun 1994." — [Koster, 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)

**Stross's claim, in Stross's words**
- [opened] Post: "How I got here in the end, part five: "things can only get better!"", Charlie's Diary, 19 June 2009 (Wikipedia's citation says 2006; the page and its URL say 2009). Summary line: "I learned Perl (against orders), accidentally provoked the invention of the robots.txt file". — [Stross, antipope.org](http://www.antipope.org/charlie/blog-static/2009/06/how_i_got_here_in_the_end_part_3.html)
- [opened] The passage: "I was testing my spider and, absent-mindedly, gave it a wired-in starting URL. What I didn't realize was that I'd picked a bloody stupid place to start my test traversals from; a website on spiders, run from a server owned by a very small company — over a 14.4K leased line. I guess I'd unintentionally invented the denial of service attack! Martin, the guy who ran the web server, got in touch, and was most displeased. First, he told me to stop hammering his system — advice with which I hastily complied. Then he invented a standard procedure: when visiting a new system, look for a file called "robots.txt", parse it, and avoid any directories or files it lists. I think I may have written the first spider to obey the robots.txt protocol; I'm certainly the numpty who necessitated its invention." Stross writes "Martin", not Martijn, and was then a technical author at SCO, where "SCO EMEA had a 64K leased line in those days, shared between 200 people." — [Stross, 2009](http://www.antipope.org/charlie/blog-static/2009/06/how_i_got_here_in_the_end_part_3.html)
- [opened] Stross's own archive page: "Websnarf Withdrawn. I wrote this robot in 1993/94 … Websnarf was written as a learning exercise in Perl (4.036 at that time)." — [Stross, robot page](http://www.antipope.org/charlie/attic/webbook/robot/index.html)

**Koster's answer**
- [opened] Koster neither confirms nor denies; the dates do the work: "Charlie Stross describes overloading my server with his Websnarf crawler. I found emails from him from 7 Mar 1994; I'm not sure if there were others. Anyway, suffice to say this was an issue that affected my attempts to offer web services." — [Koster, 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)
- [opened] Koster's own candidate cause, quoting an old page: "Then in September 1993 my Web server mailed me a report indicating someone had been retrieving an unusually large number of documents … at an enourmeous rate, in the order of 1 document per second." And: "I found an email exchange with Wayne Allen at MCC dated 23 Sept 1993 where I complained about his frequent repeated automated downloads; so perhaps that was what motiviated me to try and do something about it." — [Koster, 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)
- [opened] Koster's hardware differs from Stross's memory: "an scrounged Sun 4/330 UNIX sever, connected to the internet over a company leased line (ISDN perhaps?)", against Stross's "14.4K leased line". — [Koster, 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)

**Since 2019: standard, then breakdown**
- [opened] RFC 9309, "Robots Exclusion Protocol", September 2022, Standards Track; authors M. Koster, G. Illyes, H. Zeller, L. Sassman (Google LLC). Abstract: it "specifies and extends the "Robots Exclusion Protocol" method originally defined by Martijn Koster in 1994". Also: "These rules are not a form of access authorization." — [RFC 9309](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309.txt)
- [opened] Google announced the standardisation effort on 1 July 2019 (tweets Koster reproduces): "after 25 years of being a de-facto standard, we worked with Martijn Koster (@makuk66), webmasters, and other search engines to make the Robots Exclusion Protocol an official standard!" and "over 500 million websites use it!" — [Koster, 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)
- [opened] Earlier strains Koster lists: Healthcare Advocates sued the Internet Archive in 2005 over robots.txt handling (settled 2006); "In 2014 Baidu won a lawsuit against Qihoo 360, involving the latter ignoring /robots.txt" with ¥700,000 ordered; "In 2017, the Internet Archive announced they would ignore /robots.txt". — [Koster, 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)
- [opened] The Verge, David Pierce, 15 Feb 2024, "The text file that runs the internet" (read via a mailing-list repost): "This text file has no particular legal or technical authority"; "It's a mini constitution for the internet, written in code." Figures: "According to a study from last fall by Originality.AI, 306 of the top 1,000 sites on the web blocked GPTBot, but only 85 blocked Google-Extended and 28 blocked anthropic-ai." It mentions the RobotsNotWanted.txt rename. The portion I read does not mention Stross. — [repost of The Verge](https://www.mail-archive.com/link@anu.edu.au/msg01766.html)
- [opened] Cloudflare, 4 Aug 2025: "We see continued evidence that Perplexity is repeatedly modifying their user agent and changing their source ASNs to hide their crawling activity, as well as ignoring — or sometimes failing to even fetch — robots.txt files." And: "we have de-listed them as a verified bot". — [Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/perplexity-is-using-stealth-undeclared-crawlers-to-evade-website-no-crawl-directives/)
- [summary] Perplexity disputed this, calling the post a "sales pitch". — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/cloudflare-says-perplexity-is-breaking-a-major-online-ai-scraping-rule)
- [summary] June 2024: TollBit told publishers, per Reuters, that "numerous" AI agents were bypassing robots.txt. July 2024: iFixit's Kyle Wiens said Anthropic's ClaudeBot hit the site about a million times in 24 hours and stopped once robots.txt was updated. 1 July 2025: Cloudflare began blocking AI crawlers by default for new customers and announced "Pay Per Crawl". TollBit later reported bypass rates rising from 3.3% (Q4 2024) to 12.9% (Q1 2025). — [Reuters via Yahoo](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/exclusive-multiple-ai-companies-bypassing-143742268.html); [404 Media](https://404media.co/anthropic-ai-scraper-hits-ifixits-website-a-million-times-in-a-day/); [The Register, 1 Jul 2025](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/01/cloudflare_creates_ai_crawler_toll/); [TollBit](https://tollbit.com/bots/25q2)

### Inferences
- Stross's "necessitated its invention" is not supported as the sole or first cause. On Koster's records the proposal was public, and already had "the major robot writers" behind it, before Stross's earliest surviving email. Stross can still be right that Websnarf hammered the server (Koster does not dispute it) and may have been an early adopter. Koster's "I'm not sure if there were others" leaves room for earlier contact. An essay should present this as an open, friendly disagreement of memory against mail archive, not a debunking.
- The irony that ties 1994 to now: the 1994 text says outright "It is not enforced by anybody", and the 2022 RFC says the rules "are not a form of access authorization". It was a courtesy between about a dozen named people, and the essay's turn is the moment the crawler authors stopped being on the mailing list.
- Two-sentence test: Koster wanted crawler authors to stay out of parts of a server without a fight over whether robots were good, and got agreement in four months because every robot author fit on one mailing list; thirty years later it became an IETF standard just as crawlers arrived whose owners had no reason to honour it. The reader learns the file was nearly `/spiders.txt`, that the famous origin anecdote does not fit the dates, and that arXiv's founder was shouting about robots on Usenet the same day.

### Gaps
- Neither Stross's March 1994 emails nor Koster's September 1993 exchange is public; both are known only through Koster's summary.
- I did not open the comp.infosystems.www thread, the robots mailing-list archive, or Koster's 1993 "Guidelines for Robot Writers".
- The first draft of the proposal is lost by Koster's own account, so the exact original syntax cannot be quoted.
- TollBit, iFixit, Reuters and the Cloudflare default-block items are from search summaries; the percentages need checking. Anything after mid-2025 is thin here.
- The Verge article was read only in a repost, and not to the end.

## New candidates: up to three other stories in this territory

### Takeaway
Two candidates clear the bar and are little known to web developers: VeriSign's Site Finder (2003) and the WoSign and StartCom affair (2015 to 2018), which is the direct sequel to DigiNotar. A third, eBay v. Bidder's Edge (2000), is weaker alone but is the missing middle act of the robots.txt story.

### Cited Findings

**Candidate D: VeriSign Site Finder, 15 September to 4 October 2003 (naming)**
- Two sentences: VeriSign, which ran .com and .net, wanted to turn every mistyped domain into its own ad-bearing search page, so on 15 September 2003 it made every unregistered name resolve; mail filters and anything else that relied on "no such domain" broke, operators patched BIND within days to ignore it, and ICANN forced it off on 4 October. The reader learns that for 19 days nothing in .com failed to exist, and that the registry lost the wildcard but kept fighting in court until 2006.
- [opened] ICANN's Paul Twomey to VeriSign's Russell Lewis ("Dear Rusty"), 3 October 2003: the changes "involved the introduction (for the first time in the .com and .net domains) of a so-called "wildcard" mechanism"; ICANN "on 19 September 2003 asked VeriSign to voluntarily suspend these changes"; "On 21 September 2003, VeriSign refused to honor that request." The ultimatum: "VeriSign must suspend the changes to the .com and .net top-level domains introduced on 15 September 2003 by 6:00 PM PDT on 4 October 2003." — [ICANN, Twomey to Lewis](https://www.icann.org/resources/unthemed-pages/twomey-to-lewis-2003-10-03-en)
- [summary] ISC released a BIND patch adding "delegation-only" zones within about two days (list posts dated 17 September 2003); the configuration was `zone "com" { type delegation-only; };`. AP quoted Paul Vixie, ISC's president, on the patch. — [TriLUG list, Sept 2003](https://trilug.org/pipermail/trilug/Week-of-Mon-20030915/020383.html); [freebsd-stable, Sept 2003](https://lists.freebsd.org/pipermail/freebsd-stable/2003-September/003499.html)
- [pointer] VeriSign sued ICANN on 27 February 2004; dismissed in August 2004; settled 1 March 2006 with a new .com registry agreement. ICANN's security committee reported on 9 July 2004. Alexa put verisign.com near 2,500th before and in the top 20 after. The IAB published a commentary on DNS wildcards. Site Finder's terms of use bound anyone who mistyped an address. — [Wikipedia: Site Finder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Site_Finder)
- Best primary sources to open next: VeriSign's NANOG announcement (15-16 Sept 2003), the NANOG thread "What *are* they smoking?", the IAB commentary of 19-20 September 2003, ICANN's advisories, the ISC delegation-only page, and the 2006 settlement text.
- Coverage: heavy at the time on NANOG and Slashdot; I found no long-form retelling. Vixie appears in both this story and Postel's.

**Candidate E: WoSign and StartCom, November 2015 to January 2018 (certificates)**
- Two sentences: WoSign's Richard Wang wanted to keep selling SHA-1 certificates past the 1 January 2016 cut-off and to own the West's best-known free CA without saying so, but Certificate Transparency timestamps embedded in the certificates proved the backdating and a company-registry search proved the purchase, so Mozilla, Apple and Google distrusted both CAs and StartCom closed. The reader learns that the free StartSSL certificate many of them ran before Let's Encrypt had been quietly sold, and that the log system built after DigiNotar caught the lie.
- [opened] Mozilla, Kathleen Wilson, 24 October 2016: "we discovered they were backdating SSL certificates in order to get around the deadline that CAs stop issuing SHA-1 SSL certificates by January 1, 2016. Additionally, Mozilla discovered that WoSign had acquired full ownership of another CA called StartCom and failed to disclose this". And: "The representatives of WoSign and StartCom denied and continued to deny both of these allegations until sufficient data was collected to demonstrate that both allegations were correct." Remedy: "Distrust certificates with a notBefore date after October 21, 2016". — [Mozilla Security Blog](https://blog.mozilla.org/security/2016/10/24/distrusting-new-wosign-and-startcom-certificates/)
- [opened] The proof: three certificates "have notBefore dates in Dec 2015, have signatures over SHA-1 hashes and have embedded SCTs which are dated after January 1, 2016." "Because these SCTs are embedded, they must have been created before the final certificate was signed". Scale: "The number of certs affected is probably 67". — [Mozilla wiki, CA:WoSign Issues](https://wiki.mozilla.org/CA:WoSign_Issues)
- [opened] The purchase: "Mozilla has confirmed with a Hebrew-speaking lawyer that, as far as the Israeli company authorities are concerned, the transaction which completed the chain to give WoSign 100% ownership of StartCom completed on November 1st 2015." WoSign "attempted to suppress further information about the ownership transfer". Wang's reply: "An announcement and disclosure will be made shortly pending completion of the business transaction." WoSign's 19 September 2016 press release said it had "made an equity investment"; Mozilla called that "economical with the truth". — [Mozilla wiki, CA:WoSign Issues](https://wiki.mozilla.org/CA:WoSign_Issues)
- [pointer] StartCom was founded by Eddy Nigg in Eilat, Israel in 1999; the parent was Qihoo 360; StartCom announced termination on 16 November 2017 and stopped issuing on 1 January 2018; Chrome removed trust from March 2017. — [Wikipedia: StartCom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/StartCom)
- Best primary sources to open next: the mozilla.dev.security.policy threads (August to October 2016), Mozilla's "WoSign and StartCom" document of 10 October 2016, Google's and Apple's distrust notices, and The Register's "Heads roll as Qihoo 360 moves to end WoSign, StartCom certificate row" (10 Oct 2016).
- Coverage: trade press only; I found no feature or podcast episode.

**Candidate F: eBay v. Bidder's Edge, 1999 to 2001 (crawling)**
- Two sentences: Bidder's Edge wanted to show eBay's auctions beside everyone else's and kept crawling after eBay withdrew permission and its robots.txt said no; on 24 May 2000 a federal judge enjoined the crawler as a trespass on eBay's servers, the first time ignoring that courtesy cost someone in court. The reader learns the argument over whether robots.txt means anything legally is 25 years old.
- [pointer] All from Wikipedia's summary of the opinion (the court text did not load): decided 24 May 2000, N.D. Cal., Judge Ronald Whyte, 100 F. Supp. 2d 1058; Bidder's Edge founded 1997; eBay verbally approved crawling for 90 days on 24 April 1999; BE "accessed eBay approximately 100,000 times a day, constituting about 1.53% of eBay's total daily requests"; eBay's robots.txt disallowed it; settled March 2001, with BE paying an undisclosed sum. — [Wikipedia: eBay v. Bidder's Edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EBay_v._Bidder%27s_Edge)
- Best primary sources to open next: the opinion on Justia, Wired's "EBay Fights Spiders on the Web" (31 Jul 2000), and CNET's settlement story.

### Inferences
- Site Finder is the best fit for this audience: many of them ran mail servers or debugged a "site not found" that suddenly was not. It has a villain with a name on a letter, a 19-day clock and a hard deadline to the hour.
- WoSign and StartCom pairs naturally with DigiNotar as one essay about who gets to be trusted: the first failure was caught by luck and a forum post, the second by the logging the first one caused.
- Bidder's Edge is better as a section of the robots.txt essay (between the 1994 handshake and the AI crawlers) than as its own piece; Koster's own list of lawsuits skips it.
- Leads noticed but not vetted: Kashpureff's 1997 InterNIC hijack as its own story (arrest, extradition, plea); the unsolved theft of CORE's two registry servers on 15 February 1998 (Gilmore only); Healthcare Advocates v. Internet Archive (2005-06).

### Gaps
- Site Finder's lawsuit and settlement dates, and all of Bidder's Edge, rest on Wikipedia.
- I did not find Vixie's or VeriSign's own words on Site Finder, or the terms of the 2006 settlement.
- I did not verify what happened to WoSign afterwards or to Richard Wang (The Register's headline implies departures; not opened).
- Routing (BGP hijacks), archives and standards-body fights were not searched; the three candidates come from naming, certificates and crawling only.

## For every topic: how well covered already, and the single most surprising documented fact

### Takeaway
By how little the story has been told against how strong it is, my ranking is: robots.txt's real origin (widely summarised, but the archive detail and the Stross chronology are almost untold), Postel (known in outline from books and Wikipedia, with the documents rarely quoted), WoSign and StartCom, Site Finder, then DigiNotar (the strongest plain story but the most told).

### Cited Findings
- **Postel, coverage:** treated in Froomkin's 2000 law review article and, per Wikipedia's citations, in Singer and Friedman (2013), Mueller's "Ruling the Root" and contemporaneous Network World, AP and InternetWeek pieces; argued over on internet-history in 2002, 2009 and 2025. Stéphane Bortzmeyer wrote in 2010 that the episode "n'avait laissé que peu de traces dans la mémoire collective de l'Internet" and that a search engine finds little. I found no podcast episode on it. — [Froomkin](https://osaka.law.miami.edu/~froomkin/articles/icann-body.htm); [Wikipedia: Jon Postel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Postel); [Bortzmeyer, 27 May 2010](https://www.bortzmeyer.org/dns-root-takeover.html)
- **Postel, most surprising fact [opened]:** the signed directive does not contain the word "test"; it says "The time has come to take a small step in one of those transitions." — [internet-history, 2002](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November/000373.html)
- **DigiNotar, coverage:** Slate feature by Josephine Wolff (21 Dec 2016), whose title itself says "a 2011 Hack You've Never Heard of"; Darknet Diaries episode 3, "DigiNotar, You are the Weakest Link, Good Bye!" (1 October 2017, 25:44, guests Gervase Markham and Josephine Wolff); the Fox-IT reports; heavy 2011 trade coverage. — [Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2016/12/how-the-2011-hack-of-diginotar-changed-the-internets-infrastructure.html); [Darknet Diaries ep. 3](https://darknetdiaries.com/episode/3/)
- **DigiNotar, most surprising fact [opened]:** DigiNotar found the compromise on 19 July "but decided not to inform embedders of their root, including Mozilla"; the browsers learned of it from a help-forum post about 40 days later. — [Markham](https://blog.gerv.net/2011/09/diginotar-compromise/)
- **robots.txt, coverage:** The Verge's 2024 feature is the standard account and covers the AI turn; Wikipedia repeats Stross's claim without Koster's dates; Koster's 2019 post, which he introduces with "Pure nostalgia, and I expect no-one cares", holds the unused detail. Several recent pieces are AI-written "imagined conversations" and one "obituary" for robots.txt; do not use them as sources. — [The Verge repost](https://www.mail-archive.com/link@anu.edu.au/msg01766.html); [Koster, 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)
- **robots.txt, most surprising fact [opened]:** Koster's earliest emails from Stross are dated 7 March 1994, after the 25 February 1994 proposal. Runner-up: Roy Fielding wanted `/spiders.txt` and needed a decision "within the next 24 hours" to finish a conference paper. — [Koster, 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)
- **Site Finder, most surprising fact [opened]:** ICANN's deadline was to the hour, "6:00 PM PDT on 4 October 2003", after VeriSign had refused a polite request two weeks earlier. — [ICANN](https://www.icann.org/resources/unthemed-pages/twomey-to-lewis-2003-10-03-en)
- **WoSign and StartCom, most surprising fact [opened]:** the backdating was proven because the certificates carried Certificate Transparency timestamps from January 2016 inside certificates dated December 2015. — [Mozilla wiki](https://wiki.mozilla.org/CA:WoSign_Issues)

### Inferences
- Story-check verdicts. Postel: passes (named protagonist, turn on 28 January 1998, ending in October 1998, real surprise), provided the essay is built on the three emails and is candid that the count and the threat are disputed. DigiNotar: passes easily; the risk is retelling Slate. robots.txt: passes, and is the best "dots not connected" piece; the risk is the present-day section going stale quickly. Site Finder and WoSign: pass on the evidence so far. Bidder's Edge: a section, not an essay.
- Postel and robots.txt share a theme that could link two essays: both were arrangements that worked because everyone involved was on one mailing list and trusted one person, and both failed the moment someone outside the list had money at stake.
- Postel's story overlaps Site Finder's (NSI became VeriSign's registry; Vixie is in both), so the two could be told as cause and sequel.

### Gaps
- "How widely told" is judged from what my searches surfaced, not from a systematic survey of books and podcasts. I did not check for episodes of other history podcasts or for conference talks.
- Books not opened in this session: Mueller, Goldsmith and Wu, Singer and Friedman, Carpenter, Wolff.
- This research ran well past the usual tool-call budget; several important pages (Washington Post 1998, Fox-IT, The Verge original, Justia, Internet Archive copies) were blocked from this environment and should be retried by hand.
