## ArsDigita, 1997 to 2002: what it made, the investment, the removal of the founders, the lawsuits, the afterlife

### Takeaway
A real story with a named protagonist, a datable turn and an ending: a profitable, bootstrapped web consultancy takes $38 million in March 2000, the founder is pushed out within a year, retakes the company by shareholder consent in early April 2001, is sued in Delaware on April 11, 2001, settles in June 2001 on confidential terms, and the remains go to Red Hat in February 2002. The weakness is that almost all the detail comes from one side (Greenspun's essay); the two best counter-accounts (Eve Andersson's diary, Michael Yoon's "Alternate Perspective") exist but could not be opened here, and the widely repeated $7.6 million settlement figure is not documented anywhere I could open other than Wikipedia.

### Cited Findings

**Greenspun's account (one side's account; label it as such)**
- The essay "ArsDigita: From Start-Up to Bust-Up" is marked "copyright 2001 Philip Greenspun" and carries reader comments dated April 24 to 26, 2001. The copy I opened is on Andy Baio's waxy.org, not on Greenspun's own site; the URL on philip.greenspun.com that I tried returned 404. — [waxy.org mirror](https://random.waxy.org/arsdigita/); [404 at philip.greenspun.com](https://philip.greenspun.com/ancient-history/arsdigita/arsdigita)
- Starting capital, per Greenspun: "Not so bad considering that we built everything from a $10,000 investment." — [waxy.org mirror](https://random.waxy.org/arsdigita/)
- Size before the investment, per Greenspun: "By March 2000 we had grown to 80 people." Service revenue was running at "a $20 million/year rate." "We'd paid nearly $1 million in income tax on our profits for calendar year 1999." — [waxy.org mirror](https://random.waxy.org/arsdigita/)
- The financing, per Greenspun: "At the end of March 2000 we closed a venture capital financing with Greylock and General Atlantic." "By the time a couple of small checks arrived we had an extra $38 million to put in the bank." "the VCs purchased stock that gave them about 30% of the issued shares." Board of seven: one Greylock seat, one General Atlantic seat, three ArsDigita officers including the CEO, two outsiders. — [waxy.org mirror](https://random.waxy.org/arsdigita/)
- People named by Greenspun: Allen Shaheen hired as CEO (Greenspun says Shaheen came from Cambridge Technology Partners); Jim Jordan CFO; Richard Buck SVP Engineering; Dave Menninger SVP Marketing; Peter Bloom (General Atlantic) and Chip Hazard (Greylock) as the investors' directors. — [waxy.org mirror](https://random.waxy.org/arsdigita/)
- Greenspun's characterisation of the year April 2000 to March 2001: the three "exercised absolute power over ArsDigita Corporation" and "spent $20 million to get back to the same revenue that I had when I was CEO." Headcount grew to "nearly 200" by his account. He also says the company "declined Microsoft's offer (summer 2000)" to be first with an enterprise .NET product. — [waxy.org mirror](https://random.waxy.org/arsdigita/)
- The turn, per Greenspun: "After December 2000 they stopped having board meetings altogether". On March 28, 2001 Peter Bloom emailed suggesting Greenspun "resign from the board before our next meeting." Shareholders then approved a written consent electing Greenspun CEO and adding Tracy Adams and Eve Andersson to the board; on April 6, 2001 Shaheen was demoted from "President and CEO" to "President." — [waxy.org mirror](https://random.waxy.org/arsdigita/)
- The lawsuit, per Greenspun: filed "in Delaware chancery court" on April 11, 2001, Civil Action No. 18821. Plaintiffs: Allen Shaheen, Ernest Blackwelder, General Atlantic Partners 64, L.P., Greylock X Limited Partnership and ArsDigita Corporation. Defendants: Philip Greenspun, Eve A. Andersson, Tracy E. Adams. He says company funds paid "a quarter million dollars" to the investors' attorneys. — [waxy.org mirror](https://random.waxy.org/arsdigita/)

**What the company made and why developers cared (pointer-level only)**
- Wikipedia lists founders Philip Greenspun, Tracy Adams, Ben Adida, Eve Andersson, Olin Shivers, Aurelius Prochazka and Jin Choi; founding in Cambridge, Massachusetts in 1997; a peak of about 180 employees; offices in Cambridge, Berkeley, Washington D.C. and Ann Arbor; the ArsDigita Prize in 1999, 2000 and 2001; ArsDigita University running September 2000 to July 2001; and recruiting rewards of a Honda S2000 for 5 hires and a Ferrari F355 for 10. - Other participants' accounts exist: Eve Andersson, "Diary of a Start-Up", and Michael Yoon, "ArsDigita: An Alternate Perspective". ### Inferences
- Two-sentence test passes: Greenspun wanted money to grow a profitable open-source web shop without giving up control, but the investors and the CEO they hired sidelined the founders, so the founders used their majority of shares to retake the company and were sued for it within days; the company settled with its founder and was dead eight months later.
- The nameable turn is the week of April 6 to 11, 2001 (demotion of the CEO by shareholder consent, then the Delaware suit).
- The story is well known to Hacker News-era readers through Greenspun's essay and *Founders at Work*, but the essay is one-sided and is no longer on its author's site. The fresh angle would be the other accounts (Andersson, Yoon, the investors) and the afterlife (OpenACS still running; ADU lectures; Red Hat).
- The disappearance of the essay from philip.greenspun.com may be related to the confidential settlement, but I found no source saying so. Treat as a guess.

### Gaps
- The $7.6 million figure: no source beyond Wikipedia. Do not print it as fact.
- Exact settlement date: Linux.com's report is dated June 22, 2001; the date the agreement was signed is not established.
- Eve Andersson's diary and Michael Yoon's essay: could not open (archive.org blocked, live sites down). Archive URLs to try: `web.archive.org/web/20041214094043/http://www.eveandersson.com/arsdigita-history` and `web.archive.org/web/20130115123445/http://michael.yoon.org/arsdigita.php`.
- The investors' and Shaheen's side: no statement found. Boston Globe coverage (Hiawatha Bray wrote on ADU in 2000) not located.
- Red Hat price, how many staff moved, and what Red Hat did with the Java ACS: not established. OpenACS history page not opened.
- The book *Philip and Alex's Guide to Web Publishing*, and the Tcl/AOLserver/Oracle stack: not researched here beyond common knowledge; need primary pages.
- No Delaware Chancery docket was opened; the case number is from Greenspun's essay only.

## CDDB and Gracenote, 1993 to 2001: volunteers, sale, licence change, lockout, freedb, Roxio

### Takeaway
A solid story with named people, a clear betrayal as the volunteers saw it, and a tidy ending (Gracenote sues its own ex-customer for switching to the fork, loses the restraining order, then settles and keeps the customer). It is moderately well told already (Wired 2004 and 2006, EFF 2021), but the dates of the key steps are inconsistent across sources and the Roxio litigation is thinly documented.

### Cited Findings
- Origins: Ti Kan created CDDB around late 1993 as a local database for the xmcd CD player; the xmcd change history first uses the name in version 1.1, February 25, 1994. In 1995 Kan enlisted Steve Scherf to build the network version; Graham Toal provided hosting. (Wikipedia, citing Kan's own xmcd changelog.) — [Wikipedia: CDDB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CDDB); changelog at [ibiblio](http://www.ibiblio.org/tkan/xmcd/changes.txt) (not opened)
- How the data got there: when a CD was not in the database, the client prompted the user to type in the album details, which were uploaded for future users. — [EFF, Danny O'Brien, 2021-05-06](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2021/05/outliving-outrage-public-interest-internet-cddb-story)
- What contributors were told: in 1997 cddb.com's download and support pages said the server software and database were GPL-licensed (Wikipedia citing archived cddb.com pages of June 24, 1997); a 1999 press release said access would "remain 100% free to software developers and consumers." (Wikipedia, citing the April 27, 1999 release.) — [Wikipedia: Gracenote licensing controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gracenote_licensing_controversy); [Wikipedia: CDDB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CDDB)
- Sale: 1998, to Escient, an Indiana consumer-electronics maker owned by Scott Jones; in 1998 CDDB also asserted ownership of submitted data and barred redistribution. Renamed Gracenote in a release dated July 24, 2000 ("CDDB Re-Launches As Gracenote With New Products"). No sale price found. — [Wikipedia: Gracenote licensing controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gracenote_licensing_controversy); [Wired 2004 via mirror](https://wirednews.pages.dev/2004/07/the-house-that-music-fans-built)
- Patent: U.S. Patent 6,061,680, inventors Steven Douglas Scherf, Ti Kan and Dale Tyson Roberts, assignee CDDB, Inc. Wikipedia's text and its own reference list disagree on the issue date (May 2005 in the text, 2000-05-09 in the references); a Slashdot commenter says it was filed July 1999. — [Wikipedia: Gracenote licensing controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gracenote_licensing_controversy)
- Lockout: in March 2001 only licensed applications could reach the database, and new CDDB1 licences stopped, forcing a move to the incompatible CDDB2. — [Wikipedia: CDDB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CDDB)
- Roxio: Roxio's Gracenote agreement expired April 22, 2001 and it began using freedb; Gracenote sued (reported May 10 to 11, 2001) alleging patent infringement, breach of contract and, per Slashdot's quotation of the claims, "violating the Digital Millennium Copyright Act by offering products that circumvent Gracenote's technological measures" to reach "an unauthorized derivative of the CDDB copyrighted database." Gracenote general counsel Dave Marglin is reported as saying Roxio wanted for free what others pay for; Gracenote claimed about 1,800 commercial licensees. — [Slashdot 2001-05-11](https://news.slashdot.org/story/01/05/11/1247214/gracenote-sues-roxio-over-switch-to-free-song-database); [MacTech 2001-05-11](https://www.mactech.com/2001/05/11/roxio-hit-with-lawsuit) (search result only)
- Roxio, continued: Gracenote sought a temporary restraining order on May 17, 2001; the court denied it on May 24, 2001, finding no likelihood of success shown; Roxio countersued in June 2001 alleging the patent and the CDDB trademark were obtained by withholding information from the USPTO. (Wikipedia, citing a Roxio press release.) — [Wikipedia: Gracenote licensing controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gracenote_licensing_controversy)
- How it ended: Wired in 2004 says Roxio "later agreed to settle for an undisclosed sum and is currently a Gracenote customer." — [Wired, Katie Dean, 2004-07-07, via mirror](https://wirednews.pages.dev/2004/07/the-house-that-music-fans-built) (canonical: https://www.wired.com/2004/07/the-house-that-music-fans-built/, not opened)
- Ti Kan in 2004, by email to Wired: "That fact is, at the time, we _had_ to do what we did," and "CDDB was generating so much commercial interest, the original principals were ill-prepared to handle it." Kan was then an occasional consultant to Gracenote; Scherf and Ty Roberts were still there. — [Wired 2004 via mirror](https://wirednews.pages.dev/2004/07/the-house-that-music-fans-built)
- Robert Kaye of MusicBrainz in the same piece: "They're the bad kid on the block," and "Everything that they did by taking the resource private was perfectly legal,". — [Wired 2004 via mirror](https://wirednews.pages.dev/2004/07/the-house-that-music-fans-built)
- 2004 numbers: 10,000 submissions a day from 160 countries in 65 languages; revenue up 90 percent from 2002 to 2003; Scott Jones and Sequoia Capital each held 20 to 25 percent. — [Wired 2004 via mirror](https://wirednews.pages.dev/2004/07/the-house-that-music-fans-built)
- Steve Scherf in 2006 (Wired Q&A by Eliot Van Buskirk, November 13, 2006): "The clock started ticking when one company made us a serious, unsolicited offer." "We needed to find a safe haven soon, and along came Escient." "I loved CDDB and could not give it up, so we had to find a company willing to take it on." "We owe everything to our users." "The plain fact is, you can't close something that has been released under the GPL." "If you look at how stagnant efforts like freedb have been, you'll see what I mean." On Kan's start: "Ti got fed up with them because they were all talk and no action". Scherf also said data submitted before commercialisation was downloadable from freedb.org. — [Wired 2006 via mirror](https://wirednews.pages.dev/2006/11/gracenote-defends-its-evolution) (canonical: https://www.wired.com/2006/11/gracenote-defends-its-evolution/, not opened)
- EFF's 2021 judgment: Scherf "fully expect[ed] our disc-recognition service to be running for decades to come" and doubted the forks would last; freedb closed in 2020 but gnudb, derived from it, still runs. O'Brien draws the IMDb parallel explicitly (the Cardiff movie database; same "only way to fund it" reasoning). — [EFF 2021](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2021/05/outliving-outrage-public-interest-internet-cddb-story)
- Afterlife of the company: Sony completed its acquisition June 2008; Tribune bought it in 2014; Nielsen bought it in December 2016 (Deadline headline: $560M). (Wikipedia.) — [Wikipedia: CDDB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CDDB)
- Afterlife of the fork: freedb was acquired by Magix in 2006 and shut down in 2020 (heise, June 23, 2020, cited by Wikipedia; not opened). EFF's follow-up piece covers MusicBrainz as the organised successor. — [Wikipedia: CDDB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CDDB); [EFF on MusicBrainz, 2021-06](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2021/06/organizing-public-interest-musicbrainz) (not opened)

### Inferences
- Two-sentence test passes: Kan and Scherf wanted every CD player to know track names and got thousands of strangers to type them in under a GPL banner; then they sold, the owner locked out unlicensed players and sued a customer for using the volunteers' fork, so the volunteers rebuilt the database twice (freedb, MusicBrainz).
- The best ending is ironic and documented: Scherf predicted in 2006 that the forks would stagnate and Gracenote's CD service would run "for decades"; the CD faded, and a fork (gnudb) and MusicBrainz are what is still open.
- The audience angle is strong: anyone who used Winamp, iTunes or a ripper typed into or read from this database. The present-day echo (user-contributed data later enclosed, e.g. arguments over Q&A sites and AI training) is my framing, not something a source here says, apart from EFF's IMDb comparison.

### Gaps
- Date of freedb's founding: Wikipedia's CDDB page says 2001, its licensing-controversy page says 1999. Unresolved. The archived "Why freedb.org?" page would settle the fork's own account; not opened.
- Exact date of the Escient sale in 1998 and the price: not found.
- Roxio: court name, case number, filing date and settlement date not found; the claim that one patent was partly invalidated is uncited on Wikipedia. Do not use.
- The claim that Gracenote "banned" freedb-style apps in March 2001 rests on Wikipedia.
- I did not open any archived cddb.com page, any Slashdot interview with Kan or Scherf, or The Register's coverage.
- The 2006 Wired Q&A does not mention regrets or Roxio.

## Peacefire versus CYBERsitter, 1996 to 1997

### Takeaway
A compact story with a clear sequence, a teenager as protagonist and a company president who put his threats in writing; the primary material exists (Wired News of December 9, 1996, Milburn's emails and the April 24, 1997 demand letter, the MIT SAFE archive, Peacefire's own pages) but much of it was unreachable from here. The weak point is the ending: I found threats but no lawsuit and no documented resolution, so "how it ended" is currently "it fizzled", which needs confirming.

### Cited Findings
- Bennett Haselton founded Peacefire in August 1996, aged 17; by December 1996 it had about 100 members averaging 15 years old; by April 1997 about 650 members averaging 16, and Haselton was 18 and a junior at Vanderbilt. — [Wired, Rebecca Vesely, 1996-12-09, via mirror](https://askhoma.pages.dev/1996/12/cybersitter-goes-after-teen/); [Wired, Jon Katz, 1997-04-18, via mirror](https://wirednews.pages.dev/1997/04/to-be-young-cyber-and-free)
- Before Peacefire's page: journalists Declan McCullagh and Brock Meeks reported that CYBERsitter blocked the National Organization for Women's site and the whole well.com domain; Jonathan Wallace says Solid Oak asked the FBI to investigate them and accused Haselton of being their source, which all three denied. (Wallace's account; Wallace was himself in dispute with Solid Oak.) — [The Ethical Spectacle](https://spectacle.org/alert/cs.html)
- November 1996: Haselton posts an article on Peacefire naming sites CYBERsitter blocks. Friday (by inference December 6, 1996): Haselton contacts Solid Oak president Brian Milburn; shortly afterwards peacefire.org is on the block list, and Milburn emails Peacefire's host, Media3, asking it to remove Peacefire or see all of Media3 (about 2,500 domains) blocked. Media3 administrator Joe Hayes refuses, says no proof of illegality was offered, and says Media3 will take legal action if blocked. — [Wired 1996-12-09 via mirror](https://askhoma.pages.dev/1996/12/cybersitter-goes-after-teen/) (canonical: https://www.wired.com/1996/12/cybersitter-goes-after-teen/, not opened)
- Milburn in writing, as quoted by Wired: "According to our sources, he has engaged in illegal criminal copyright violations"; Haselton "has made it his mission in life to defame our product"; "We realize this is an extreme measure and regret that our customers will no longer have access". — [Wired 1996-12-09 via mirror](https://askhoma.pages.dev/1996/12/cybersitter-goes-after-teen/)
- Haselton: "It's pretty ironic that Cybersitter, which is supposed to help kids, is blocking a student-run organization". — [Wired 1996-12-09 via mirror](https://askhoma.pages.dev/1996/12/cybersitter-goes-after-teen/)
- Peacefire's own notice (relayed in the French newsletter lambda 2.13, December 1996): "Sometime today or yesterday, CYBERsitter put www.Peacefire.org on its list of blocked sites." dated December 7, 1996; Haselton's mail to Solid Oak then bounced with an automated notice, "this account has been configured to reject all messages on this topic...". CPSR sent Solid Oak a protest letter on December 27, 1996 and reported yahoo.com fully or partly blocked in its testing. — [lambda 2.13 in cypherpunks archive](https://mailing-list-archive.cryptoanarchy.wiki/archive/1996/12/f59f5ebb1b76bde5e628432178b462af404ed236f039de9c53831954ad90efd5)
- Block list shown to include, across these sources: now.org, mojo.com (Mother Jones), members.gnn.com, c2.org, well.com, peacefire.org, and later the whole of spectacle.org including "An Auschwitz Alphabet". Gay Today adds the International Gay and Lesbian Human Rights Commission. — [lambda 2.13](https://mailing-list-archive.cryptoanarchy.wiki/archive/1996/12/f59f5ebb1b76bde5e628432178b462af404ed236f039de9c53831954ad90efd5); [Jonathan Wallace, "403 Forbidden", via mirror](https://internetunderground.pages.dev/iu/archive/issue16/403f16); [Gay Today, Patricia Conklin, 1997-05-05](https://gaytoday.com/garchive/tech/050597te.htm)
- The critic's critic gets blocked: Wallace added a "Don't Buy Cybersitter" link to The Ethical Spectacle; Solid Oak blocked spectacle.org on January 19, 1997. Wallace says Milburn called Haselton an "aspiring felon" and replied to Wallace "Do not contact this company again."; a second reply read "Your 'appeal' is noted. DO NOT SEND E- MAIL TO THIS DOMAIN AGAIN." Wallace says Milburn and marketing executive Marc Kanter described him to the press as a "publicity seeker". (All Wallace's account.) — [The Ethical Spectacle](https://spectacle.org/alert/cs.html); ["403 Forbidden" via mirror](https://internetunderground.pages.dev/iu/archive/issue16/403f16)
- Second round, April 1997: Haselton posted a DOS program that decrypts CYBERsitter's filter file; Wired reported it on Monday April 21, 1997. Milburn sent a certified demand letter dated April 24, 1997: "This program illegally modifies and decodes data and source code"; "We demand that this program be removed immediately."; and, about hyperlinks to Solid Oak's site, "We demand that these links be removed immediately." and "Further use of these links to our private facilities will be viewed as trespassing and intentional harassment." Milburn to Wired: "He is violating every intellectual property law ever written,". Haselton to Wired: "I think they are misrepresenting their product,". — [Declan McCullagh post, 1997-04-24, cypherpunks archive](https://mailing-list-archive.cryptoanarchy.wiki/archive/1997/04/ad037090ff25f9e1a2eb08dbea9e68515993c09bc250aa50d8bc15959f00f51f)
- Milburn's reply to the charge of false advertising: "never misrepresented our product---ever." Haselton: "I've talked to a lawyer who offered to represent me in the event that Cybersitter goes after me." — [Gay Today 1997-05-05](https://gaytoday.com/garchive/tech/050597te.htm)
- A law firm for Haselton replied in May 1997, and McCullagh reported Milburn still threatening suit in July 1997. (Search-result summaries of MIT SAFE pages `decrypt-sue-threat` and `decrypt-sue-reply` and of Computer Underground Digest; the pages themselves returned 403/404.) — [MIT SAFE: decrypt-sue-reply](https://www.mit.edu/activities/safe/labeling/cybersitter/decrypt-sue-reply) (not opened)
- Afterwards, Haselton (Wikipedia, pointer only): born November 20, 1978; master's in mathematics from Vanderbilt; seven months on Visual Basic at Microsoft; a $15,000 Netscape bug bounty in 2001; small-claims wins against spammers in 2002; testified to the COPA Commission at 21; ACLU expert witness in 2007; ran Circumventor. — [Wikipedia: Bennett Haselton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bennett_Haselton)
- Afterwards, Solid Oak: in January 2010 the company sued the Chinese government, two Chinese software firms and PC makers including Sony, Lenovo, Toshiba, Acer, Asus and BenQ for $2.2 billion, alleging that about 3,000 lines of CYBERsitter code were copied into China's Green Dam censorware. Attorney Greg Fayer: "This lawsuit aims to strike a blow against the all-too-common practices of foreign software manufacturers". — [The Register, John Oates, 2010-01-06](https://www.theregister.com/2010/01/06/china_green_dam/)

### Inferences
- Two-sentence test passes: a 17-year-old wanted to show what a "parental control" product really blocked, so its maker blocked him, threatened to block his host's 2,500 other customers, and later claimed that linking to its site was trespass; the threats produced no lawsuit that I could find, and the list he exposed became the standard evidence against filter makers.
- The strongest surprise is the afterlife symmetry: the company that called decoding its block list a violation of "every intellectual property law ever written" later went to court saying a government's censorware had lifted its code, on evidence that came from outside researchers reading Green Dam's files. That juxtaposition is mine; the two facts are sourced separately.
- Coverage is light: contemporaneous Wired News items, mailing lists and Wallace's essays; it appears in censorware histories but I found no long-form retelling. Under-reported for this audience.

### Gaps
- No documented ending. I found no suit by Solid Oak against Haselton, no retraction and no date when peacefire.org came off the list. Needs Peacefire's archived CYBERsitter pages and later Wired News coverage.
- The full text of Milburn's December 1996 email to Media3 and of the April 24, 1997 letter: the MIT SAFE archive (`mit.edu/activities/safe/labeling/cybersitter/`) holds them, but returned 403/404 here. Only excerpts via Wired and McCullagh.
- The "aspiring felon" phrase rests on Wallace's account only.
- Outcome of the $2.2 billion Green Dam suit not established (a separate CBS Interactive matter was reported settled confidentially; search result only). University of Michigan as the source of the code comparison is from a search result, not an opened page.
- Milburn's and Kanter's own later statements about the episode: none found.
- Gender: no source opened states pronouns for Milburn or Kanter; Wired's quotes use "he" for Haselton.

## New candidates in the same territory

### Takeaway
The best new find is Hotline (1996 to 2001): a 17-year-old Australian writes the software, the fans who form a company around it end up obtaining a search order against its author after the source code is encrypted, and a court gives the code to the company, which then dies anyway. The AOL volunteer lawsuit (1999 to 2010) is the strongest "community versus its unpaid workers" story and ties directly to CDDB; TextDrive's "lifetime" hosting (2004 to 2014) is closest to this audience but its ending is the least well sourced.

### Cited Findings

**Candidate D: Hotline Communications versus Adam Hinkley, 1996 to 2001**
- Two-sentence story: Adam Hinkley wanted to ship the chat-and-files program they wrote at 17 and let fans turn it into a company in Toronto; within a year Hinkley left with the code and encrypted what stayed, so the company and a former employer took Hinkley to court on two continents, won the copyright, and went out of business months later.
- Hinkley ("Hinks") began Hotline in 1996 aged 17. Regulars persuaded Hinkley to form a company in early 1997; Hotline Communications Limited formed July 1997; debuted at MacWorld Boston August 1997; investor Austin Page joined late 1997 with $500,000 promised. More than half the founders were teenagers. — [Salon, Janelle Brown, "Hotline's Civil War", 1999-02-25](https://www.salon.com/1999/02/25/feature_224/)
- Roks: "We all met on Hotline, we had never met in any way on the Net before." — [Salon 1999](https://www.salon.com/1999/02/25/feature_224/)
- The turn: in March 1998 Hinkley shut the website, left with the AppWarrior library code and encrypted what remained. In September 1998 Hinkley's home in Australia was searched and disks seized. Around January 1999 a Canadian judge ruled for Hotline on AppWarrior; by February 1999 Hinkley's lawyers had conceded in the Australian court that the conduct with the code was wrongful. — [Salon 1999](https://www.salon.com/1999/02/25/feature_224/)
- Each side, per Salon: Hinkley's family said Hinkley was overworked far from home and pressured into a shareholder agreement not understood, and that an oral agreement left AppWarrior with Hinkley; mother Meg Lehmann: "His subsequent experiences with them have been emotionally very draining and expensive for him and his family." The company said the code was company property; lawyer Simon Wilson: "No one's going to invest in a company that doesn't have exclusive control of its own property." CEO Steve Bielawski said Hinkley remained the largest shareholder. Salon also reports, from affidavits, that Hinkley's younger sister was murdered a few months before the shareholder agreements were signed. — [Salon 1999](https://www.salon.com/1999/02/25/feature_224/)
- Court record: *Hotline Communications Ltd v Hinkley & Ors* [1999] VSC 74, Supreme Court of Victoria, March 24, 1999, concerning an Anton Piller (search) order and an order to disclose the encryption key (search-result summary of a law-firm note; page did not load). Final judgment: *Redrock Holdings Pty Ltd & Hotline Communications Ltd & Ors v Hinkley* [2001] VSC 91, April 4, 2001; Hinkley lost copyright in AppWarrior and rights in Hotline, partly because parts of AppWarrior were written while employed by Redrock. (Wikipedia; AustLII returned 403.) — [Wikipedia: Hotline Communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotline_Communications); [AustLII judgment](http://www.austlii.edu.au/au/cases/vic/VSC/2001/91.html) (not opened)
- Ending: Slashdot, April 9, 2001: by 22 Hinkley was broke and had lost the IP, and published warnings for young programmers, including "NEVER write your own software while you are an employee of someone else, EVEN if you do it in your own time" (as quoted by a commenter from hinks.hypermart.net). The company lost most of its funding, laid off most staff in September 2001 and closed later that year (Wikipedia). — [Slashdot 2001-04-09](https://slashdot.org/story/01/04/09/1639212/adam-hinkleys-ip-hindsights); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotline_Communications)
- Best primary sources: the two VSC judgments on AustLII; Salon's two-part 1999 series; Nathan Cochrane's reporting in The Age; ABC Radio National Law Report transcript; Hinkley's own page (hinks.hypermart.net, archive needed); Wired "Street Cred", September 1997.
- Caution: a family tragedy and a then-teenager are part of the record. Handle with restraint; both sides are living people.

**Candidate E: AOL's Community Leaders, 1999 to 2010**
- Two-sentence story: thousands of volunteers ran AOL's chat rooms and forums in exchange for free accounts and a sense of ownership; when AOL cut the perks and sold ads against their work, two of them sued for wages, and eleven years later AOL paid $15 million, having long since closed the programme.
- Kelly Hallisey and Brian Williams filed a class action in May 1999 under the Fair Labor Standards Act; the New York Times reported it May 26, 1999. Williams reported 3,000 hours over two years, and had tried to organise a strike before being removed. Williams: "They're making thousands and thousands of dollars off your slave labor, off each and every member." (NYT, as quoted by Priceonomics.) — [Priceonomics, 2014-08-21](https://priceonomics.com/the-aol-chat-room-monitor-revolt/)
- Scale: about 14,000 volunteers at peak including 350 minors (Wikipedia); about 10,000 in 1999 (Priceonomics). Volunteers had applications, training, minimum hours and timecards. — [Wikipedia: AOL Community Leader Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AOL_Community_Leader_Program); [Priceonomics](https://priceonomics.com/the-aol-chat-room-monitor-revolt/)
- Dates: Department of Labor closed its inquiry in 2001 without a finding against AOL; programme ended June 8, 2005; class certified February 2008; $15 million settlement, preliminary approval February 2010 and final approval May 2010 (the final-approval date is flagged uncited on Wikipedia), split roughly in thirds between volunteers, lawyers and charity (Priceonomics). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AOL_Community_Leader_Program); [Priceonomics](https://priceonomics.com/the-aol-chat-room-monitor-revolt/)
- Best primary sources: Lisa Margonelli, "Inside AOL's 'Cyber-Sweatshop'", Wired, October 1999 (https://www.wired.com/1999/10/volunteers/, not opened); NYT May 26, 1999; SDNY docket for *Hallissey v. America Online*; overtimelaw.com case page. Spelling of the surname varies (Hallisey / Hallissey).
- Coverage: moderate. A Wired feature, a Priceonomics retrospective and academic work exist. Known to media scholars, less to developers. It pairs naturally with CDDB (same years, same question of who owns volunteer work).

**Candidate F: TextDrive's "lifetime" hosting, 2004 to 2014**
- Two-sentence story: Dean Allen and Jason Hoffman funded a hosting company in 2004 by selling a few hundred developers hosting "for the lifetime of the company"; in 2012 the venture-funded successor, Joyent, ended those accounts by email, Allen came back to revive TextDrive and honour them, and that revival also shut down.
- TextDrive was founded in 2004 by Dean Allen and Jason Hoffman and bootstrapped by customers paying up front for lifetime hosting; Joyent acquired it in 2005; a 2006 offer promised service "As long as we exist." — [TechCrunch, Klint Finley, 2012-08-16](https://techcrunch.com/?p=629889)
- On August 16, 2012 Joyent emailed lifetime customers that the accounts were ending; Joyent had closed an $85 million round in January 2012. Hoffman: the hardware is "simply old (6-8 years old), it's failing," and "I'm sorry that I've lost that trust and I've upset you." Marco Arment: "'lifetime' never means your lifetime." — [TechCrunch 2012-08-16](https://techcrunch.com/?p=629889)
- On August 30, 2012 Allen announced a relaunch of TextDrive as a separate company that would honour the lifetime deals (Wikipedia mirror, search result only). A Google Groups thread is titled "Textdrive to shut down - 14 March" (year not confirmed; likely 2014). — [TechCrunch follow-up](https://techcrunch.com/?p=638334) (not opened); [ex-joyeurs group](https://groups.google.com/g/ex-joyeurs/c/-wAUbE_2Y-A) (not opened)
- Price and count disagree: TechCrunch says $500 for the 2006 offer, a commenter says $200; "original 200" backers (the VC200) is from a Hacker News commenter, Drew McLellan.
- Why this audience: TextDrive was where much of the early Rails and Textpattern community hosted; the customers were web developers. Coverage is thin (tech-news items, forum threads), and no long-form account was found.

**Considered and set aside**

Well documented and well sourced, but widely known to anyone who used a PHP CMS. — [Open letter, reposted by signatory Emir Sakic](https://www.sakic.net/news/mambo-open-source-development-team-letter-to-the-community/); [Wikipedia: Mambo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mambo_(software))
