Cornify: unicorns on every page

On the last day of January 2009, somebody asked Hacker News to review a website whose only feature was putting glittery unicorns on other people's websites, and a little over thirteen months later it won the popular vote at South by Southwest.

cornify_add();

That is the whole programming interface. Each call drops one sparkly unicorn, or one rainbow, at a random spot on top of whatever page you happen to be reading. The site that serves the pictures describes itself as "the #1 unicorn and rainbow service on the internet, and quite possibly in the world," a ranking nobody has challenged.

The launch post went up on January 31, 2009, under the title "Review my website: Cornify", from an account also named Cornify. It read like a startup pitch, which was the joke:

Cornify is all about giving internet users choice of customization. MySpace is a great example of a successful company with a similar vision. Facebook with its Windows 3.1 look - not so much. Cornify provides a service that allows users to cover any website in a beautiful array of unicorn and rainbow imagery - taking back the web one 'corn at a time.

Cornify came in two shapes: a button a site owner could paste into their own page, and a link you could drag to your bookmarks bar and then press on anybody's page. The second was a bookmarklet, a kind of program that had been around for a decade by then, and Cornify may be the most widely seen joke ever told in it. I can't prove that, but I can't think of a rival that made ESPN's home page.

Bookmarks that run code

A bookmarklet is a bookmark whose address starts with javascript: where you would expect http:. Click it and the browser runs the rest of the address as script, inside the page you are already on, with all the rights of that page. Jay Hoffmann's history of the bookmarklet quotes Netscape's own JavaScript guide telling developers that "you can put any statements or function calls after the javascript: URL prefix."

The name came later. Hoffmann dates it to December 1998, when Steve Kangas launched Bookmarklets.com. Kangas's About page is still up, and it states the idea better than anyone has since: "Bookmarklets allow anybody to use JavaScript - on whatever page you choose (not just your own page)." Asked further down whether bookmarklets are the coolest thing since sliced bread, the page answers: "Um, maybe. Sliced bread is pretty cool."

For ten years the form was mostly earnest. Bookmarklets validated your markup, opened every link on a page, and posted the current address to whichever service you were loyal to that year. The whole program had to fit in a URL, so each one did a single job. What they did not have was an installer, a store, a reviewer or a permission dialog. A bookmarklet was a link, and you gave it to someone the way you gave them any other link.

By 2009 site owners had a similar habit. Sharing widgets such as AddThis had taught bloggers that adding a feature meant pasting in one script tag from a company's server and getting a little button in return. The Cornify launch post ends with a credit: "this concept was heavily inspired by AddThis." The Know Your Meme entry, which is user-written, puts it more bluntly: the goal was "to come up with the most ridiculous single-feature web company."

A launch thread in character

The Hacker News thread is the best record of the first day. It collected 61 points and a few dozen comments, most of them playing along. One commenter predicted the thing would "go viral" and advised seeking venture capital at once. The Cornify account answered in the voice it would mostly keep: "No worries, the business model is fully thought through. We're expecting to be profitable in phase 3."

Remember the date. In January 2009 the economy had fallen over, and the thread knows it. A commenter who installed the button wrote, "Who said this is going to be a bad year for IPOs?" I read the thread as a room full of startup people enjoying a parody of their own pitch decks.

The thread also shows the form at work. A few hours in, a commenter posting as tlrobinson wrote a bookmarklet of their own. When someone couldn't get it to run, they posted this tidier version about 22 hours later, with the instruction to "remove the newlines":

javascript:(function(){if(typeof cornify_add==="undefined"){
document.body.appendChild(document.createElement('script')).src=
'http://www.cornify.com/js/cornify.js';}
else{cornify_add()}})();

That is the standard shape of the loader bookmarklet, for programs too big to fit in a URL. It makes a script element, points it at a file on somebody's server, and appends it to the page. The file then runs as if the page's author had put it there. The bookmarklet on Cornify's button page now does the same with more ceremony.

What the script does

The oldest copy of cornify.js I could read is the one committed to GitHub in April 2011, two years after launch. It is 138 lines, and it behaves the way people described in the spring of 2009, so I'll treat it as close to the original. The heart of it is this:

var img = document.createElement('img');
var currentTime = new Date();
var submitTime = currentTime.getTime();
if( cornify_count==15 ) submitTime = 0;
img.setAttribute('src',cornify_url+'getacorn.php?r=' + submitTime + '&url='+document.location.href);

The script has no idea what a unicorn looks like. It builds a div with position: fixed, gives it a random top and left, and puts an image inside whose address is a PHP page on cornify.com. The server picks the picture. The timestamp in r defeats the cache. The other parameter is less cute: url is the address of the page you are on, sent to cornify.com with every unicorn. The joke about being a "service" was true: a server learned where each unicorn landed. My guess is that this is how the counter on the home page, which reads a little over 444 million "unicorns and rainbows provided since 2009", was kept.

Clicking a unicorn makes another unicorn. On hover the image wobbles to a random angle and size, using CSS transforms and a 0.1-second transition. The 2011 source still has the commented-out attempts at other spellings and prefixes around the WebkitTransform line that was kept, an honest record of writing CSS animation in that period.

Then come two surprises. On the fifth click the script appends a stylesheet to the page. The file now served is a single rule:

h1, h2, h3, h4, h5, h6, h1 a, h2 a, h3 a, h4 a, h5 a, h6 a, a, a:link {
	color: #FF00FF;
	font-family: "Comic Sans MS", "Marker Felt";
}

The script then walks every heading on the page and sticks a word in front of it, chosen from a list: Happy, Sparkly, Glittery, Fun, Magical, Lovely, Cute, Charming, Amazing, Wonderful. On the fifteenth click, the picture is centred in the window and the cache-buster is set to zero. The current source calls this one "the grand unicorn." Both tricks were in place by April 10, 2009, when a Hacker News reader wrote that after five clicks "every piece of text gets a very happy modifier" and the Cornify account replied: "Try pressing 15 times for some extra sparkle."

Four days to Wired

On February 4, 2009, four days after launch, Bruce Sterling's Wired blog ran a post titled "Proof why the Cornify button is a great Internet invention". The whole post is a Flickr link, the line "A comprehensive map of all possible human futures," and an instruction: "Now Cornify! See how great that is? Wow!"

The same day, Paul Irish published a jQuery snippet that hid Cornify behind the Konami code, the old Nintendo cheat sequence. I'll come back to it, because it changed where the unicorns could live.

On April 1, Stack Overflow joined in openly. The company's blog recalled a year later that its 2009 April Fools' joke "added a cornify button to every post."

Then, on April 27, Kotaku's Michael McWhertor reported that typing the Konami code and Enter on ESPN.com would "infest the official ESPN web site with mystical ponies," with "a heaping help of Comic Sans," and that every story became "cute," "magical" or "sparkly." That matches the stylesheet and word list the script applies on its fifth call, so my guess is ESPN's wrapper called it several times. David Murphy, in a PCWorld piece carried by ABC News, filed it "right next to Slashdot's 'OMG Ponies' redesign in the Web Pranks Hall of Fame." An update stamped 4:44 p.m. says ESPN had removed it. Irish added a note to the post the same day: "In fact it had been up there for weeks." I found no report that named who at ESPN had done it, or confirmed whose Konami code they had used.

On March 14, 2010, ten days after the IE6 funeral, the SXSW Interactive Web Awards gave Cornify the People's Choice Award. The official results say more than 12,000 people signed up to vote. The Amusement category, which a student reviewer at Texas State had been sure Cornify would take, went to Atlas Obscura.

The include and its wrappers

For site owners, the product was one line, given here as the project's README has it now:

<script type="text/javascript" src="https://www.cornify.com/js/cornify.js"></script>

After that, anything on the page could call cornify_add(). People in the launch thread did so within hours: "I just cornified my blog," "Put it on my site," and, from tlrobinson, "I just added it to my personal website (down at the bottom)."

The include was a building block, and the best-known early thing built on it was Irish's snippet:

var kkeys = [], konami = "38,38,40,40,37,39,37,39,66,65";
$(document).keydown(function(e) {
  kkeys.push( e.keyCode );
  if ( kkeys.toString().indexOf( konami ) >= 0 ){
    $(document).unbind('keydown',arguments.callee);
    $.getScript('http://www.cornify.com/js/cornify.js',function(){
      cornify_add();
      $(document).keydown(cornify_add);
    });
  }
});

It records key codes until the list contains up, up, down, down, left, right, left, right, B, A, and only then fetches the script, with $.getScript. After that, Irish wrote, "every other keystroke will add happiness to the world." Later in 2009, after the ESPN episode, Cornify put the same trigger into cornify.js itself. The object is called cornami, and its comment says it was adapted from Konami-JS, a library George Mandis published on April 28, 2009, citing "the recent attention surrounding ESPN." From then on any site with the plain include had the secret knock too.

The wrappers kept coming. In May 2010 John Hobbs posted Konami Unicorn Blitz to Hacker News: fifty unicorns on the Konami code, with no dependencies, "so people can just drop it right in." Its page credits Eric Ryan Harrison with a jQuery version. The Cornify account turned up to say it looked "very, very inspired by Cornify" and to ask for the artwork. Hobbs said the unicorns came from a MySpace glitter-graphics page. "You have great taste in unicorns," Cornify replied.

That is most of the documented plugin history. I found snippets and one-file libraries, passed around in blog posts and comment threads, and no official jQuery plugin. Irish's later note on the post is modest: "this feature kinda triggered a meme."

Why a joke travelled

It cost nothing to pass on. There was no download and no account. The whole product was a link, and it worked on the page you were already looking at, so the demo and the punchline arrived together.

It used a channel the industry had already built. The paste-this-script-tag habit existed to distribute sharing buttons. A site owner who had pasted three widgets that week needed no instruction to paste a fourth.

Its first audience had production access. This is the force I'd weigh most. Cornify launched on a forum read by people who ran websites, and within three months it was running on Stack Overflow and ESPN.com. Irish's Konami wrapper mattered because it lowered the stakes: an Easter egg nobody sees is easier to ship than a button everybody does.

The tone was airtight. In almost every comment I read, the Cornify account stays in character. In April 2009 it brought a sister product, Hapylink, to Hacker News for review and defended it as "not a url-shortener, but a url-happifier." It also conceded a design flaw: "Most links are actually longer - but they are a lot happier and more like to viewers smile, which is the core focus of Hapylink."

Living with unicorns

The launch thread doubles as the first bug tracker. The earliest report was that unicorns land at random, including on top of the Cornify button. "I almost cried when a unicorn covered up the 'cornify' button and I had to stop," one reader wrote. Another supplied the developer's workaround, which was to skip the button and open Firebug:

for (var i = 0; i < 10000; ++i) { cornify_add() }

The second was that there was no way back. "An 'uncornify' bookmarklet might be nice," a reader suggested, since "sometimes it blocks something important." The 2011 script has no undo short of reloading the page. The current source does include an exit, a "rainbow cupcake button" that removes every unicorn, with a comment that it "should only be used in an emergency, since it's sad."

The third was Internet Explorer 7, where one visitor could not drag the link to the toolbar at all. Two days later the same visitor reported that it worked in Firefox, but that after the tab was closed "the 'music' kept running," and warned that people "in the wild" would be less polite. A fourth reader just wanted "[joke] or something in the title, so those of us short on time can skip it." A prank is only funny to the person who chose it, and ESPN's reaction on April 27 is the corporate form of the same feedback.

And one reader on day one named the real issue, in the middle of a joke review: "I am also a bit ashamed that I executed a stranger's Javascript in my Hacker News context. Who knows what just happened to my cookie?" They read the URL and decided they were safe, "at least for the javascript present the instant I downloaded it."

What it left behind

The defence against it is why it often doesn't work now. That last comment describes the risk exactly. A site that included cornify.js ran whatever cornify.com chose to serve that day, with full access to the page. I've found no sign Cornify ever abused that, but an unapproved script from another host is exactly what Content Security Policy lets a site refuse. When GitHub turned CSP on in April 2013, Joshua Peek's announcement warned that "it may cause issues with some browser extensions and bookmarklets." The spec said bookmarklets should be exempt, Peek wrote, but "none of the browsers get this correct." The current CSP draft still says a policy "SHOULD NOT interfere with the operation of user-agent features like addons, extensions, or bookmarklets." In practice, as far as I understand it, a loader bookmarklet that injects a script from another host is asking for exactly what a strict policy refuses. The web got safer from strangers' scripts, and on those sites the unicorn link went quiet.

The bookmarklet turned into the extension. Hoffmann's history ends with Firefox's add-ons gallery in 2007 and Chrome's extension gallery in 2009, after which "extensions began to overtake bookmarklets in popularity." Cornify's button page still offers the draggable link "for all browsers," and beneath it a Chrome extension and a Firefox one. The Chrome Web Store listing shows version 1.6.2, updated in May 2024, with 10,000 users. A joke that once needed nobody's permission now ships through a store. I think that trade was worth making, and I also think something was lost when "here, drag this" stopped being a way to hand a friend a program.

The parody became a genre, and then a dependency. Know Your Meme lists the imitators: Sharkify, Hoffify, Ninjafy. In March 2011 came Katamari Hack, a bookmarklet that rolls the page you're on into a ball, using the same loader. In June 2010 Newsweek's developers hid zombie headlines behind the Konami code, and The Register cited ESPN's unicorns as the precedent. The original, meanwhile, settled into maintenance. The GitHub history, kept by the account GBKS, whose profile gives the name Christoph Ono and links to the studio credited in Cornify's footer, shows a 2020 commit titled "The most amazing code cleanup and comments" and, that October, a pull request from Wes Bos adding TypeScript typings. A spoof of single-feature startups has outlasted a fair number of them and now has a type definition.

The pitch was a real argument. "Giving internet users choice of customization" was written as a parody of startup prose, with MySpace as the hero and Facebook's "Windows 3.1 look" as the villain. But it is also the bookmarklet's actual claim: that the person at the browser gets a say in what a page looks like after it arrives. I'd argue that claim survives in reader modes, content blockers and the console, and in that one sentence of the CSP draft, which says such features "advance the user's priority over page authors." Cornify made the case in magenta Comic Sans, and I think it was still a serious case.

The takeaway

Cornify was a joke about startups that turned out to be a very good demonstration of the web's loosest mechanism: any link can be a program, and any script tag is an open door to someone else's server. In 2009 that meant a unicorn could get from a forum thread to a sports network's home page in three months, carried by developers who thought it was funny. The same looseness is why browsers and sites spent the next decade adding policies, stores and reviews, and why the bookmarklet is now mostly a curiosity.

I don't think the lesson is that the old way was better. The reader who worried about their cookie was right. But the playful web of those years ran on trust that was mostly repaid, and Cornify is the proof: a stranger's script that has served, by its own count, more than 444 million pictures onto pages its author never saw, and as far as I can find did only what it said it would. The site is still up, the counter is still on the home page, and cornify_add() still produces a unicorn.

What I couldn't verify

  • I could not open the Internet Archive's 2009 captures of cornify.com or its script, so my account of the original code rests on the copy committed to GitHub in April 2011 and on what people described in 2009.
  • The history of the wrappers around the script is thin: I found Paul Irish's snippet, John Hobbs's Unicorn Blitz and a 2010 Hacker News thread, but no official jQuery plugin and no count of how many sites used any of them.
  • I found no report naming who put the unicorns on ESPN.com or confirming whose Konami code was used; that they sat there for weeks rests on one line in Paul Irish's post, The Register later dated the prank to April Fools' Day, and my suggestion that ESPN's wrapper called the script several times is a guess from how the reports describe it.
  • I name Christoph Ono only from the GitHub profile of the account that maintains the repository, which links to the studio in Cornify's footer; the Cornify site and its Hacker News account never give a person's name.
  • The claim that Cornify began as a spoof of single-feature startups comes from the user-written Know Your Meme entry, Stack Overflow's 2009 joke is known to me only from the company's 2010 blog post, and my guess that the url parameter fed the home-page counter is an inference from the code.
