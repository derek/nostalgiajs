The Firefox ad: a page of names in 4.5-point type

Ten thousand users of a free web browser bought it two pages of the New York Times in December 2004, on the promise of their names in the advertisement, and it was the names that made it late.

Are you fed up with your web browser? You're not alone.

Rob Davis was fed up. Davis lived in Minneapolis and worked as an account director at a PR firm, Haberman & Associates, and he had spent a weekend in the summer of 2004 indoors with a PC that had been taken over through Internet Explorer and put to work sending spam. Josh McHugh told the story in Wired early in 2005: Davis dropped out of a camping trip on Lake Superior, backed up his files, reformatted the drive, and drove to a programmer friend's house to copy a browser the friend had recommended. It was called Firefox, and it had not yet reached version 1.0. By December the question was the headline of an advertisement Davis had proposed, printed across the middle of a page of the names of the people who paid for it.

Spread Firefox

Firefox came from the Mozilla Foundation, a non-profit set up in July 2003 that called itself the heir to Netscape and that its chief engineer, Chris Hofmann, described in November 2004 as "a nonprofit foundation, a bunch of engineers". They decided to market the 1.0 release the way they wrote the code, by opening the door to anyone with an idea.

The door was a site called Spread Firefox, launched in September 2004. Wired credits Blake Ross, one of the browser's original developers, with starting it. It ran on CivicSpace, software that had come out of Howard Dean's presidential campaign, and it had the campaign's habits: members proposed stunts, rated each other's proposals and earned points. A press release that October counted close to 25,000 registered users after four weeks.

They had something to push against. Internet Explorer was the browser of about nine users in ten, by Wired's figure, and that year's malware had been arriving through it. Davis said he was no zealot about Microsoft. His complaint was that a company that made, by his reckoning, 98 percent of the world's operating systems had let this happen to people. "There's a lot of pain out there."

Thirty dollars for a name

Davis had gone to Ross in July, Wired says, two months before the site existed, with an idea: raise enough money from Firefox's fans to run an ad in the New York Times. None of the accounts says when the donors' names came into it, but by October they were the offer: a full page to mark the 1.0 release, with the names of the people who paid for it. Newspapers had carried donor-paid ads of that kind for decades, as Bruce Byfield's 2006 article on the tactic points out, usually a stand on some public issue, and Davis wanted one for a download. He worked on grassroots campaigns for a living, and the model he cited for getting Firefox's users to make its marketing was MoveOn's nationwide contest to make a 30-second ad.

The drive opened on Tuesday 19 October 2004. The press release called the effort "endorsed by the Mozilla Foundation" and was careful about whose work it was: the idea, the research and the design had all been done by volunteers. The terms survive, of all places, in Microsoft's archive of its old blogs, where a post from that day passes them along: $30 got your name into the ad, and students paid $10. The target, ZDNet UK reported, was 2,500 donors in ten days.

Two bloggers picked the release up the same day. John Battelle reprinted it under the remark that the word "irony" came to mind, without being able to say why. Jason Fried, on Signal vs. Noise, admired the hook of seeing your own name in the paper and then asked the obvious question: "Is putting money towards an ad in the Times the best way for a fledgling browser to spend precious money?" Davis, asked by ClickZ what the ad was for, said downloads were the secondary intent. Its first job was to thank the foundation and to speak for the people who had been spreading the browser.

A target passed on the first day

The target lasted seventeen hours, a figure Davis gave a few weeks later. By the Friday, Bart Decrem, Mozilla's marketing contact, put the fund at more than $100,000, which he reckoned would pay for a couple of ads in the Times. When the ten days were up it held $250,000 from 10,000 people, an average of $25 each.

Success changed what kind of problem the ad was. A page with 2,500 names on it is an advertisement with a long credit line, and a page with four times as many is a telephone directory with a logo. Davis: "We went back to the drawing board to figure out how many [donor] names we could include in the space and still run the message." They settled on 10,000. That was a limit on names, set once the first target had gone. When the drive closed, 10,000 was also the reported number of donors, and no account says whether the drive stopped at the limit or happened to end there.

Fitting the names on a broadsheet page

The design was led by a volunteer, Christopher Messina, whom Mozilla's press release describes as a San Francisco designer and a leader at Spread Firefox. Firefox 1.0 shipped on 9 November, and the ad, expected within a few weeks of the launch, did not appear that month.

What Mozilla's staff made of the problem survives in one place. Gervase Markham, commenting in 2013 under a Mozilla history post, said the only record they had of the ad was a staff-meeting agenda, and quoted it without a date:

  • Challenge to fit all the names legibly on one page
  • May end up taking out two ads in successive weeks
  • A double-page ad is ridiculously expensive
  • Ad may (co-incidentally) run around the time of 10M downloads

The designers' side of it reached print once. BetaNews reported, without saying who told it so, that a wrong setting in the layout program had left them waiting hours for the ad to render after adding a single name, that the fix took a weekend, and that the ad was two weeks later than hoped. ZDNet UK said only that fitting every donor's name on a single broadsheet page had been a technical challenge.

German fans got into print first. A separate drive raised over €48,000, the same ZDNet report says, and at the start of December it bought a full page in what Computerwoche names as the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung. The Times ad ran on 16 December, five weeks after the launch. No account says who decided it, but the option the agenda had called ridiculously expensive was the one taken: two pages, in a single issue, with the name of every donor, in Wired's words, "in barely readable, 4.5-point type".

Counting the names in the PDF

Mozilla posted the ad as a PDF with its press release, and the file is on Mozilla's archive site now. Page one is the names, a block above the headline and a block below it, with the Firefox logo ghosted over them, set in Univers Condensed Bold at a size the file gives as 4.5 points. Page two is the advertisement you would expect, logo and testimonials, down to a footer of thanks to the employees of eight companies. The first of the eight is Haberman & Associates, where Davis worked.

Most of the reports I found said the page held 10,000 names. ZDNet's did, and so did a donor, Ben Ramsey, who had enlarged the PDF to find the family's entries. BetaNews said 8,000. So I counted. The names in the PDF are separated by bullets, alphabetical by surname from Oliver Aaltonen, and there are 8,566 bullets, which makes 8,567 entries. BetaNews was nearer. Ten thousand people gave, and the page holds 1,433 fewer names than that, at least in Mozilla's file, which is what I counted; I have not seen the newsprint. Nothing I read explains the gap, though donors who gave without asking for a name would account for some of it.

Nor are the entries 8,567 separate people. Three names appear ten times each and two more appear five times. ZDNet had reported that anyone who signed up ten extra names would be made a "Community Champion", and my guess is that some champions supplied all ten themselves. At least 139 entries are families. Twenty are a single word, filed after the Zs, and after those comes the last entry of all, Eileen Veit, out of alphabetical order as if added late. Some donors got less than their $30 had promised: Byfield's article records complaints of misspelled names, and of not being told the date. One entry is a dog. Ramsey wrote that the list held three names from the Ramsey household, Ben, Liz and Ashley, and that Ashley was the dog, and all three are there under R.

There is a Rob Davis under D, in the same type as everyone else, and I am taking it to be him. There is a Blake Ross. Markham says that quite a few core Mozillians are not in the list: Markham did not submit a name, having "thought it was a thing for Americans", and adds that Mitchell is not there either, by which I take them to mean Mitchell Baker, the foundation's president. The list has no Gervase Markham and no Mitchell Baker. It does hold a Jason Fried and a John Battelle. I can't show they are the same two, but I would like them to be.

Where the Times ad went

The ad became a poster. The Mozilla Store sold it at full size for just under seven dollars, Computerwoche reported the day after it ran: about 53 by 66 centimetres, which is the PDF's two 13-by-21-inch pages side by side. Paul Thurrott, whose name is in the list, wrote in 2017 of buying a copy, framing it and keeping it on an office wall for many years.

Mozilla planned no sequel. Its spokesperson said on the day that there were no plans for more ads, and in 2006 Ross gave Byfield the verdict Fried had asked for: "Contrary to popular belief, we found the ad itself to be less effective than the buzz surrounding it before and after it ran."

The drive was copied without the names. Byfield's article describes a pledge-paid ad for OpenOffice.org, organised by a volunteer, that ran in the New York Metro on 31 July 2006 and listed no contributors. Henri Poole of CivicActions, in the same article, held that recognition was what donors to such an ad were after: "You're buying yourself karma." Byfield's comparison was with free software itself, whose contributors expect to be paid in credit. At $30 a line, the Times ad had extended the credits file to people who could not write code.

The takeaway

The names did three jobs: they raised the money, they delayed the ad, and they proved its headline. As advertising, Ross judged the two pages less effective than the talk around them, though I would add that the talk was about two pages somebody had to buy.

What the donors bought was a line in a list. "You're not alone" is a claim, and the ad printed its evidence above and below the claim in 4.5-point type, which I think is why a page that had to be squinted at ended up framed on an office wall.

What I couldn't verify

  • The count of 8,567 names is my own, checked twice, and it is a count of the PDF Mozilla posted with its press release, a file made the day before the ad ran. I have not seen the newsprint, and nothing I read says why the page holds fewer names than there were donors.
  • Rob Davis's weekend with the hijacked PC comes from Wired, and the seventeen hours is Davis's own figure, given to ClickZ. I found no second source for either. ClickZ's short version of the weekend says a security hole wiped the hard drive where Wired says Davis reformatted it.
  • The cause of the delay rests on BetaNews, which names no source, and on one sentence in ZDNet UK. The staff-meeting agenda is known only from Gervase Markham's 2013 comment, which gives it no date. Any account by Christopher Messina was on the Spread Firefox site, which is gone, and I could not open an archived copy.
  • That Blake Ross started Spread Firefox is Wired's statement; Mozilla's own announcement of the site names nobody. Ross's verdict on the ad comes from one article, Bruce Byfield's in 2006.
  • Byfield's article says the drive was announced on 29 October 2004. The press release and the reports from that week say Tuesday 19 October, and I went with those.
  • I never found what the two pages cost. Markham believed Mozilla got a non-profit rate and said there was no evidence for it, so the essay gives no figure.
  • Three readings are mine: that the "Mitchell" in Markham's comment is Mitchell Baker, that the names printed ten times belong to Community Champions, and that the last entry in the list was added late. I also cannot show that the Rob Davis, Blake Ross, Jason Fried and John Battelle in the list are the people in the story.
  • That Davis's hijacked-PC weekend fell in the summer of 2004 is my inference; the Wired piece gives no date for it.
  • My reading of the "$30 a line" credits-file gloss on Byfield's account is my own inference, not something he states.
