The Rhino book: when the bookstore was the curriculum

For a good many years, the standard way to learn JavaScript was to go to a shop and buy a book with a rhinoceros on the cover, and the book kept a note at the back about the rhinoceros.

The animal on the cover of JavaScript: The Definitive Guide, Fourth Edition is a Javan rhinoceros.

That is the first sentence of the colophon, the page at the very end of an O'Reilly book where the publisher tells you about the animal and the typefaces. It goes on to say that "Fewer than 100 of these animals are still living", that "Edie Freedman designed the cover of this book", and that "The cover image is a 19th-century engraving from the Dover Pictorial Archive." The copyright page at the other end of the same book makes a stranger claim: "the association between the image of a Javan rhinoceros and the topic of JavaScript is a trademark of O'Reilly & Associates, Inc."

Its title is JavaScript: The Definitive Guide, and developers have long called it the Rhino book. It sat on a shelf beside the Camel book and the koala one, and its author, David Flanagan, revised it six times between 1996 and 2020. I knew the nickname and the reputation. What I had not understood until I did the reading was how much of the web's early training ran through a retail product, and how many odd things that fact explains: why the books were so fat, why one of them was sold as a beta, and how the book's author came to work on the free website that took over its reference pages.

Staples and brown covers

O'Reilly did not start as a publisher. A profile the company reprints, "Tim O'Reilly in a Nutshell", says it was founded in 1978 and published its first computer book in 1985, and it has Tim O'Reilly explaining how a technical-writing consultancy drifted into books: in the slow periods, "we started writing manuals for programs we were using that didn't have very good documentation." That clause is the whole business model. The software existed, the vendor's documentation was thin, and somebody could charge for the gap.

The animals came from an outsider. In "A short history of the O'Reilly animals", Edie Freedman recalls that in the mid-1980s the company "was selling short books on Unix topics via mail order", and that these Nutshell Handbooks "were held together by staples, and had plain brown covers." Tim O'Reilly wanted them in real bookstores and did not like the covers a designer had produced. A neighbour who worked there showed them to Freedman, who at the time made slides and video in the world of Digital Equipment Corporation. "I'd never met a Unix programmer or tried to edit a document using vi", Freedman writes.

The usual story is that the Unix command names reminded Freedman of strange animals. Freedman's own account is better. The terms "sounded to me like words that might come out of Dungeons and Dragons". The engravings came second: looking for images, "I came across some odd-looking animal engravings from the 19th century", and they seemed to match. The covers took a whole weekend. Back at the office, "Some of the people at O'Reilly were taken aback: they thought the animals were weird, ugly, and a bit scary." Tim O'Reilly, by Freedman's account, got it immediately.

So before the web existed, the pieces were in place: a publisher whose trade was filling documentation gaps, a cover design you could spot from across a shop, and shops worth crossing. The best of those was Computer Literacy Bookshops in Silicon Valley. The Computer History Museum's record of an oral history with its founders, Dan Doernberg and Rachel Unkefer, says the first store opened in March 1983. In a footnote to the transcript, Doernberg quotes what they told a magazine in 1987: "14,000 titles in our database and 60,000 books in stock." A tenth-anniversary notice in TidBITS quoted Gene Miya on what that did to people: "every one of us who first walked into the place spent $300 in less than a hour of browsing".

A book printed in beta

Flanagan had already written Java in a Nutshell for O'Reilly when he picked his next subject. In a 2020 interview with JavaScript Weekly he remembers telling an engineer from Sun Microsystems, Java's maker, that "I thought JavaScript might become more important in the browser than Java". The engineer "scoffed".

The trouble was that the subject would not hold still. The preface to the second edition tells it plainly:

When Netscape released a final version of Navigator 2.0, I imagined that JavaScript would finally be stable, and that the time was ripe for a book documenting it. Soon after I started writing, a beta release of Netscape 3.0 was announced. It seems like I've been playing catch-up ever since.

O'Reilly's answer was one I did not expect from a print publisher in 1996. In Flanagan's words from the same preface, "we printed a 'beta edition' of this book". He confirmed the label on his own site in 2020: the first edition was published "as the 'beta edition'", in August 1996. Kevin Savetz's Internet book list of that winter records it at 454 pages and $29.95, with a note that it covered the JavaScript of Navigator 2.0 and of the Navigator 3.0 betas, and that "the 3.0 information is current as of the 3.0b4 release." A paper book, in other words, pinned to a beta build number.

The second edition followed in January 1997. Its preface calls it "far superior to the last", "over one hundred pages longer", and is dated November 1996, with visible relief that it "goes to print before the first beta version of Navigator 4.0". Here is the first program in it, Example 1-1:

<HTML>
<BODY>
<SCRIPT LANGUAGE="JavaScript">
document.write("<h2>Table of Factorials</h2>");
for(i = 1, fact = 1; i < 10; i++, fact *= i) {
    document.write(i + "! = " + fact);
    document.write("<br>");
}
</SCRIPT>
</BODY>
</HTML>

It still runs. It is also a tidy record of its year: uppercase tags, a LANGUAGE attribute, undeclared variables and document.write(), which Flanagan later described as "pretty much the main feature of JavaScript at the very beginning, before the DOM." If you wanted the examples without typing them in, the preface sent you to anonymous FTP at ftp.ora.com.

What surprised me most is in the acknowledgments. Brendan Eich "read and provided helpful comments on the beta edition", which you might expect. But Flanagan also thanks three of "the primary developers of Microsoft's version of JavaScript", who "reviewed the Beta edition" too. In 1996 people at the two companies fighting over the browser were both sending comments on the same independent book. My reading is that the book was already doing a job neither vendor's documentation did, which was to describe both.

The shelf fills up

Then the book grew, edition by edition, with the platform. The third came in 1998. The fourth, with a preface dated September 2001, announced a change of method: the focus had moved "from documenting particular JavaScript and browser implementations" to "documenting the standards upon which those implementations are (or ought to be) based", because it was "no longer practical for any one book to attempt to document" every quirk of every browser. The fifth, in 2006, added Ajax and ran to 994 pages by the Internet Archive's catalogue record. The sixth, in 2011, Flanagan himself later called "a 1096 page brick".

It had neighbours. The colophons of the editions on sale in the early 2000s give a koala to HTML & XHTML: The Definitive Guide, a crab spider to Webmaster in a Nutshell, a flamingo to Danny Goodman's Dynamic HTML: The Definitive Reference and salmon to Eric Meyer's Cascading Style Sheets: The Definitive Guide. Freedman reports that the spider drew a complaint from the husband of a reader it terrified. A webmaster with those four and the rhino on the desk owned, more or less, the documentation of their job.

And O'Reilly's was only one colour of spine. Goodman's own list of publishing credits starts its JavaScript line with Danny Goodman's JavaScript Handbook from IDG in 1996, which became the JavaScript Bible and ran to a seventh edition in 2010. Laura Lemay's Teach Yourself Web Publishing with HTML in a Week came out in January 1995, according to a 1997 Salon profile that credits her with 23 titles two years later. Philip Greenspun, writing in 1997 about a visit to a large Barnes & Noble in Manhattan, found a table of Internet books at the front of the computer section, "a massive 'for Dummies' section behind the table", and behind that "an even larger 'how to program HTML' section."

Why the books got so thick

The first force was the software's own pace. Andrew Leonard's Salon piece says that for a computer book "three months from start to finish is not uncommon", and describes a new genre that a literary agent, David Rogelberg, called the "beta book". The technology books editor of Publishers Weekly, Paul Hilts, told Leonard: "These books are out of date before they go to press". And then the part I find most interesting: "But the readers know that. They buy the book as if they are buying a subscription." A new edition every year or two was not a failure of the format. It was the format.

The second force was the shelf itself. Greenspun, who had just written one of these books for Macmillan, reported the trade's belief without much affection: "It is an article of faith in the computer publishing that bigger books sell better. They take up more space on the shelf and readers with a tech problem find a bigger book comforting." This is one author's account, not publisher data, but it fits what you could see. A book was chosen in a few minutes, standing up, from a wall of rivals. Thickness and a bright spine were the only interface it had.

The third was money. Wired reported in 1997 an industry newsletter's figure that sales of trade and professional computer books came to $586 million in 1996, up from $500 million the year before. Salon counted "more than 40 computer book publishers" and quoted Rogelberg on the consequence: "It's cheaper to publish the book and have it fail than to do some market research to see whether it will fail."

The fourth is the one that made a brand matter: most of the results were bad. Douglas Crockford's essay "JavaScript: The World's Most Misunderstood Programming Language" has a section headed "Bad Books", and it does not hedge. "Nearly all of the books about JavaScript are quite awful. They contain errors, poor examples, and promote bad practices." Then: "I have reviewed dozens of JavaScript books, and I can only recommend one", which is Flanagan's. To be fair to the others, Crockford was hard on the winner as well. In a later memoir Crockford calls the Definitive Guide "the only reliable source" and adds that "it was too big", and that "it completely left out closure, which is JavaScript's best part." Still, in a shop with no reviews pinned to the shelf, an animal on a white cover was a working quality signal. It was the nearest thing the aisle had to a ranking algorithm.

How readers coped

These books were expensive, so people read them where they stood. Al Sweigart, who went on to write programming books, describes going to Barnes & Noble after school, "pulling thick $80 tech books off the shelf, reading them in the store for a few hours, then putting them back on the shelf. I did this for years." Sweigart says the employees came to recognise their regular visitor and never made a fuss. I'd guess every large bookshop of the period had a few of these unofficial students, and that some of them now review your pull requests.

You also learned to distrust the page a little. The books had errata lists, and serious readers treated them as part of the text. The FAQ of the Usenet group comp.lang.javascript, in a posting from June 2008 that survives on a forum mirror, says most regulars believe the best book to be the Definitive Guide's fifth edition, and immediately adds: "The errata should be considered a must read along with the book." The best recommendation a newsgroup of experts could give was a book plus its list of known mistakes, and one reply in the same thread disputed even that.

The publisher's own fix for the weight was to put the books on a CD-ROM, a line O'Reilly came to call the CD Bookshelf. An O'Reilly press release from October 1997, reposted at the time to a Boston user group's mailing list, announces WebMaster in a Nutshell Deluxe: one CD-ROM holding five books in HTML, the second edition of the Rhino book among them, sold with a printed copy of WebMaster in a Nutshell for $69.95. HTML on a disc is easy to put on a server, and people did. Tim O'Reilly's 2002 essay "Piracy is Progressive Taxation" says the biggest problem with unpaid copies was not file sharing but "copies of our CD Bookshelf product line being put up on public Web servers, or copied wholesale and offered for sale on eBay." Then it declines to panic: "While these pirated copies are annoying, they hardly destroy our business." The servers that ignored a polite letter, the essay notes, were "typically in countries where the books are not available for sale or are far too expensive for local consumers to buy." The first lesson in that essay is the line people still quote: "Obscurity is a far greater threat to authors and creative artists than piracy."

The legitimate version of the same idea was Safari. A press release of October 1, 2001 announced a joint venture with Pearson and mentioned that the service for individuals had "been available at safari.oreilly.com since February 1, 2001", for $9.99 a month. Hilts had said readers bought computer books like a subscription. Four years later you could buy the subscription and skip the books.

What the shelf left behind

The market that built the shelf peaked early. Publishers Weekly's "After the Bust", in September 2002, reports a McGraw-Hill publisher's estimate that the category reached its apex in 2000 and that the number of titles had since fallen by 25 to 30 percent. O'Reilly's Mike Hendrickson, in a 2009 market review that survives as a long quotation on a librarian's blog, wrote that the market "cratered in 2001" and "stabilized in 2004 at about half the size that it was in 2000." The shops went with it. Barnes & Noble's online arm bought Computer Literacy's successor company, Fatbrain, in September 2000, and Doernberg's footnote in the oral history says the original Sunnyvale store stayed open "until Barnes & Noble closed all the stores in I believe 2001."

By 2008 the replacement could say so out loud. Announcing Stack Overflow that April, Jeff Atwood wrote of programming knowledge "hidden away in books that nobody buys any more." Twelve days later, in a post that walks some of that back, Atwood put the case at full strength first: "The internet has rendered programming books obsolete." Atwood's complaint about the survivors would have been familiar to Greenspun: "Programming books sold by weight, not by volume."

Three things from the shelf are still in use, and none of them is where I expected to find it.

The first is the reference section of the Rhino book, which moved to the web, and its author went the same way for a while. Mozilla's developer site began, by Mozilla's own account, in 2005 as a rescue of Netscape's DevEdge documentation, which had vanished from the web under AOL. That is the vendor documentation the books had been competing with since 1996. In 2011, with the sixth edition just out, Flanagan wrote a post about pirated copies of it, and about Google suggesting them to anyone who researched the book. The post is gone from his site, but Mark Bernstein quoted it that April: "I've worked really hard on this book, and I've got to say that this just feels like a kick in the gut." I have no source that ties what happened next to that post, so take the order of events as only that. By the JavaScript Weekly interview, Flanagan had been self-employed "from about 1991 to 2011", mostly writing books; in 2011 he joined Mozilla, and the biography printed with the interview says he has since "worked as a full-stack engineer on MDN". When the seventh edition came out in 2020 it was about 400 pages shorter, and he gave the reason himself: "I've removed the reference section. In 2020 it is faster to look reference information up on MDN than it is to flip through a printed book."

The second is the animal, which escaped into software. Mozilla's history of its Java-based JavaScript engine says: "Rhino gets its name from the animal on the cover of the O'Reilly book about JavaScript." The project started at Netscape in the autumn of 1997, when the book was a year old. A publisher claimed a trademark on the link between an animal and a language, and the language's own makers named an engine after the cover. I can't think of another piece of technical documentation that named part of the thing it documented.

The third is the pirated bookshelf, which has quietly turned into an archive. O'Reilly's site would not show me the old prefaces, and the Internet Archive's scans of the early editions are available only for lending. The 1997 preface and acknowledgments I quote above I read in an unauthorised copy of one of those CD Bookshelves, still being served from a web server two decades on, complete with O'Reilly's copyright notices. I have not linked it. But it is an awkward fact that the copy the publisher wanted taken down is the one a reader can still open, and it is Tim O'Reilly's own lesson about obscurity, applied to his back catalogue.

There is a fourth, smaller one. The "beta edition" of 1996 looked like a stunt. It is now simply how technical books ship: Flanagan's announcement of the seventh edition says "The early release version is available online from the publisher today", with print to follow weeks later.

The takeaway

The bookstore was the curriculum because, for a while, it was the only place where the web's documentation had been edited. Browsers shipped faster than their makers could explain them, the explanations that existed were scattered or wrong, and a publisher with a recognisable cover could sell you a checked, bound, slightly out-of-date account of the whole thing. The thickness, the yearly editions and even the beta label all followed from selling a moving subject through a physical shelf.

What ended it was not that developers stopped reading. The parts of a book that were really a database (the reference pages, the errata, the worked answers) moved to places that could be searched and corrected: MDN, Stack Overflow, a subscription library. What stayed on paper is the part that was always an argument about how to write the language, which is why the seventh edition of the Rhino book spends most of its pages on JavaScript itself and no longer tries to be the manual for the browser. The same rhinoceros is on the cover. Flanagan thinks it looks grumpier these days.

What I couldn't verify

  • O'Reilly's own pages for the old editions would not load for me, so I read the 1997 and 2002 editions' prefaces, acknowledgments, colophon and copyright page in an unauthorised web copy of O'Reilly's CD Bookshelf, which I have not linked. I could not compare that text with a printed copy, and I never saw the 1996 beta edition itself.
  • Sources disagree about which Navigator 3.0 beta the beta edition covered. The 1996 book list says the 3.0b4 release, while the second edition's preface says the final beta and its acknowledgments mention Beta 6 features.
  • Flanagan's 2011 post about pirated copies is gone from his site, and I know its words only from Mark Bernstein's quotation, which does not give the post's own date. Nothing I read connects that post to Flanagan joining Mozilla the same year.
  • Mike Hendrickson's statement that the computer book market halved between 2000 and 2004 comes from an O'Reilly post I could not open, quoted on a librarian's blog. The same quotation admits O'Reilly had reliable sales data only from 2004, so the size of the fall is the company's estimate.
  • That the books were thick because thick books sold is Philip Greenspun's account of what publishers believed, not publisher data. A founder's recollection, hedged with "I believe", is my only source for Computer Literacy's stores closing in 2001.
  • The animals I give for the HTML, Webmaster, Dynamic HTML and CSS books come from the colophons of editions published from 2000 to 2002, and I did not check the 1990s covers. That the 1997 CD-ROM belongs to the line O'Reilly later called the CD Bookshelf, that reviewers at both Netscape and Microsoft show the book filled a gap in vendor documentation, and that the unauthorised copies now work as an archive are my own readings.
