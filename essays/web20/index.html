Web 2.0: the buzzword with a serial number

In May 2006 a lawyer's letter told a non-profit group in Cork to take "Web 2.0" out of the name of its €50 half-day conference, and listed among the group's offences a page that asked what Web 2.0 was and linked to Tim O'Reilly for the answer.

CMP hereby demands that you immediately cease and desist from utilizing Web 2.0 as the title of your event

IT@Cork had billed its event for 8 June as the "Web 2.0 half-day conference", with Microsoft as sponsor, according to the Irish Times. The demand came in a letter dated 24 May from a lawyer acting for CMP Media, the company that put on the Web 2.0 Conference in California with O'Reilly Media. CMP, it said, had applied to register "Web 2.0" as a service mark for live events. IT@Cork's use of the name was a "flagrant violation", made worse by promotional material that put the question and sent readers to an article by O'Reilly. That left IT@Cork fifteen days.

O'Reilly's essay, defined by example

The Register does not name the article IT@Cork linked to, but the question on the Cork page is its title: "What Is Web 2.0", which O'Reilly put on the web in September 2005. It opens with the phrase's birth certificate. "Web 2.0", it says, began with a conference brainstorming session between O'Reilly and a company called MediaLive International, at which O'Reilly's Dale Dougherty observed that the web had not crashed with the dot-com shares and that the survivors seemed to have things in common. Perhaps the collapse was a turning point, "such that a call to action such as 'Web 2.0' might make sense". The first Web 2.0 Conference followed in October 2004.

A year on, the essay set out to say what the call to action meant, and began the way the brainstorm had, with a list:

Web 1.0               Web 2.0
Ofoto             --> Flickr
Britannica Online --> Wikipedia
personal websites --> blogging
screen scraping   --> web services
publishing        --> participation
stickiness        --> syndication

Those are six of its fourteen rows, with the spacing closed up. Nothing in the essay reads like a brand guideline. Web 2.0 has no hard boundary, it says, only a gravitational core, and anything that shows some of the principles can orbit at its own distance. The essay was pleased with how far the phrase had already travelled, counting more than 9.5 million citations in Google, and its one complaint was about the people adopting it: the "meme" had spread so widely "that companies are now pasting it on as a marketing buzzword, with no real understanding of just what it means."

The phrase also had a file at the United States Patent and Trademark Office, opened before the essay and before the conference.

The service mark filed in 2003

MediaLive filed for the service mark on 3 November 2003. It was an intent-to-use application, the kind you file for a name you plan to trade under and have not yet used; Marty Schwimmer, a trademark lawyer who blogged about the dispute, wrote of "when the ITU was filed for WEB 2.0 in 2003". Wikipedia says November 2004, and is alone in that. Tim O'Reilly's own reply to the row dates the filing to the start of the partnership with MediaLive on the conference.

So the paperwork came eleven months before anybody sat down at the first conference and nearly two years before the essay. "Web 2.0" was a product name with a pending registration first, and a description of the web second.

The application reached CMP Media by acquisition. In a press release of 11 January 2006, CMP announced it had bought MediaLive's Technology Media Group for $65 million, and the list of events that came with it includes "Web 2.0 (in partnership with O'Reilly Media.)". On 16 May, by Slashdot's account, the trademark office notified CMP that the mark was entitled to be registered. The lawyer wrote eight days later.

The letter reaches a blog

Tom Raftery sat on IT@Cork's committee and kept a blog, and he published the letter there. To the Register he argued that "Web 2.0" was a generic term and that Cork was outside American jurisdiction, and then he did some arithmetic. O'Reilly's conference charged a VIP fee of $2,795, the Cork event cost €50, and "I think it would be difficult to mix up these conferences: one is in California and the other is in Cork."

Raftery also mentioned that he had invited Tim O'Reilly to speak at the Cork conference back in February. O'Reilly had been unavailable until October.

By 26 May the story was on Slashdot under the headline "O'Reilly and CMP Exercise Trademark on 'Web 2.0'", with commenters offering Web 2.1 and Web 2.1 Service Pack 1. The letter was CMP's, but the name people knew was O'Reilly's, and Tim O'Reilly was on vacation and out of reach.

The company answered for him that same day. A statement from Sara Winge, O'Reilly's vice president of corporate communications, went up on its Radar blog. It wished they had talked to IT@Cork before sending legal correspondence, admitted that the conference team had not known about the speaking invitation, and apologised for how the matter had been handled. A follow-up letter, it said, had agreed that IT@Cork "can use the Web 2.0 name this year".

Wikipedia records the demand as retracted, which is tidier than what happened. Winge's statement stood by the principle of protecting the mark. The Irish Times reported that the permission covered that year's conference only, and that O'Reilly would go on defending the name against other organisers. Raftery, by the Register's account, was not pleased to be granted permission for something he thought needed none.

When Tim O'Reilly replied, it was the audience he went after. Chris Shiflett, who took O'Reilly's side, quotes the reply: "The flap about the Web 2.0 Conference trademark has shaken my faith in the collective intelligence of the blogosphere." "Harnessing collective intelligence" is a section heading in "What Is Web 2.0". Raftery, Shiflett reports, later apologised for not emailing O'Reilly first.

Why the Web 2.0 mark was so thin

The owners had a real case. They had come up with the name for their own conference, filed for it before the phrase was in general use, and claimed it only for the titles of events; nobody was being told to stop saying Web 2.0. Nick Carr, in the comments under his own post on the row, needed eight words: "It was their idea, and it's their property."

The trouble was what the owners had done with the phrase after filing for it. They had spent two years persuading the industry that "Web 2.0" named a kind of web, and the essay's 9.5 million citations measured how well that had gone. Schwimmer's diagnosis was that the owner had worn out its own mark: "this may indeed be that rare trademark creature, genericide, but self-incurred genericide." Promote a brand as a buzzword, he wrote, and the protection left is razor-thin, with a backlash waiting when you assert it. The phrase was not even new: Wikipedia credits it to a 1999 magazine article by Darcy DiNucci.

Raftery told a reporter from IDG: "If this had happened three or four years ago, we would have just rolled over". IT@Cork was a non-profit with no money for a legal fight. What it had in 2006 was a committee member with a blog, readers with blogs of their own, and Slashdot. A letter dated 24 May and meant for one desk in Cork was in front of the whole trade by 26 May, and O'Reilly apologised that day. Blogging is in the right-hand column of O'Reilly's list.

Where the service mark went

The mark was registered anyway, on 27 June 2006, as number 3110027, for live events including trade shows, expositions and conferences in computing and information technology. That was nineteen days after the date of the Cork conference.

O'Reilly asked for the mark to be enforced less. Chris Messina, writing that September under the title "Web 2.0 Trademark Closure", reported that Tim O'Reilly "has asked CMP to take a more narrow view": to act only against events whose names could be confused with those of the two companies' own.

Europe declined. CMP had filed a European application in March 2006, and it was open to objection while the row went on. Wikipedia says it was refused on 23 May 2007, almost exactly a year after the letter told an Irish organisation it was infringing.

And the American registration has lapsed. The record shows it cancelled under Section 8 on 3 February 2017, for failure to file the ten-year renewal. I can't see the file, so I can't say whether somebody decided to let "Web 2.0" go or whether nobody was left who thought to keep it.

The takeaway

"Web 2.0" was filed as a conference brand in 2003 and then promoted, by the people who filed it, as a name for the whole direction of the web. Both jobs were done well, and the second undid the first. By May 2006 the phrase belonged to its users in every sense but the legal one, and the first attempt to use the legal one lasted two days against a blog post from Cork.

The letter's oddest line is the one about the link. IT@Cork's promotional page did what the essay asks of its readers: it took up the idea, cited its author and passed it on. CMP's lawyer read that as aggravation. The mark behind the demand to stop "utilizing Web 2.0 as the title of your event" stayed on the register for ten more years, and since February 2017 there has been nothing behind it.

What I couldn't verify

  • The trademark record (serial 78322306, filed 3 November 2003, registered 27 June 2006 as number 3110027, cancelled under Section 8 on 3 February 2017 for want of the ten-year renewal) I know only from search-engine summaries of Justia's listing. Justia and the trademark office's own database would not open, so the cancellation in particular rests on one database I never saw directly.
  • Wikipedia says the application was filed in November 2004. I went with 2003 because the record, Martin Schwimmer, IDG's report and Silicon Republic all say 2003. Tim O'Reilly's reply, which names MediaLive as the filer, I know only from search snippets.
  • Slashdot says the trademark office notified CMP on 16 May 2006; Wikipedia says the application passed final review on 10 May. These may be two events. I used Slashdot's date and could not check either against the file, and I cannot explain how an intent-to-use application reached registration six weeks later.
  • I could not open the O'Reilly Radar posts (the statement of 26 May 2006, Tim O'Reilly's reply, and the later post Chris Messina summarises) or Tom Raftery's own blog. Their words are quoted as the Register, Nick Carr and Chris Shiflett quoted them, and no source I could open gives the day Raftery posted the letter, so the essay does not date it. One Japanese library notice dates the letter itself to 25 May; I followed the Register's 24 May.
  • No source names the article that IT@Cork's page linked to. That it was "What Is Web 2.0" is my inference from the Register's description of a page asking "what is Web 2.0?" and linking to an article by Tim O'Reilly.
  • That the European application was refused on 23 May 2007 is Wikipedia's statement alone; I found no register entry for it. Darcy DiNucci's 1999 article is also taken from Wikipedia, whose own pages disagree on the month, and I did not open the article.
  • I read "What Is Web 2.0" in a 2007 journal reprint, because O'Reilly's site would not serve the original. The reprint adds at least one later figure, and I have assumed the passages I quote stood as they are in September 2005.
  • Most of the reports and blog posts reached me as a tool's summary with short quotations, not as full pages, so a quotation may have lost its context. The description of what the registration covers follows the Register and one search summary; another summary words it differently.
  • The €50 was the price for non-members; one notice says staff of member companies got in free. I found no account of the Cork conference itself, so the essay does not say whether it ran on 8 June or under what name, and I found no report of any other event being sent a letter.
  • Why the registration was allowed to lapse is not something any source told me.
