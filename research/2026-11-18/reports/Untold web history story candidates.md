# The web's best untold fights, ranked

The strongest under-reported story in the research is Pei-Yuan Wei against Michael Doyle: a Berkeley student gave away a browser that ran embedded programs in 1993, told the person patenting that idea so on a public mailing list in 1995, and then waited 17 years, through a $520,562,280 verdict the first jury reached without being allowed to hear the evidence, for a second jury to agree. I would commission it first because it is the only candidate that is at once complete (named protagonist, two dated turns, a clean ending), built on primary documents that were opened and are still online, tied to something the readers typed (Internet Explorer's "click to activate" and the script-injected embeds that dodged it), and told so far only as a patent-troll news story in which Wei is a paraphrased witness. Two smaller stories are nearly as ready and cheaper to finish: Roy Fielding's ten-line Apache commit that deleted Internet Explorer 10's Do Not Track header, and the two Firefox bugs in which a 2008 library, MooTools, cost JavaScript the method name `contains`. Behind them sit five stories that pass the collection's story test but each have one named hole (Apple and KHTML, the real origin of robots.txt, Jon Postel's "test", Hotline, CDDB), and eight more that rank lower on novelty, protagonist or sourcing. The research vetted 31 distinct candidates; 16 are ranked here and the rest are sorted into fold, hold or park. One caution applies to everything below: the researchers read pages through a fetch tool that summarises, the Internet Archive was unreachable throughout, and so every quotation in this report must be re-read on its page before it goes into an essay.

## How 31 candidates became a ranked sixteen

The ranking weighs four things in this order: whether the story is really untold or its dots unconnected; whether it has a named person who wanted something, a dated turn and an ending; how much of it rests on pages a researcher opened; and how close it sits to what a JavaScript or web developer aged 40 to 60 actually touched. A story that is dramatic but already has a definitive telling (DigiNotar, the Texas Eolas trial as courtroom news) loses to a smaller one that nobody has assembled. A story whose one-sided or single-source record would force the essay to guess (ArsDigita, Spyglass) is held back however good its bones.

The sourcing labels are used consistently. "Opened" means a researcher fetched the page and reported words from it. "Search summary" means the fact came from a search result or a summary of a page that was not opened, and is a pointer only. "Wikipedia only" means no primary was checked. "Inference" means the connection is the researcher's or mine and no source states it. Where the notes mark something unverified, this report says so and does not assert it.

Three overlaps with the collection need the editor's eye before commissioning. One researcher's brief treated ViolaWWW, robots.txt, Prototype.js, CommonJS, npm's origins, Node at JSConf EU and the IE8 version-targeting row as excluded topics; I could not see that list, and the first and fifth picks below touch the first two. The picks below also brush against queued topics in `TOPICS.md`: Opera's "Bork" edition (the EU ballot and vendor-prefix stories share Opera), the promises-to-`async` idea (Node's deleted promises), and the Prototype.js idea (MooTools).

## Three stories are ready to commission now

### 1. The student who told the patent holder first

Pei-Yuan Wei, a student at UC Berkeley, wrote the Viola language and then ViolaWWW, which Tim Berners-Lee's own FAQ calls, for a year or so, "the best way to browse the web" and the recommended browser at CERN, with "downloaded applets and animations way back then" ([Berners-Lee FAQ](https://www.w3.org/People/Berners-Lee/FAQ.html)). Wei wanted a HyperCard-like programmable web and gave the code away. Michael Doyle's team at UCSF built on Mosaic, filed a patent on embedded interactive programs on 17 October 1994, and the University of California licensed it exclusively to Doyle's company, Eolas. The opening scene is public and dated: on **21 August 1995**, the day Eolas announced its licence, Wei wrote to www-talk, "I sincerely hope this patent isn't going to stick, for the good of the web as a whole", and said the technology had been released in full source in 1993 ([www-talk, Wei](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-talk/1995JulAug/0429.html)). Doyle answered within the hour that Wei had already admitted otherwise ([www-talk, Doyle](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-talk/1995JulAug/0431.html)), and Wei replied with the line the whole story turns on: "Wish I had kept better records and wrote papers about things as they happened!" ([www-talk, Wei](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-talk/1995JulAug/0446.html)).

The conflict then runs through two trials that hinge on the same week in May 1993. The Federal Circuit found that Wei demonstrated the relevant build to two Sun engineers on 7 May 1993 and that the surviving file was dated 12 May; it also found that Doyle had "downloaded and read" Wei's paper in 1994, kept a folder labelled "Viola stuff" in 1998 and told the patent office nothing. The Chicago trial judge nonetheless ruled that Wei had "abandoned, suppressed or concealed" the demo by improving it, kept it from the jury, and the jury awarded **$520,562,280** against Microsoft. The first turn is **2 March 2005**, when the appeals court held that "creating an improved version of an invention does not in any sense abandon the original invention" and sent the case back ([Eolas v. Microsoft, 399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html)). Microsoft settled anyway, and developers paid the bill in the meantime: from April 2006 Internet Explorer made users click to activate any control loaded by `<object>`, `<embed>` or `<applet>`, while controls injected by JavaScript were exempt, until Microsoft licensed the patent and removed the click in 2008 ([IE blog](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/ie/ie-automatic-component-activation-changes-to-ie-activex-update)). The second turn is Tyler, Texas, in February 2012, where Berners-Lee testified in court for the first time, Wei, Scott Silvey, Eric Bina and Dave Raggett also testified, and Doyle said under oath, "We were excited to use Mosaic and Viola as building blocks" ([Wired, 8 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/tim-berners-lee-patent/); [Wired, 9 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/texas-jury/)). On 9 February 2012 the jury found every asserted claim invalid after a few hours ([Wired, verdict](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/interactive-web-patent/)), and an appeals panel affirmed without comment in July 2013 ([Mullin, 2013, reposted](https://attrition.org/pipermail/infowarrior/2013-July/010311.html)).

The most surprising documented fact is the combination: Wei warned Doyle in public three years before the patent issued, Doyle had read Wei's paper before filing, and the first jury was still barred from hearing any of it. The ending has a quieter second half. Berners-Lee wrote that Wei "has passed though history unnoticed", and a September 2023 message to the W3C's web-history list reports that a Computer History Museum post announcing Wei's death had gone unremarked for months, including on that list ([public-webhistory](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-webhistory/2023Sep/0001.html)).

The existing tellings are Joe Mullin's courtroom reports of 2012 and 2013, which frame it as the troll and the trial, plus passages in books the researchers did not open. No one appears to have told it from the 1995 exchange forward, and the W3C's part gives the essay its "why" section for free. In October 2003 Berners-Lee asked the patent office's director to re-examine the patent, warning it "will cause cascades of incompatibility to ripple through the Web" ([W3C letter](https://www.w3.org/2003/10/27-rogan.html)), and the body that made that request had only just become royalty-free by public revolt: a draft allowing royalty-bearing standards drew, by Adam Warner's count, two relevant comments in six weeks ([LWN](https://lwn.net/2001/1004/a/w3c-patents.php3)) and then, by the researcher's own count of the archive index, 617 messages on its final day, 30 September 2001 ([www-patentpolicy-comment](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Sep/)). That the 2001 fight is what gave W3C the standing and the mechanism to act in 2003 is the researcher's inference; the shared people (Daniel Weitzner, Alan Kotok, Microsoft's Michele Herman, Barry Rein) are documented.

Sourcing is the best of any candidate. Opened: the three www-talk messages, the Federal Circuit opinion, the W3C FAQ, letter and meeting report, the IE blog post and three Wired trial reports. Not opened, and the most important gap: Wei's own page "Personal Comments on Eolas vs Microsoft", Viola's site, and the trial transcripts, so there is no direct quote from Wei on the stand and nothing on how Wei regarded the outcome. Wikipedia only: Wei's biography and the year of death, the 2005 re-examination result, the 27 August 2007 settlement date, and everything about Eolas after 2013. Sources disagree on the first verdict (use the court's $520,562,280, not "$521 million" or Mullin's "$540 million"), on the demo date by a day (the court says 7 May, Wei's 1995 post says "by May 8"), on when Viola began (Wei says "around 88", Wikipedia 1989 or 1990), on how many defendants held out in 2012, and on whether the appeal was decided on 22 or 23 July 2013. The link between click-to-activate and the era's script-based Flash embedding libraries is an inference and needs a source before it is printed. No source states Wei's pronouns.

### 2. Ten lines of Apache config as a protest sign

Roy Fielding, a co-founder of the Apache web server and an editor of the W3C's Do Not Track work, held that the header only meant something if a person had chosen it. Microsoft announced that Internet Explorer 10 would send it by default, with its chief privacy officer Brendon Lynch arguing that consumers should have more control over tracking. Fielding's answer was a commit to Apache's default configuration with the message "Apache does not tolerate deliberate abuse of open standards" and two directives that fit on a phone screen: `BrowserMatch "MSIE 10.0;" bad_DNT` and `RequestHeader unset DNT env=bad_DNT` ([apache/httpd commit](https://github.com/apache/httpd/commit/a381ff35fa4d50a5f7b9f64300dfd98859dee8d0)). The Register reported it on 10 September 2012, quoting Fielding, "The only reason DNT exists is to express a non-default option", and citing Netcraft for Apache running about 60% of sites ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2012/09/10/fielding_apache_ie10_windows_8/)).

The conflict is a fair one, which is its strength. Microsoft could say Windows 8's setup told users the setting would be on; privacy advocates attacked the patch; and the same Register piece carries a commentator's point that the draft's user-choice wording was added after the Windows 8 preview. The surprising fact is who did it: the person switching a privacy signal off for a browser was not an advertiser but someone drafting the standard, in a config file, alone. Coverage amounts to one week of trade press; neither researcher found a retrospective.

The ending is where the work remains. That the patch was neutralised within weeks rests on a MediaPost report of 9 October 2012 seen only as a search summary, the claim that it shipped in httpd 2.4.3 rests on a forum quoting the changelog, and Microsoft's later change of the default, the closing of the W3C working group and Do Not Track's own death are all unverified in the notes. The commit page showed no date. This is the cheapest story to finish: the httpd-dev list for September and October 2012, the commit's comment thread and the W3C tracking list are the missing primaries, and all should be reachable.

### 3. The library that named a language feature

TC39 and Mozilla wanted the obvious name `contains`, first for strings and then for arrays. MooTools, shipped on sites from around 2008 that nobody would update, had already put its own `contains` on the built-in prototypes. The first break is Bugzilla bug 789036, where Firefox 18's `String.prototype.contains` broke MooTools 1.2 sites and Brendan Eich and Boris Zbarsky discussed backing it out ([Bugzilla 789036](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=789036)). The second is bug 1075059: JSFiddle's Save button died with "TypeError: this.contains is not a function", and the cause was subtler than a name clash. MooTools copied array methods onto its own collection type by enumerating `Array.prototype`; the native method was non-enumerable, so it was never copied. Jason Orendorff saw "no choice but to back this out" ([Bugzilla 1075059](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1075059)). The ending is in the proposal's own README: the name "is not web-compatible", and at the November 2014 TC39 meeting both methods became `includes` ([tc39/Array.prototype.includes](https://github.com/tc39/Array.prototype.includes)). Firefox had shipped the string method from version 18 and carried an alias until version 40 ([Bugzilla 1102219](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1102219)).

Every reader types `includes` and most know the one-line trivia; almost none know there were two rounds, that the same people met the problem twice, or that the mechanism was enumerability. That gap between trivia and mechanism is the essay. Its weakness against the story test is the protagonist, who is a committee and a bug tracker; the fix is to tell it through Zbarsky and Orendorff's back-out decision. The MooTools authors' own account is missing and the essay must not mock them: they shipped a 2008 library under 2008 rules.

All four pages were opened. Bugzilla showed only relative dates, so filing dates need the bugs' history views; the November 2014 meeting notes were not opened, so no champion should be named yet; and the 2018 sequel in which `flatten` met the same library is mentioned in the notes without a source.

## Five more pass the story test, each with one hole to fill

### 4. "I'm eagerly awaiting for you to merge them back"

On 7 January 2003 Apple's Don Melton emailed the KDE developers to thank them for KHTML and promise changes back, and Lars Knoll replied warmly that "convergence will come" ([kfm-devel](https://mail.kde.org/pipermail/kfm-devel/2003-January/002446.html); [khtml-devel](https://mail.kde.org/pipermail/khtml-devel/2003-January/000025.html)). What KDE's volunteers got, in Zack Rusin's words, were "periodical code bombs". The turn is 28 April 2005: after Safari passed Acid2, someone posted a link to Dave Hyatt's patches and Rusin answered with one sarcastic line, "I'm eagerly awaiting for you to merge them back." ([kde-core-devel](https://mail.kde.org/pipermail/kde-core-devel/2005-April/025706.html)). The same day Rusin wrote that Apple did "the very, very minimum required by LGPL" and that developers had offered to sign NDAs just to see Apple's version history ([Rusin](https://blogs.kde.org/2005/04/28/so-when-will-khtml-merge-all-webcore-changes/)). Within six weeks, on 7 June 2005, Apple opened WebKit's CVS and bug database ([KDE News](https://dot.kde.org/2005/06/07/apple-opens-webkit-cvs-and-bug-database/)). The ending is ironic: the trees never reunited, the open WebKit replaced KHTML, and KHTML is reported to have been removed in KDE Frameworks 6, with Konqueror now rendering through a Chromium-based engine (both from search summaries of 2024 KDE messages, not opened).

The outline "WebKit came from KHTML" is common knowledge; the 2003 to 2005 quarrel in its own words exists only as an LWN news item, a CNET piece and Wikipedia paragraphs. The research also corrects a common attribution: "The bitter failure named 'Safari and KHTML'" was written by Cristian Tibirna on 29 April 2005, not by Rusin ([Tibirna](https://blogs.kde.org/2005/04/29/bitter-failure-named-safari-and-khtml/)). Sourcing is strong on one side only. Four blog posts, three list messages, the KDE News item and LWN were opened. Hyatt's April 2005 posts could not be opened, so no Apple-side quotation is verified; CNET's report that Apple proposed KDE adopt its code wholesale is a search summary; the "two years of trouble" rests on Rusin's 2005 retrospective with no dated earlier complaint found; the identity of the blogger "Carewolf" is a probable guess and must not be stated; and where the KDE authors ended up is from the researcher's memory.

### 5. The file that was nearly `/spiders.txt`

Martijn Koster at Nexor wanted crawler authors to stay out of parts of a server without a debate about whether robots were good, and posted a proposal to www-talk on 25 February 1994 ([www-talk](https://ksi.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/archives/WWW-TALK/www-talk-1994q1.messages/712.html)). Koster's own 2019 reconstruction holds what the standard account leaves out. The file was first `/RobotsNotWanted.txt`; the name was settled by a June 1994 vote in which Roy Fielding argued for `/spiders.txt` and wanted a decision "within the next 24 hours" to finish a paper; and Paul Ginsparg, later arXiv's founder, started a Usenet thread about "MISERABLE AUTOMATED DOWNLOADS" on the same day as the proposal. The surprising fact concerns the famous origin anecdote. Charlie Stross wrote in 2009 of being "the numpty who necessitated its invention" after a crawler hammered Koster's server ([Stross](http://www.antipope.org/charlie/blog-static/2009/06/how_i_got_here_in_the_end_part_3.html)), but Koster found emails from Stross dated **7 March 1994, ten days after the proposal**, and points to a September 1993 incident as the likelier spur, while adding "I'm not sure if there were others" ([Koster, 2019](http://www.greenhills.co.uk/posts/robotstxt-25/)). That is an open, friendly disagreement of memory against mail archive, and should be written as one, not as a debunking.

The turn and ending are the present. The 1994 text says it "is not enforced by anybody" ([robotstxt.org](https://www.robotstxt.org/orig.html)); it became an IETF standard only in September 2022, with a line saying the rules "are not a form of access authorization" ([RFC 9309](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309.txt)); and in August 2025 Cloudflare accused Perplexity of disguising crawlers to evade it ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/perplexity-is-using-stealth-undeclared-crawlers-to-evade-website-no-crawl-directives/)), which Perplexity disputed (search summary). The Verge's 2024 feature is the standard telling and covers the AI turn, so the novelty lies entirely in the 1994 archive detail and the chronology, which Wikipedia does not carry. The 1994 material is all opened; the first draft is lost by Koster's own account; the two private email exchanges are known only through Koster's summary; the TollBit, iFixit and Reuters items are search summaries; and the present-day section will date quickly. *eBay v. Bidder's Edge* (2000) belongs here as a middle act but rests on Wikipedia alone.

### 6. The directive that never said "test"

Jon Postel wanted the DNS root to be publishable by IANA and not hostage to Network Solutions' "A" server. On 28 January 1998, two days before the White House's Green Paper on DNS reform ([Froomkin, 2000](https://osaka.law.miami.edu/~froomkin/articles/icann-body.htm)), Postel sent root operators a PGP-signed request to pull the zone from IANA's own machine. The episode is universally called "Postel's test", and the surprising fact is that the directive does not contain the word: it says "The time has come to take a small step in one of those transitions." "Test" appears only in the press statement and in the 3 February message that ended it, about six days later ([internet-history, Craig Simon](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2002-November/000373.html)). The ending is Postel's death in October 1998 (Wikipedia only) and, in John Gilmore's partisan telling, an ICANN "created based on Magaziner's model".

What makes it an essay is that people who knew Postel still disagree. Dave Crocker calls it a contingency drill; Joe Touch recalls a demonstration "that the role was mutable" ([Touch, 2025](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2025-July/010569.html)); John Gilmore says it was groundwork for adding seven new top-level domains ([Gilmore, 2025](https://elists.isoc.org/pipermail/internet-history/2025-July/010553.html)). The episode is covered in a law review article and in books the researchers did not open, and a French engineer wrote in 2010 that it had left few traces in the internet's collective memory ([Bortzmeyer](https://www.bortzmeyer.org/dns-root-takeover.html)).

The essay must be candid about two things that cannot be resolved from the notes. The number of servers that switched is given as five, six, eight, "more than half" and "most" by different sources. Ira Magaziner's threat ("you'll never work on the Internet again") is Gilmore's paraphrase 27 years later, hedged with "basically", while Magaziner's on-the-record remarks at the time were mild (search summary) and the claim that Postel proposed the word "test" as a face-saver rests on one researcher's report of an unpublished tape. Gilmore's account of an unsolved theft of two registry servers weeks later is single-source and should stay out or be labelled. It is further from JavaScript than the picks above.

### 7. The company that got a search order against its own author

Adam Hinkley began the Hotline chat-and-files program in 1996 at 17, and regulars who had met only on Hotline persuaded Hinkley to form a company in Toronto in 1997. The turn is March 1998: Hinkley left with a code library and encrypted what remained. A home in Australia was searched that September, a Canadian judge ruled for the company around January 1999, and Salon reported the chief executive saying Hinkley was still the company's largest shareholder while it was suing ([Salon, 1999](https://www.salon.com/1999/02/25/feature_224/)). The ending is bleak on both sides. A 2001 Slashdot item has Hinkley, at 22, broke and without the code, publishing warnings for young programmers ([Slashdot](https://slashdot.org/story/01/04/09/1639212/adam-hinkleys-ip-hindsights)); the company closed later that year (Wikipedia only).

Salon's 1999 two-parter is the only substantial telling found, and web developers who were not Mac users in 1998 are unlikely to know it. Its appeal is that it is unclear who betrayed whom, and a court record would let the essay report both accounts. That record is the hole: neither Supreme Court of Victoria judgment was opened, the final ruling is known from Wikipedia, and whether the 1998 search was by police or under a civil order is unresolved. The record includes a family tragedy and a then-teenager; both sides are living people and the essay needs restraint.

### 8. The database its users typed in, then got locked out of

Ti Kan and Steve Scherf built CDDB so every CD player would know track names, and strangers typed the data in under what the site then described as a GPL licence (Wikipedia, citing archived pages). They sold in 1998, the owner became Gracenote, and in 2001 it sued its own former customer Roxio for switching to the volunteer fork freedb, alleging among other things a DMCA violation for reaching "an unauthorized derivative of the CDDB copyrighted database" ([Slashdot, 2001](https://news.slashdot.org/story/01/05/11/1247214/gracenote-sues-roxio-over-switch-to-free-song-database)). That claim, aimed at the volunteers' own submissions, is the surprise. The ending is ironic and documented by the EFF: Scherf expected the service to run "for decades" and doubted the forks would last; the CD faded, freedb closed in 2020, and a fork of the fork still runs ([EFF, 2021](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2021/05/outliving-outrage-public-interest-internet-cddb-story)).

This is the most told of the five (Wired in 2004 and 2006, the EFF in 2021), so the novelty is in the ending and in pairing it with AOL's unpaid Community Leaders, who sued for wages in May 1999 and settled eleven years later ([Priceonomics](https://priceonomics.com/the-aol-chat-room-monitor-revolt/)); the pairing is the researcher's framing, not a source's. Sourcing is mixed. The Wired pieces were read on an unofficial mirror, not wired.com. The key dates (the 1998 sale, the March 2001 lockout, the Roxio restraining order denied on 24 May 2001) rest on Wikipedia; freedb's founding is 1999 on one Wikipedia page and 2001 on another; the Roxio court, case number and settlement are not established; and an uncited claim that a patent was partly invalidated should not be used.

## Eight further candidates rank lower on novelty, protagonist or sourcing

**9. Netscape's random numbers and the Bugs Bounty (1995).** Ian Goldberg and David Wagner, Berkeley students, decompiled Navigator by hand because Netscape would not say how it seeded SSL keys, found the seed was the time plus two process IDs, and posted attack code on 18 September 1995 ([Goldberg's post](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~daw/my-posts/netscape-cracked-0)). Their own write-up says the unknown part could be searched in "about 25 seconds" and that RSA's offer to review the code beforehand had been declined ([Dr. Dobb's](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~daw/papers/ddj-netscape.html)). Netscape announced its "Bugs Bounty" on 10 October 1995 ([Dark Reading](https://www.darkreading.com/vulnerabilities---threats/the-bug-bounty-model-21-years-and-counting/a/d-id/1327752)). Each half is well known in its own field; joining them is the novelty, and that is the problem: the causal link is the researcher's inference, the press release was not opened, and the story of who proposed the bounty is single-sourced. An essay can say "22 days later" and no more.

**10. WoSign and StartCom, with DigiNotar as the prelude (2011 to 2018).** DigiNotar found its break-in on 19 July 2011 "but decided not to inform embedders of their root" ([Markham](https://blog.gerv.net/2011/09/diginotar-compromise/)); the alarm came about 40 days later from a pseudonymous Gmail help-forum post in Iran, and the company was declared bankrupt that September (Wikipedia only). That half has a Slate feature and a podcast episode and should not be retold alone. The sequel is nearly untold: Mozilla found that WoSign's Richard Wang had backdated SHA-1 certificates and quietly bought the free certificate authority StartCom, and both were denied "until sufficient data was collected" ([Mozilla Security Blog](https://blog.mozilla.org/security/2016/10/24/distrusting-new-wosign-and-startcom-certificates/)). The surprising fact is the proof: certificates dated December 2015 carried Certificate Transparency timestamps from January 2016 inside them ([Mozilla wiki](https://wiki.mozilla.org/CA:WoSign_Issues)), so the logging built after the first failure caught the second. Mozilla's two pages were opened; StartCom's history and closure are Wikipedia only, DigiNotar's headline numbers (531 certificates, 300,000 addresses) rest on press summaries because the Fox-IT reports would not open, and what became of Wang is unverified. Many readers ran StartSSL certificates, which is the audience hook.

**11. The EU browser choice screen (2007 to 2014).** Opera's Jon von Tetzchner complained to the Commission in December 2007 ([Opera](https://press.opera.com/2007/12/13/opera-files-antitrust-complaint-with-the-eu/)); the remedy was a screen offering Europeans a choice of browsers; and it vanished from Windows 7 SP1 for 14 months because, by Microsoft's account, nobody updated an update-detection rule ([Microsoft](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2012/07/17/statement-of-microsoft-corporation-on-eu-browser-choice-screen-compliance-3/)). The fine was **561 million euros** ([Commission, IP/13/196](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_13_196/IP_13_196_EN.pdf)). The surprise is that a user asked Microsoft's own support forum on 28 March 2011 whether the update was really in SP1 and was told it was ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1419490/microsoft-ignored-tip-that-it-botched-browser-choice-in-windows-7-sp1.html)); that is one journalist's find about a forum answer, not a formal warning. Numbers are the safest of any candidate, though sources disagree on the count affected (the Commission's 15 million users against Microsoft's "roughly 28 million PCs"). It ranks here because it was heavily covered as news, the protagonist goes missing after 2007, and the neatest irony, that Opera was reported to have dropped its own engine three weeks before the fine, rests on a search summary.

**12. Vendor prefixes and Glazman's call (2012 to 2016).** CSS Working Group co-chair Daniel Glazman published an all-caps appeal on 9 February 2012 warning that other browsers would implement `-webkit-` prefixes and "turn a market share into a _de facto_ standard" ([Glazblog](https://www.glazman.org/weblog/dotclear/index.php?post/2012/02/09/CALL-FOR-ACTION:-THE-OPEN-WEB-NEEDS-YOU-NOW)). It failed: Opera shipped aliases within eleven weeks, chosen by what Bruce Lawson called "a subjective judgment" ([Dev.Opera backup](https://raw.githack.com/operasoftware/devopera-static-backup/master/http/dev.opera.com/articles/view/opera-mobile-emulator-experimental-webkit-prefix-support/index.html)), and by 2016 Firefox 49 implemented WebKit's prefixes to match a WHATWG standard ([Mozilla Hacks](https://hacks.mozilla.org/2016/09/firefox-49-fixes-sites-designed-with-webkit-in-mind-and-more/)). No single piece was found carrying it from 2012 to 2016. The working group minutes and the statements usually quoted from them are unverified. It is the same underlying story as the MooTools pick at another layer, so the two should not run close together.

**13. Web SQL Database (2009 to 2024).** A web API died because its implementations were too compatible. The W3C note of 18 November 2010 says work stopped because "all interested implementors have used the same SQL backend (Sqlite)", and it contains the sentence that caused the impasse: "User agents must implement the SQL dialect supported by Sqlite 3.6.19." ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/webdatabase/)). Mozilla's Arun Ranganathan made the case against it in June 2010 ([Mozilla Hacks](https://hacks.mozilla.org/2010/06/beyond-html5-database-apis-and-the-road-to-indexeddb/)). Nothing long-form was found and the tombstone text is a ready-made hook, but the conflict played out on mailing lists that were not opened, so there are no named people making dated decisions yet, and Chrome's removal in 2024 is a search summary.

**14. Internet Exploder (1996).** Fred McLain wanted to show that Microsoft's Authenticode proved who wrote a control and nothing about what it did, so McLain wrote one that shut down Windows 95 from a web page and signed it with a VeriSign certificate; Microsoft's own technote conceded that "Authenticode doesn't verify that software is free of bugs and malicious intent" ([MSDN technote mirror](https://techshelps.github.io/MSDN/TECHART/html/msdn_explode.htm)). It is a strong fit with almost no modern retelling, and it is not ready: McLain's own pages could not be opened, so the motive above is unverified, and the best fact, that this was the first certificate VeriSign revoked against its holder's will, was seen only in a search result for one 1997 book.

**15. VeriSign Site Finder (2003).** For 19 days every mistyped .com name resolved to VeriSign's ad-bearing search page, until ICANN's Paul Twomey set a deadline to the hour, "6:00 PM PDT on 4 October 2003", after VeriSign had refused a polite request ([ICANN](https://www.icann.org/resources/unthemed-pages/twomey-to-lewis-2003-10-03-en)). Anyone who ran a mail server then will remember it. Only that letter was opened; the BIND patch is a search summary and the lawsuit and 2006 settlement are Wikipedia only.

**16. favicon.ico's late check-in (1998 to 1999).** Ray Sun's first-person post says Bharat Shyam showed a build late one evening after the schedule had closed, asked "this is good, right? Check it in?", and that Sun, a junior program manager, said yes and was reprimanded ([Ray Sun](https://ruthlessray.wordpress.com/2013/09/02/inventing-favicon-ico/)). It is charming, has one source, lacks Shyam's account, and is too slight for a full essay unless widened into the files browsers request without being asked.

## Conclusion

The research changes what "under-reported" should mean for this collection. The famous fights (the browser wars, DigiNotar, the Texas patent trial) are not where the room is; it is in the small dated artifact that a famous outcome hangs from and that nobody has put at the front of the story: a mailing-list reply from 1995, a ten-line config change, a non-enumerable property, a signed email that lacks the one word everyone uses for it. Four of the top six picks correct something the audience believes it knows (who wrote "bitter failure", why it is `includes`, what prompted robots.txt, whether Postel ran a "test"), and that is a more reliable source of surprise for readers who lived the period than an obscure subject would be.

Two practical points follow. First, the single largest obstacle across all five research threads was not missing history but a blocked Internet Archive; Wei's own account, Hyatt's posts, McLain's pages and the Hotline judgments are probably one working fetch away, and reopening them would promote several holds. Second, the picks cluster in a way an editor can use: Wei and the W3C policy fight make one essay, DigiNotar and WoSign make one, CDDB and AOL make one, and Postel, Site Finder and robots.txt form a loose trilogy about arrangements that held while everyone involved fit on one mailing list. Commission Wei first, Fielding second because it is quick, and MooTools third because it is the one the readers type every day.
