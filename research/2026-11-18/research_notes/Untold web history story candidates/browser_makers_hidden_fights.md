## Key question 1: KHTML and Apple, 2003 to 2005 and after

### Takeaway
There is a real story with a dated turn: a warm January 2003 welcome, two years of "code bombs", a one-line sarcastic email on 28 April 2005 that became a public row within 24 hours, and Apple opening WebKit's CVS and bug database on 7 June 2005. Important correction to the brief: "The bitter failure named 'Safari and KHTML'" was written by Cristian Tibirna on 29 April 2005 in reply to Zack Rusin, not by Rusin; Rusin's own post is "So when will KHTML merge all the WebCore changes?" of 28 April 2005.

Dirk Mueller forwarded it to kfm-devel the same day at 20:19:48 GMT. ### Inferences
- Two-sentence test: KDE's volunteers wanted Apple's Safari fixes to flow back into the engine they had written, but Apple shipped them as periodic unmergeable code drops, so when Safari passed Acid2 and users asked when Konqueror would, the volunteers said in public that the collaboration "doesn't exist", and six weeks later Apple opened its repository. The reader ends knowing that the engine in Chrome, Edge and Safari began as a KDE library whose authors lost control of it, and that KDE's own browser now runs on the great-grandchild (KHTML to WebKit to Blink to QtWebEngine to Konqueror).
- The ending is ironic rather than triumphant: Apple's opening did not reunite the trees; the open WebKit simply replaced KHTML. The surprise for this audience is likely the smallness of the trigger (a one-line email, "I'm eagerly awaiting for you to merge them back.") and the full-circle ending.
- "Carewolf" is very probably Allan Sandfeld Jensen (LWN attributes a nearby kdedevelopers.org post about the IRC talks to "Allan Sandfeld"), but the blog page shows only the handle. Do not state the identity without confirming.
- Coverage level: I found no book chapter or long-form feature that tells 2003-2005 as a narrative. It exists as LWN and CNET news items, Wikipedia paragraphs, and the 2006 Yahoo talk. Don Melton's later blog memoirs about Safari and the Knoll/Staikos video are the nearest things (not opened here).

## Key question 2: The EU browser choice screen, 2007 to 2014

### Takeaway
The Commission's own releases give a clean spine: complaint 12 Dec 2007, Statement of Objections 15 Jan 2009, commitments made binding 16 Dec 2009, screen live from March 2010, missing from Windows 7 SP1 from May 2011 to July 2012 for 15 million users, fine of 561 million euros on 6 March 2013. The "ignored tip" is real but smaller than the headline: it was one user's question on the Microsoft Answers forum on 28 March 2011, answered wrongly by a support engineer, reported by one journalist.

### Cited Findings
- Opera's press release is dated 13 Dec 2007 and says the complaint was filed "yesterday" (12 Dec 2007). Two remedies asked: unbundle IE from Windows and/or pre-install alternative browsers; and require Microsoft to follow open web standards. Jon von Tetzchner (CEO): "We are filing this complaint on behalf of all consumers who are tired of having a monopolist make choices for them," and "We cannot rest until we've brought fair and equitable options to consumers worldwide." Jason Hoida (Deputy General Counsel): "Our complaint is necessary to get Microsoft to amend its practices." No Håkon Wium Lie quote in the release (OPENED) — [Opera press release, 13 Dec 2007](https://press.opera.com/2007/12/13/opera-files-antitrust-complaint-with-the-eu/)
- Commission release IP/09/1941, Brussels, 16 December 2009: commitments made legally binding; Choice Screen via Windows Update for five years in the EEA for users of Windows XP, Vista and 7; follows a Statement of Objections of 15 January 2009; market test launched October 2009; breach could bring "a fine of up to 10% of Microsoft's total annual turnover without having to prove any violation of EU antitrust rules"; Microsoft "will report regularly to the Commission, starting in six months' time". Neelie Kroes: "Millions of European consumers will benefit from this decision by having a free choice about which web browser they use." (OPENED as PDF text) — [IP/09/1941](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_09_1941/IP_09_1941_EN.pdf)
- Commission release IP/13/196, Brussels, 6 March 2013: "a €561 million fine"; Microsoft "failed to roll out the browser choice screen with its Windows 7 Service Pack 1 from May 2011 until July 2012. 15 million Windows users in the EU therefore did not see the choice screen during this period."; the screen "was provided as of March 2010"; "until November 2010, 84 million browsers were downloaded through it"; "This is the first time that the Commission has had to fine a company for non-compliance with a commitments decision."; mitigating circumstance was Microsoft's cooperation. Joaquín Almunia: "A failure to comply is a very serious infringement that must be sanctioned accordingly." Investigation opened July 2012 (IP/12/800), objections October 2012 (IP/12/1149) (OPENED as PDF text) — [IP/13/196](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_13_196/IP_13_196_EN.pdf)
- Figures disagree by source: Microsoft's July 2012 statement says "roughly 28 million PCs"; the Commission's fine release says 15 million users; Almunia is quoted elsewhere as "around 15.3 million users" over about 14 months. Start of the gap is given as SP1's release in February 2011 (July 2012 reporting) and as May 2011 (the decision). (28m and 15m OPENED; 15.3m and February SNIPPET) — [Microsoft statement, 17 Jul 2012](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2012/07/17/statement-of-microsoft-corporation-on-eu-browser-choice-screen-compliance-3/), [TheJournal.ie, Mar 2013](https://www.thejournal.ie/microsoft-european-commission-fine-browser-822179-Mar2013/)
- Microsoft's explanation (statement of 17 July 2012): a technical error; the Windows Update "detection logic" that decides which PCs get the Browser Choice Screen was not updated for SP1; the engineering team maintaining the code "did not realize that it needed to update the detection logic"; Microsoft filed a compliance report in December 2011 believing all was well; it learned of the problem from the Commission, began testing a fix on 2 July 2012 and distributing it on 3 July 2012; it "deeply regret[s] that this error occurred", retained outside counsel to investigate, and offered to extend the obligation by 15 months. No individual is named (OPENED) — [Microsoft statement, 17 Jul 2012](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2012/07/17/statement-of-microsoft-corporation-on-eu-browser-choice-screen-compliance-3/)
- The "ignored tip": Gregg Keizer, Computerworld, 18 July 2012. A user called "RichardD" asked on Microsoft Answers on 28 March 2011, about five weeks after SP1 shipped, whether KB976002 (the choice screen update) was included; a day later Afzal Taher, signing as "Microsoft Support Engineer", replied that SP1 "contains all the previous Windows Updates."; RichardD asked again "is it really installed with Service Pack 1?" and got no answer. The article cites the forum thread, not a Commission document, and does not say who complained to the Commission (OPENED) — [Computerworld, 18 Jul 2012](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1419490/microsoft-ignored-tip-that-it-botched-browser-choice-in-windows-7-sp1.html)
- Who told the Commission: unnamed. Almunia said the Commission had "received indications from third parties", admitted "We trusted in the reports on the compliance [from Microsoft]", and declined to name the complainant; one report speculates Mozilla. Google and Opera are not named in anything I opened (SNIPPET) — [Computerworld, "EU let Microsoft police itself"](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1400074/eu-let-microsoft-police-itself-on-browser-ballot-promises.html), [The Register, 17 Jul 2012](https://www.theregister.com/2012/07/17/microsoft_ec_browser_choice_fresh_investigation/)
- Who paid inside Microsoft: the fiscal 2012 proxy (October 2012) cut Steve Ballmer's bonus to $620,000, 91% of target, citing among other things the Windows division's failure to provide the browser choice screen on some PCs in Europe; Steven Sinofsky got 90% of target ($7.65 million in bonus and stock). Sinofsky left months later (SNIPPET for figures; the Sinofsky/Ballmer cut is confirmed in an OPENED Computerworld piece without amounts) — [Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/features/microsoft-ceos-bonus-trimmed-for-windows-browser-misstep), [Computerworld, 18 Dec 2014](https://www.computerworld.com/article/2860886/microsoft-nixes-eu-browser-ballot-screen.html)
- Did it work? Keizer, Computerworld, 18 Dec 2014: "There is little evidence that the choice screen changed the browser landscape." StatCounter Dec 2009 to Nov 2014: Opera's share down 27%, Firefox down 42%. The Commission called it "a successful remedy", saying the screen was viewed 795 million times and 165 million browsers were installed through it. Microsoft retired the screen in December 2014: "The obligations imposed by that decision have expired." (OPENED; the percentages are relative declines in share as the article frames them, recheck) — [Computerworld, 18 Dec 2014](https://www.computerworld.com/article/2860886/microsoft-nixes-eu-browser-ballot-screen.html)
- Opera's reaction in March 2010: downloads roughly doubled to tripled in several EU countries (TechCrunch "more than a doubling"; other outlets "tripled" in France, Spain, UK, Poland). Opera hedged: "The tripling is overall downloads but we believe most of those are attributed to the choice screen." Opera 10.5 shipped at the same moment. Håkon Wium Lie (CTO) said the results confirm that when users are given a real choice "they will try out alternatives" (SNIPPET) — [TechCrunch, 18 Mar 2010](https://techcrunch.com/2010/03/18/microsofts-european-browser-choice-screen-causes-spike-in-opera-downloads), [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/news/internet/web/opera-very-satisfied-with-browser-ballot-676718)
- Opera by 2013: on 13 February 2013, three weeks before the fine, Opera announced 300 million monthly users and that it would drop its Presto engine for WebKit; in April 2013 it said it would follow Google to Blink. A headline says Opera's workforce fell about 10% with the switch (SNIPPET; layoff figure is a headline only) — [Engadget, 13 Feb 2013](https://www.engadget.com/2013-02-13-opera-300-million-webkit-switch.html), [TNW, 3 Apr 2013](https://thenextweb.com/news/opera-confirms-it-will-follow-google-and-ditch-webkit-for-blink-as-part-of-its-commitment-to-chromium?fromcat=all)
- Smaller browser makers complained about the ballot's design in 2010 (SNIPPET, title only) — [OSNews](https://www.osnews.com/story/22967/smaller-browser-makers-complain-about-ballot-design/)

### Inferences
- Two-sentence test: Opera wanted regulators to break IE's free ride on Windows, and got a screen that offered Europeans a choice of browsers; then a missed update rule made the screen vanish for 14 months without Microsoft or the Commission noticing, so Microsoft paid 561 million euros for a remedy that, by the time of the fine, the complainant had outlived as an engine maker. The reader ends knowing how the screen disappeared (a detection rule), who noticed first (a forum user), and that the winner of the period was a browser that was not on the market when Opera complained (Chrome shipped in September 2008).
- The irony worth checking and using: Opera's second demand was that Microsoft be forced to follow web standards; five years later Opera gave up its own standards-first engine.
- The "Microsoft ignored a tip" framing is accurate only in the narrow sense of a support-forum answer. It is one journalist's find; do not present it as a formal warning.
- Coverage: heavily covered as news in 2009-2013 (Reuters, Computerworld, Register, Ars). I found no long-form feature or book treatment joining 2007 to 2014. Known to the audience as "that ballot thing"; the mechanism and aftermath are not.

## Key question 3: Spyglass and Microsoft, 1994 to 1997

### Takeaway
The outline holds (Microsoft licensed Spyglass Mosaic, gave IE away, Spyglass got minimum payments, settled for $8 million on 22 January 1997), but the popular one-liner "a percentage of IE revenue, and IE was free, so Spyglass got nothing" is not what the contemporary reports I opened say. They describe a $2 million flat purchase for Windows 95 in December 1994 plus a later per-copy licence for other platforms with quarterly minimums and a $5 million annual cap. I could not open the trial record, the 10-K or any Colbeth quote, so this topic is under-sourced for now.

### Cited Findings
- Seattle Times, Michele Matassa Flores, 2 January 1997, "Spyglass Blames Loss On Microsoft -- Firm Hasn't Received Browser Royalty Fees": Spyglass projected a $4 million first-quarter loss (10 to 13 cents a share) and blamed missing Microsoft royalties; it had received only a $400,000 minimum payment where analysts expected $1 million to $2 million; it would audit Microsoft's IE distribution. Terms as reported: in December 1994 Microsoft paid $2 million to buy some of Spyglass's browser code outright; a year later it licensed more code on a per-user basis for platforms other than Windows 95; that arrangement is a minimum quarterly payment plus royalties based on distribution; the Windows 95 deal is a flat fee with no per-licence royalty. Spyglass's letter said it "is not able to determine the amount of royalties due from Microsoft in excess of the minimum payment". Spyglass spokesman Randy Pitzer called the missing royalties "a very large part" of the loss. Microsoft spokesman Mark Murray said Microsoft had "prepaid several million dollars in royalties." Shares closed at $11.375, down 9% (OPENED) — [Seattle Times, 2 Jan 1997](https://archive.seattletimes.com/archive/?date=19970102&slug=2516784)
- Business Week Online News Flash, Peter Elstrom, 22 January 1997 (mirrored copy): Microsoft pays $8 million to end the dispute, $7.5 million in cash, once, plus $500,000 in software and other considerations, and may use the technology with no further payments; the licence had given Spyglass a royalty per copy of Explorer issued, capped at $5 million a year; the audit announced 2 January is cancelled. Spyglass CFO Gary Vilchick: "We're just happy that didn't have to go down the road [of auditing Microsoft] that we started to go down." Spyglass planned to focus on browsers for televisions, pagers and other devices (OPENED, on a third-party mirror) — [Business Week via landley.net](https://www.landley.net/history/mirror/ms/new0122d.htm)
- Spokesman-Review ran the settlement on 24 January 1997 (SNIPPET, headline) — [Spokesman-Review](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/1997/jan/24/spyglass-microsoft-settle-royalty-dispute/)
- Eric Sink, project lead of the Spyglass browser team, "Memoirs From the Browser Wars", 15 April 2003: "We won the Microsoft deal." "Internet Explorer 2.0 was basically Spyglass Mosaic with not too many changes." "Over 120 companies licensed Spyglass Mosaic so they could bundle it into their product." On Netscape: "they began to give their browser away at no cost to end users." On learning Microsoft's team size: "I was stunned." "I left with no regrets and no bitterness." The memoir as returned gives no licence amounts (OPENED) — [Eric Sink, 2003](https://ericsink.com/Browser_Wars.html)
- Dates disagree: Seattle Times and Forbes say December 1994; Wikipedia says the deal was reached in January 1995, citing the New York Times of 1 March 1995; Microsoft's appeal brief says it licensed Mosaic from Spyglass in 1994 (SNIPPET for Forbes, NYT and the brief) — [Wikipedia: Spyglass, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spyglass,_Inc.), [Forbes, 7 Nov 1997](https://www.forbes.com/1997/11/07/col.html), [Microsoft's brief on appeal](https://cyber.harvard.edu/msdoj/ms-appeal.html)
- Forbes (7 Nov 1997) gives the popular version: Spyglass was to get a royalty for every copy of IE sold, but Microsoft gave the browser away, and Spyglass got $8 million to drop all claims (SNIPPET) — [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/1997/11/07/col.html)
- Background per Wikipedia and its footnotes: Spyglass licensed NCSA Mosaic in May 1994 for "several million dollars" (Computerworld, 29 Aug 1994); IE 6 and earlier credited Spyglass and NCSA Mosaic in the About box, removed in IE7; OpenTV announced on 26 March 2000 it would buy Spyglass in a stock swap put at $2.5 billion (Variety) and completed it in July 2000; Kudelski took full ownership of OpenTV on 29 March 2010 (OPENED as Wikipedia; underlying sources not opened) — [Wikipedia: Spyglass, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spyglass,_Inc.)
- Douglas Colbeth: president and CEO from 1990, chairman and CEO June 1999 to July 2000 (SNIPPET). The Internet History Podcast interviewed Colbeth (episode dated 28 August 2016, host Brian McCullough); the page has no transcript, and its summary says "Internet Explorer was developed as a competitor to Netscape Navigator because Spyglass did a deal with Microsoft." (OPENED) — [Internet History Podcast](https://www.internethistorypodcast.com/2016/08/douglas-colbeth-co-founder-and-ceo-of-spyglass/)
- Spyglass's Form 10-K for the year ended 30 September 1997 exists on EDGAR and is the likeliest place for the exact terms (not opened; sec.gov returned an error page) — [EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/945256/0000950137-97-004112.txt)

### Inferences
- The commonly told version needs three corrections, on the evidence above: (1) the Windows 95 code was bought for a flat $2 million, not licensed for a share of revenue; (2) the royalty was per copy distributed, with quarterly minimums and a $5 million annual cap, on a later non-Windows-95 licence; (3) the fight was over Microsoft paying only the minimum and not reporting copies, and Microsoft said it had prepaid. "Percentage of revenue from a free product" appears in secondary sources (Wikipedia mirror text, Forbes) but not in the two January 1997 reports. Two reporters, one each, so still thin.
- The stronger angle may be the damage to Spyglass's other customers: Sink's 120 licensees were paying for what Microsoft then gave away. That is my inference; I found no opened source stating the revenue effect.
- Two-sentence test (provisional): Spyglass wanted to be the arms dealer of the browser war and sold Microsoft its code for $2 million, but Microsoft gave the result away free and so undercut every other Spyglass customer, so Spyglass took $8 million in January 1997 and left the desktop for set-top boxes, then sold itself at the top of the bubble. The surprise could be the ending: the "victim" was bought for about $2.5 billion in stock three years later.
- Coverage: told in passing in every browser-war history and in the 2016 podcast; Sink's memoir is well known among developers. Moderately known, rarely told accurately.

### Gaps

- What Spyglass's revenue did after IE went free (the 1996 and 1997 10-K numbers): not obtained.

## Key question 4: New candidates

### Takeaway
Three candidates meet the test of named protagonist, dated turn and ending. The Fielding/IE10 Do Not Track patch and the death of Web SQL Database are little told and come with real code; Eolas is the biggest story but was covered at length in 2012.

### Cited Findings

**D. Roy Fielding patches Apache to ignore Internet Explorer 10 (2012)**
- Two-sentence story: Microsoft wanted IE10 to send the Do Not Track header by default, but Roy Fielding, co-founder of Apache and an editor of the W3C's DNT specification, held that a default was not a user's choice, so he committed a change to the Apache web server that stripped the header from IE10 requests, under the message "Apache does not tolerate deliberate abuse of open standards". The reader ends knowing that a standards editor used the world's most popular web server to overrule a browser vendor, that it was undone within weeks, and that DNT itself died anyway.
- The Register, Gavin Clarke, 10 September 2012, "Apache man disables Internet Explorer 10 privacy setting": the commit message is "Apache does not tolerate deliberate abuse of open standards."; Fielding said "Microsoft deliberately violates the standard." and "If you have a problem with it, choose a better browser."; Fielding was helping draft the W3C Do Not Track spec in the Tracking Protection working group, of which Microsoft was also a member; Microsoft's chief privacy officer Brendon Lynch had framed default DNT as "privacy by design and putting people first"; Mozilla also opposed default-on; Tim Anderson noted the draft's user-choice wording was added after Windows 8's February consumer preview (OPENED) — [The Register, 10 Sep 2012](https://www.theregister.com/2012/09/10/fielding_apache_ie10_windows_8/)
- Fielding's stated rationale: "The only reason DNT exists is to express a non-default option" (SNIPPET) — [itwriting.com](https://www.itwriting.com/blog/6519-adobes-roy-fielding-patches-apache-to-ignore-ie10-do-not-track-privacy-request.html/comment-page-1)
- The change shipped: the httpd 2.4.3 changelog added configuration directives setting a `bad_DNT` environment variable for the IE10 user-agent and removing the DNT request header (SNIPPET from a forum quoting the changelog) — [Apache Lounge](https://www.apachelounge.com/viewtopic.php?p=22599)
- The reversal: MediaPost reported on 9 October 2012 that the latest Apache code effectively negated the patch, spotted by researcher Jonathan Mayer (SNIPPET; page returned 403) — [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/184853/apache-no-longer-negates-ie10-do-not-track-headers.html)
- Counter-argument at the time: IE10 told users during "express settings" setup that DNT would be on, so it arguably complied; other Apache contributors disapproved in comments; Yahoo also said it would ignore IE10's DNT (SNIPPET) — [Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1536081/privacy-experts-criticize-moves-to-sidestep-ie10-s-default-do-not-track-se.html)
- Best primary sources to open: the httpd commit and its GitHub/SVN comment thread; the httpd-dev list for September-October 2012; Microsoft's 31 May 2012 IE10 DNT announcement (Brendon Lynch); the W3C Tracking Preference Expression drafts; the public-tracking list.

**E. Web SQL Database: killed because everyone used the same engine (2009 to 2010, removed 2024)**
- Two-sentence story: Apple, Google and Opera shipped a SQL database in the browser and the editor wrote it into a W3C draft, but Mozilla refused to implement a spec whose definition of SQL was "whatever SQLite does", so the W3C stopped work on 18 November 2010 and the web got IndexedDB instead. The reader ends knowing that a web API died for the unusual reason that its implementations were too compatible, and that Chrome carried the dead API for another 13 years.
- The W3C document itself, "W3C Working Group Note 18 November 2010", editor Ian Hickson (Google, Inc.): "Beware. This specification is no longer in active maintenance"; "This document was on the W3C Recommendation track but specification work has stopped."; "The specification reached an impasse: all interested implementors have used the same SQL backend (Sqlite)" "but we need multiple independent implementations to proceed along a standardisation path."; and the line that caused it, "User agents must implement the SQL dialect supported by Sqlite 3.6.19." (OPENED) — [W3C Web SQL Database](https://www.w3.org/TR/webdatabase/)
- Mozilla's case, Arun Ranganathan, hacks.mozilla.org, 1 June 2010: "there isn't a single normative SQL standard that defines the technology."; "SQLite itself isn't a specification — it's a release-ready technology!"; "we don't want changes to SQLite to affect the web later"; Microsoft "agree with us that IndexedDB is a good option"; developers at Mozilla's summit showed "resigned acceptance of a SQLite-based API." In comments Mozilla's Shawn Wilsher said neither Microsoft nor Mozilla supported Web SQL (OPENED for the post; comment is SNIPPET) — [Mozilla Hacks, 1 Jun 2010](https://hacks.mozilla.org/2010/06/beyond-html5-database-apis-and-the-road-to-indexeddb/)
- The ending: Chrome's developer blog describes Web SQL as introduced in April 2009 and abandoned in November 2010; Chromium 124 (April 2024) removed it (SNIPPET) — [Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/deprecating-web-sql), [Wikipedia: Web SQL Database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_SQL_Database)
- A hostile contemporary view: a blog post accusing Mozilla and Microsoft of torpedoing Web SQL "on spurious grounds" (SNIPPET) — [majid.info](https://blog.majid.info/incensed-at-mozilla/)
- Later regret thread on public-webapps, late 2012 (SNIPPET, list index only) — [public-webapps 2012](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-webapps/2012OctDec/0436.html)
- Best primary sources to open: public-webapps archives for 2009 (the Mozilla objections, Oracle's Nikunj Mehta proposing what became IndexedDB), Mozilla's "There is no simple solution for local storage" (2012), the Chrome deprecation posts. The Mehta/Oracle and Jonas Sicking roles are from my memory and were not confirmed by any page I opened.

**F. Eolas and the '906 patent: the man who claimed the plug-in (1994 to 2013)**
- Two-sentence story: Michael Doyle's company Eolas, holding a University of California patent on embedding interactive programs in web pages, won $520.6 million from Microsoft in August 2003 and forced IE to make users click to activate every Flash movie, but when Eolas sued much of the rest of the web in Texas, Tim Berners-Lee and Pei-Yuan Wei, author of the Viola browser, testified, and a jury threw the patents out in February 2012. The reader ends knowing why "click to activate this control" and a generation of JavaScript embed workarounds existed, and that a 1993 student browser ended it.
- Verdict of August 2003: $520.6 million against Microsoft on US patent 5,838,906, owned by the University of California and exclusively licensed to Eolas; upheld by the judge in January 2004; overturned in part on appeal in March 2005; settled in 2007 on confidential terms; Microsoft removed click-to-activate in April 2008 after announcing in November 2007 that it had licensed the technology (SNIPPET) — [The Register, 12 Aug 2003](https://www.theregister.com/2003/08/12/microsoft_fined_520m_for_infringing/), [eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/court-overturns-eolas-browser-judgment-against-microsoft/)
- The Register, Iain Thomson, 8 February 2012: Berners-Lee was flown to Texas to testify before an eight-person jury; defendants named are Adobe, Oracle, Apple, Texas Instruments, Yahoo! and YouTube, with Oracle and Texas Instruments settling; Eolas "won $520m" in 2003; the University of California reported making over $30m on the Microsoft settlement; an earlier Berners-Lee filing spoke of "Removing the improperly disruptive effect of this invalid patent" (OPENED) — [The Register, 8 Feb 2012](https://www.theregister.com/2012/02/08/eolas_patent_claim_adobe/)
- Verdict: the Tyler, Texas jury found the two patents invalid after a few hours; defence witnesses included Berners-Lee, Eric Bina, Dave Raggett and Pei-Yuan Wei; winners included Google, Amazon, Adobe, Yahoo, JCPenney and YouTube; Ars Technica reported in July 2013 that the patents were "dead on appeal" (SNIPPET; defendant lists differ between sources) — [Boing Boing, 10 Feb 2012](https://boingboing.net/2012/02/10/patent-troll-that-claimed-owne.html), [The Register, 9 Feb 2012](https://www.theregister.co.uk/2012/02/09/eolas_loses_patent_case/), [Ars via Infowarrior, Jul 2013](https://attrition.org/pipermail/infowarrior/2013-July/010311.html)
- Best primary sources to open: Berners-Lee's October 2003 letter to the USPTO (w3.org), Microsoft's 2006 IE "click to activate" developer notes, the Wired trial reports of February 2012, the Federal Circuit opinions.

### Inferences
- D is the best fit for the brief: few developers know it, the protagonist is famous for other reasons (REST, HTTP), the conflict is a principled one where both sides have a case, and the artifact is a few lines of server config. The ending needs one more source: DNT's own death (the W3C working group's closure and Safari dropping the header, both from my memory, unverified).
- E is under-told and suits the audience's memory of `openDatabase()`; the W3C tombstone text is a ready-made hook. Its weakness is that the conflict played out on mailing lists and needs names attached from the 2009 archive.
- F is the strongest drama but the Texas trial was widely reported in 2012. It would be a "dots connected" essay (click-to-activate, swfobject-style workarounds, Viola), not an unknown story.
- Other leads I considered but did not research, all from memory and unverified: the Phoenix to Firebird to Firefox naming fights and Debian's Iceweasel (2003-2016); AOL's 2003 settlement with Microsoft followed weeks later by the Netscape layoffs and the Mozilla Foundation; the 2012 "-webkit- prefix" crisis in the CSS Working Group ending with Opera aliasing WebKit prefixes; Apple's patent disclosures over canvas and later over W3C Touch Events, which produced Pointer Events.

### Gaps
- D: the exact commit date, the config lines as committed, who reverted or neutralised it and in which release, and Fielding's later comments are not verified on an opened primary page.
- E: which person at Mozilla first objected and when; Hickson's own account; Apple's position. All need the public-webapps archive.
- F: the Wired pieces, the Berners-Lee letter and the 2013 appeal ruling were not opened; the 2007 settlement amount is confidential by all accounts.
