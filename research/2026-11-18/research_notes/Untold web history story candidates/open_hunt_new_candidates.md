# Open hunt: new under-reported story candidates (web and JavaScript, 1990 to 2016)

Reading note for the report writer. Twelve candidates are vetted below, ranked by how well they meet the "Is there a story?" test in AGENTS.md. Every quotation was returned from a page I opened, but the pages were read through a fetch tool that summarises, so **each quotation must be re-read on the source page before it goes into an essay**. Anything marked UNVERIFIED was not confirmed on an opened page. None of the twelve is on the excluded list; overlaps with excluded topics are flagged per candidate.

Ranking, best first: (1) Fielding's Apache patch against IE10's Do Not Track; (2) MooTools breaks `contains` twice; (3) Goldberg and Wagner break Netscape's random numbers; (4) favicon.ico's late check-in; (5) Internet Exploder; (6) vendor prefixes and Glazman's call; (7) Node's removed promises and Fantasy Land; (8) PNG in ten weeks, then MNG; (9) GeoCities "Haunting"; (10) Erwise; (11) the 1996 web blackout; (12) the DOCTYPE switch.

## What fights, betrayals, reversals, near-misses or accidents are well documented in primary sources but rarely retold?

### Takeaway
Twelve candidates hold up, and the strongest five each have a single surviving artifact a reader can open (a commit, a Bugzilla bug, a mailing-list post, a first-person blog post, a vendor technote). The richest unmined seams turned out to be Bugzilla web-compat bugs, the cypherpunks list of 1995 to 1996, and GitHub/Google Groups threads from 2010 to 2014, more than the early W3C lists.

### Cited Findings

#### 1. Roy Fielding patches Apache to ignore IE10's Do Not Track (2012)
- **Two sentences.** Roy Fielding, an editor of the Do Not Track work and an Apache httpd co-founder, wanted DNT to mean a choice a person had made, but Microsoft announced IE10 would send it by default, so Fielding committed ten lines to Apache's default config that deleted the header from every IE10 request. The reader learns that a privacy signal was neutralised on roughly 60% of web servers by one person's commit, with a commit message as the manifesto.
- The commit is in `docs/conf/httpd.conf.in`, +10 lines, author `royfielding`, message: "Apache does not tolerate deliberate abuse of open standards" (svn trunk r1371878). — [apache/httpd commit a381ff3](https://github.com/apache/httpd/commit/a381ff35fa4d50a5f7b9f64300dfd98859dee8d0)
- The added lines, verbatim (real period code, 31 characters at the widest directive, good for a hook):
  `BrowserMatch "MSIE 10.0;" bad_DNT` and `RequestHeader unset DNT env=bad_DNT`, under the comment "# Deal with user agents that deliberately violate open standards". — [apache/httpd commit a381ff3](https://github.com/apache/httpd/commit/a381ff35fa4d50a5f7b9f64300dfd98859dee8d0)
- The Register (Gavin Clarke, 10 Sep 2012) quotes Fielding: "The only reason DNT exists is to express a non-default option."; "Microsoft deliberately violates the standard."; "If you have a problem with it, choose a better browser." It cites Netcraft for Apache running nearly 600 million sites, about 60%. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2012/09/10/fielding_apache_ie10_windows_8/)
- The W3C draft text at issue, as quoted by The Register: "it MUST reflect the user's preference, not the preference of some institutional or network-imposed mechanism". The same article reports commentator Tim Anderson's point that this user-choice wording was added after the Windows 8 consumer preview of 29 February 2012. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2012/09/10/fielding_apache_ie10_windows_8/)
- Microsoft's side, via chief privacy officer Brendon Lynch: "We believe consumers should have more control over how data about their online behaviour is tracked, shared, and used". Mozilla, which shipped DNT first, opposed default-on. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2012/09/10/fielding_apache_ie10_windows_8/)
- Press coverage says critics (privacy advocates) attacked the patch, that Microsoft argued Windows 8 "express settings" informed users, and that Fielding then worked at Adobe. — [Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1536081/privacy-experts-criticize-moves-to-sidestep-ie10-s-default-do-not-track-se.html); [itwriting.com](https://itwriting.com/blog/6519-adobes-roy-fielding-patches-apache-to-ignore-ie10-do-not-track-privacy-request.html/comment-page-1)
- **How well told.** News coverage for one week in September 2012 (Register, Computerworld, Threatpost, Neowin). I found no retrospective that follows the story to DNT's end.
- **Weak points.** Commit date not shown on the page I opened (the svn revision and the 10 Sep 2012 press date bracket it). Whether the lines were later reverted from httpd trunk, Microsoft's later reversal of the default, and the closing of the W3C Tracking Protection group are all UNVERIFIED here and need sources. Fielding's employer (Adobe) comes from press, not from him. Both sides have a fair case and the essay must give Microsoft's and the privacy advocates' arguments properly.

#### 2. MooTools breaks `contains` twice, and JavaScript gets `includes` (2012 to 2014)
- **Two sentences.** TC39 and Mozilla wanted the obvious name `contains` for strings and then arrays, but a 2008-era library, MooTools, had already put its own `contains` on the built-in prototypes of sites nobody would ever update, so the committee gave up the name in November 2014 and every JavaScript developer now types `includes`. The reader learns the exact mechanism of both breakages and that the same library forced the same retreat twice.
- First break: Bug 789036, "MooTools 1.2.x was broken by adding String.prototype.contains", reporter Benjamin Peterson. Peterson: "MooTools defines a incompatible version of String.prototype.contains." MooTools 1.2.x assumed its own version was in use; Gian-Carlo Pascutto raised MooTools' estimated 5 to 8% share; Brendan Eich asked whether to disable it in 18.0.1; Boris Zbarsky: "Looks like we shipped this breakage in Firefox 18". Daniel Buchner: "MooTools will be releasing a 1.2.6 for that legacy code which corrects the issue for MooTools users." — [Bugzilla 789036](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=789036)
- Second break: Bug 1075059, "non-enumerable Array.prototype.contains is not web-compatible (breaks jsfiddle.net)", reporter Boris Zbarsky. JSFiddle's Save button died with "TypeError: this.contains is not a function" in `moo-clientcide-1.3.js`. Mechanism: MooTools copies Array methods onto its `Elements` type by enumerating `Array.prototype` with `for...in`; the native method is non-enumerable, and MooTools' own guarded assignment "does not change enumerability", so the method was never copied. Jason Orendorff saw "no choice but to back this out"; Till Schneidereit: "too many sites are affected". JSFiddle's Piotr Zalewa patched their copy; John-David Dalton pointed at a MooTools patch, and Zbarsky replied it would not help until sites deployed it. — [Bugzilla 1075059](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1075059)
- The official record of the retreat: "This proposal was formerly for Array.prototype.contains, but that name is not web-compatible. Per the November 2014 TC39 meeting, the name of both String.prototype.contains and Array.prototype.contains was changed to includes". — [tc39/Array.prototype.includes](https://github.com/tc39/Array.prototype.includes)
- The string rename bug: Till Schneidereit writes that "we discovered that `Array.prototype.contains` breaks the web", that "the IE team also reported web-compat issues with `String.prototype.contains`", and that Firefox had "shipped `String.prototype.contains` since Firefox 18", so an alias-and-warn transition was needed; fixed in Firefox 40. — [Bugzilla 1102219](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1102219)
- **How well told.** Known as a one-line trivia item ("includes because of MooTools"), repeated in blog posts and the Ember RFC renaming its own `contains`. The 2012 first round, the enumeration mechanism, and the people in the bugs are almost never told. The 2018 sequel ("smoosh", `flatten` versus MooTools again) is outside the date range but is the natural "What it cost" coda.
- **Weak points.** Bugzilla pages show relative dates only; exact filing dates need the bug's history view. I did not open the November 2014 TC39 meeting notes or Domenic Denicola's proposal history; name the champion only after opening those. MooTools authors' own account (Valerio Proietti, Christoph Pojer and others) is missing and the essay must not punch down at them: they shipped a 2008 library under 2008 rules. Slight thematic overlap with excluded "Prototype.js" (prototype extension).

#### 3. Two Berkeley students break Netscape's random numbers, and Netscape invents the bug bounty (September to October 1995)
- **Two sentences.** Ian Goldberg and David Wagner wanted to know how Netscape Navigator chose its SSL keys, but Netscape would not say, so they decompiled the binary by hand, found the seed was the clock plus two process IDs, and posted working attack code to a mailing list on a Sunday night. The reader learns that the secret behind early web commerce could be guessed in 25 seconds, and that three weeks later Netscape announced the "Bugs Bounty".
- The post: From iang@CS.Berkeley.EDU, dated 18 Sep 1995 00:46:51 -0400, subject "Netscape SSL implementation cracked!". It says the seed was "fairly trivial to guess by someone with an account on the machine running netscape", and the code header reads "netscape seeds the random number generator it uses to produce challenge-data and master keys with a combination of the time in seconds and microseconds, the pid and the ppid." The code describes its job as "Break netscape's shoddy implementation of SSL on some platforms". — [Goldberg's post, archived by Wagner](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~daw/my-posts/netscape-cracked-0)
- The write-up: Goldberg and Wagner, "Randomness and the Netscape Browser", Dr. Dobb's Journal, January 1996. They "resorted to the tedious task of reverse-engineering Netscape's algorithm by manually decompiling their executable program" (Netscape 1.1 for Solaris). Searching the million microsecond values "takes about 25 seconds on an HP 712/80". "Netscape did not encourage outside auditing or peer review of its software." RSA's Jim Bidzos had offered to review Netscape's security before release and was declined; afterwards Bidzos said, "They're asking us to review it this time." — [Dr. Dobb's article, author copy](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~daw/papers/ddj-netscape.html)
- Wagner's follow-up post of 20 September 1995 refers to a front-page New York Times article that day; the San Francisco Chronicle profiled the pair on 20 Sept 1995, pp B1, B2. — [Wagner's post](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~daw/my-posts/netscape-cracked); [Chronicle clipping](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~daw/press/iang/ian1.html)
- Netscape announced the "Bugs Bounty" for the Navigator 2.0 beta on 10 October 1995, with cash for significant security bugs and merchandise for others; later accounts credit support engineer Jarrett Ridlinghafer with the idea. — [Dark Reading](https://www.darkreading.com/vulnerabilities---threats/the-bug-bounty-model-21-years-and-counting/a/d-id/1327752); [press release as posted to cypherpunks](https://cypherpunks.venona.com/date/1995/10/msg01169.html) (server returned an error when I tried to open it; found via search only)
- **How well told.** A staple of cryptography courses and of bug-bounty history listicles, but the two halves are rarely joined, and web developers mostly do not know either. The Dr. Dobb's article is the authors' own account.
- **Weak points.** The causal link from the September break to the October bounty is my inference; no source I opened states it. The Ridlinghafer story (budget of $50,000, an engineering VP overruled) traces to Wikipedia-style summaries and is single-sourced. The NYT byline is unconfirmed. Do not assume pronouns for anyone. An earlier brute-force of a 40-bit key (the Dr. Dobb's piece mentions "as few as 30 hours") is a separate episode and needs its own source if used.

#### 4. favicon.ico: the late check-in that made every server on earth log a 404 (1998 to 1999)
- **Two sentences.** Bharat Shyam, a senior developer on Internet Explorer 5, wanted icons next to Favorites, but the schedule had closed to new features, so late one evening Shyam showed a build to junior program manager Ray Sun, asked "this is good, right? Check it in?", and got a yes. The reader learns that a file every site now serves was approved by the wrong person on purpose, that Sun was reprimanded for it, and that Yahoo found the feature in its logs before it had an icon.
- Ray Sun's first-person post, 2 September 2013: "In 1998, I was a junior Program Manager on the Internet Explorer team at Microsoft."; "Bharat said, 'this is good, right? Check it in?'"; "you just had to put a file named favicon.ico at the root of your IIS Server, and you were good to go!"; the manager "said Bharat had taken advantage of me"; "I promised to do better."; "But now I look back & realize that we did the right thing."; "Seriously, how risky was this feature?" Sun later told Michael Radwin at Yahoo, who had seen odd request traffic: "He was so excited that he slammed a favicon.ico onto the server". — [Ray Sun, "Inventing Favicon.ico"](https://ruthlessray.wordpress.com/2013/09/02/inventing-favicon-ico/)
- Jay Hoffmann's retelling (24 July 2017) adds that IE5 shipped March 1999, that the icon first appeared only in Favorites, so a site owner could count bookmarkers from requests for the file, and that standards bodies later answered with `<link rel>`, with the meaningless word `shortcut` preserved for old IE. — [The History of the Web, "How We Got the Favicon"](https://thehistoryoftheweb.com/?p=767)
- **How well told.** Told once by Sun, retold by The History of the Web and Wikipedia. Short and charming; most developers have not met it.
- **Weak points.** Single source for the anecdote (Sun); Shyam's own account is missing. It may be too slight for 2,200 words unless widened into "files browsers request without asking" (favicon.ico, robots.txt is excluded, `/apple-touch-icon.png`, `.well-known`), with the bookmark-counting privacy leak and the W3C's "don't squat on the root" objection as the trouble. The W3C objection and the first `rel="icon"` text need primary sources (not opened). Reader comments on Sun's post raise the privacy and free-hosting problems, which is evidence people noticed but not of when.

#### 5. Internet Exploder: the signed ActiveX control that turned your PC off (June to October 1996)
- **Two sentences.** Fred McLain, a Seattle-area programmer, wanted to show that Microsoft's Authenticode proved who wrote a control and nothing about what it did, but warnings were not landing, so McLain wrote a control that shut down Windows 95 from a web page, bought a VeriSign publisher certificate and signed it. The reader learns that this prank produced, per the O'Reilly account, the first certificate VeriSign ever revoked against its holder's will, and that revocation did not stop the control running.
- Microsoft's own technote ("What Is the Exploder Control and How Does It Relate to Authenticode?", updated 3 September 1996): McLain posted the control on 17 June 1996; "When downloaded via the Internet to a PC that has a power conservation BIOS, this control shuts down" Windows 95 and powers the machine off; "As of August 23" McLain had signed it with an Individual Software Publisher Digital ID from VeriSign. Microsoft's position: "Authenticode doesn't verify that software is free of bugs and malicious intent." and "By default, Internet Explorer 3.0 will not download unsigned code." — [MSDN technote mirror](https://techshelps.github.io/MSDN/TECHART/html/msdn_explode.htm)
- Garfinkel and Spafford, "Web Security and Commerce" (O'Reilly, 1997), as surfaced in search: after "several weeks of back-and-forth arguments, VeriSign revoked McLain's software publisher's certificate. It was the first digital certificate ever revoked by VeriSign without the permission of the certificate holder"; the revocation did little for IE 3.0 users because the signed control still ran; IE 3.0.1 followed. — [O'Reilly, ch. 4 "ActiveX and Authenticode"](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/web-security-and/1565922697/ch04s03.html) (page returned 403 to my fetch; text seen in search results only)
- A September 1996 cypherpunks post reports VeriSign asking McLain to take the control down. — [cypherpunks archive](https://cypherpunks.venona.com/date/1996/09/msg02034.html) (server error on fetch; search result only)
- **How well told.** A chapter in a 1997 security book and a paragraph in ActiveX histories. No modern long-form retelling found. Strong fit for the site: an artifact, a named person, a turn, and a direct line to later code-signing and extension-store debates.
- **Weak points.** McLain's own pages (halcyon.com/mclain/ActiveX) survive only in the Wayback Machine, which my tools could not open; his motive and words are therefore UNVERIFIED and essential. Exact revocation date not confirmed. The "first revoked certificate" claim is single-source (O'Reilly). Check whether the Princeton Java-security work of the same year belongs as the contrast (not researched).

#### 6. Vendor prefixes: the safety valve that became the standard (February 2012 to September 2016)
- **Two sentences.** The CSS Working Group wanted prefixes like `-webkit-` to keep experiments out of the standard, but mobile sites shipped the experiments as production code for iPhone and Android only, so in February 2012 Microsoft, Mozilla and Opera told the group they would implement WebKit's prefixes themselves. The reader learns that co-chair Daniel Glazman's all-caps appeal failed, Opera aliased the properties within eleven weeks, and by 2016 `-webkit-` properties were written into a WHATWG standard that Firefox implements.
- Daniel Glazman, CSS WG co-chair, 9 February 2012, "CALL FOR ACTION: THE OPEN WEB NEEDS YOU *NOW*": "other browsers will start supporting/implementing themselves the `-webkit-*` prefix"; this would "turn a market share into a _de facto_ standard"; vendors said they had "_no other option_"; he asked authors to stop WebKit-only sites "NOW!". — [Glazblog](https://www.glazman.org/weblog/dotclear/index.php?post/2012/02/09/CALL-FOR-ACTION:-THE-OPEN-WEB-NEEDS-YOU-NOW)
- Bruce Lawson, Opera, 27 April 2012: an Opera Mobile Emulator build aliasing `-webkit-` `box-shadow`, `transform`, `border-radius`, `transition`, `-webkit-linear-gradient` and the `webkitTransitionEnd` event, because "many authors of (especially mobile) sites only use -webkit- prefixed CSS"; chosen from the Alexa top 10,000 by "a subjective judgment". — [Dev.Opera backup](https://raw.githack.com/operasoftware/devopera-static-backup/master/http/dev.opera.com/articles/view/opera-mobile-emulator-experimental-webkit-prefix-support/index.html)
- Mike Taylor and Justin Crawford, Mozilla Hacks, 20 September 2016: Firefox 49 supports features that are "non-standard, vendor-specific, and quite prevalent" and brings Gecko "up to date with the WHATWG Compatibility Standard", which Taylor edits. — [Mozilla Hacks](https://hacks.mozilla.org/2016/09/firefox-49-fixes-sites-designed-with-webkit-in-mind-and-more/)
- Contemporary reaction pieces exist from Adrian Roselli (Feb 2012) and net magazine. — [Adrian Roselli](https://adrianroselli.com/2012/02/browser-makers-caving-to-vendor-prefix.html)
- **How well told.** Heavily blogged in spring 2012 (Glazman, Lawson, Meyer, PPK, Roselli) but I found no single piece that carries it from the 2012 meeting to the 2016 Compatibility Standard, and the ending (Opera abandoning its own engine for WebKit in February 2013, then Blink) makes it a story. It fits the "fix that caused the next problem" form exactly.
- **Weak points.** I did not open the CSS WG minutes of the February 2012 face-to-face (the Tantek Çelik and Florian Rivoal statements usually quoted from them are UNVERIFIED), nor Eric Meyer's A List Apart interview with Çelik, nor the Blink "no new prefixes" policy. Opera's February 2013 engine switch is from memory, UNVERIFIED. Audience familiarity is medium: CSS people know the opening, few know the end.

#### 7. Node's deleted promises and the insult that named Fantasy Land (February 2010 to April 2013)
- **Two sentences.** Ryan Dahl wanted Node's core small, but its early API returned promises that everyone wanted to work differently, so in February 2010 Dahl deleted them in favour of a callback as the last argument, and three years later the people rebuilding promises for the language had a one-day fight about what a promise is. The reader learns that Node had promises before it had "callback hell", and that the Fantasy Land specification takes its name from a dismissal in a GitHub issue.
- Node v0.1.30 release, 22 February 2010, Ryan Dahl: "Promises removed." with the new form `fs.readdir("/usr", function (err, files) {`; follow-up: "If you need promises for legacy code, then include this file in your application:" (a gist). — [nodejs Google Group, "node-v0.1.30"](https://groups.google.com/g/nodejs/c/5sxvBM0N3xQ)
- 20 February 2010, thread "First steps in removing promises": Rasmus Andersson reports starting the removal ("the code is now much cleaner just by these simple changes."); Dahl replies "Sorry, I've already done it."; Kris Kowal (later author of Q) asks about return values. — [nodejs Google Group](https://groups.google.com/g/nodejs/c/RvNoQtoWyZA)
- 10 April 2013, promises-aplus/promises-spec issue #94, "Incorporate monads and category theory", opened by paulmillr on the basis of Brian McKenna's critique. Domenic Denicola: "Yeah this is really not happening." and the proposal "totally ignores reality in favor of typed-language fantasy land." Brian Cavalier: "However, changing `then` simply is not an option." McKenna (puffnfresh): "I'm going to work on a separate specification." The issue was closed the same day and people were still commenting on it in 2021, 2022 and 2024. — [promises-spec issue 94](https://github.com/promises-aplus/promises-spec/issues/94)
- **How well told.** The Fantasy Land naming is known among functional JavaScript people; the Node removal is cited in "history of async" talks. I found no piece joining the two through named people (Kowal appears in both threads).
- **Weak points.** Dahl's actual reasons are in two linked messages I did not open; the search summary's paraphrase (many people wanted promises "but different in some way") is UNVERIFIED wording. A later howtonode article misdates the removal to the 0.2 era. The September 2013 TC39 resolution of the promise design is not researched. Risk of overlap with the excluded "Node.js at JSConf EU", "CommonJS" and "npm's origins" essays; this one would need to stay on the promise thread only. The 2013 thread is heated: quote sparingly and fairly, since Denicola and McKenna are both working developers.

#### 8. PNG in ten weeks, and the animation format that never arrived (December 1994 to 2007)
- **Two sentences.** Thomas Boutell and a few dozen strangers on Usenet wanted a free replacement for GIF after Unisys and CompuServe announced royalties over the holidays, and they froze a better format in about ten weeks, but they left animation for later, so GIF kept the one job that mattered and is still doing it. The reader learns the dates, the names behind each PNG feature, and that Mozilla removed PNG's animated sibling in 2003 against its most-voted bug ever.
- Greg Roelofs's history: the Unisys/CompuServe agreement was "quietly announced on 28 December 1994, right in the middle of the Christmas holidays." CompuServe's Tim Oren: "The patent was brought to our attention, much to our displeasure." Boutell posted the first draft, "PBF" (Portable Bitmap Format), on 4 January 1995 to comp.graphics, comp.compression and comp.infosystems.www.providers; Oliver Fromme proposed the name PNG on 6 January; Adam Costello's Adam7 interlacing arrived 2 February; CompuServe named PNG as GIF's successor on 7 February; Draft 9 was "officially frozen" in early March 1995; W3C Recommendation 1 October 1996; RFC 2083 on 15 January 1997. Boutell is described as the "benevolent dictator". Roelofs on browsers: "Discerning readers will note the conspicuous absence of Netscape Navigator." On MNG, it "may never be perceived as anything more than PNG's response to GIF animations." — [Roelofs, "History of the Portable Network Graphics (PNG) Format"](http://www.libpng.org/pub/png/pnghist.html)
- MNG developers' log, 2003: on 3 June 2003 MNG/JNG support was removed from the Mozilla trunk by Tim "tor" Rowley and Stuart "pavlov" Parmenter (bug 195280) for "not very well understood reasons"; "The user community is not amused." By 12 June 2003 the restore bug, 18574, passed 400 votes, Mozilla's most-voted open bug: "The user community has spoken!" — [MNG news 2003](https://libpng.org/pub/mng/mng2003.html)
- Search results (not opened) say the PNG Development Group voted APNG down on 20 April 2007 ("VOTE FAILED: APNG 20070405a") after Mozilla had put APNG into Firefox 3 trunk builds on 23 March 2007. — [Bugzilla APNG tracking](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=apng) (pointer only, UNVERIFIED)
- **How well told.** The PNG birth is well told by Roelofs (a participant) and summarised on Wikipedia; this audience half-knows it. The MNG removal and the APNG vote are Bugzilla folklore that I have not seen told as a story with the PNG founding attached.
- **Weak points.** Roelofs's history does not contain the expansion "PNG's Not GIF" or "Burn All GIFs Day" (1999); both need other sources. The APNG vote details and the eventual closing of bug 18574 are UNVERIFIED. The original Usenet posts should be opened (Google Groups) before quoting Boutell. The stated reasons for removing MNG (often given as code size) are not in the page I opened.

#### 9. The GeoCities "Haunting" (25 June to early July 1999)
- **Two sentences.** Yahoo, having just bought GeoCities, wanted one set of terms across its properties, but the terms it posted on 25 June 1999 claimed a perpetual licence to everything on members' pages and locked them out of editing until they agreed, so homesteaders drained their own pages to grey, posted the terms in place of their content, and got the clause rewritten within a week. The reader learns that the first mass revolt against a platform's terms of service was won by people with free home pages, in about six days.
- InternetNews, 1 July 1999: the terms posted 25 June let Yahoo use member content "in any way it wished" with "irrevocable rights to the work"; members could not change or remove sites without accepting; Jim Townsend of SitePowerUP.com hosted the "Boycott Yahoo! Home Page" (more than 300,000 page views); late Wednesday (30 June by the article's dating) Yahoo posted terms saying it "does not own or control content that is posted on GeoCities sites."; Townsend called that "only a good first step" and "They have not addressed multiple deficiencies"; a Yahoo spokesman said "there were never any plans for the company to use sites in books". — [InternetNews](https://www.internetnews.com/developer/yahoo-revises-geocities-terms-many-call-for-more)
- Search results (not opened): Wired News "Yahoo: Keep Your Homestead" (June 1999) quotes the new terms as "Yahoo does not own content you submit"; the Irish Times headline was "Yahoo surrenders to its homesteaders"; an academic paper is titled "The Haunting of GeoCities and the Politics of Access Control on the Early Web"; the GeoCities Research Institute blog describes users replacing "blinking GIFs with excerpts of the offending Terms of Service". — [Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/news/yahoo-surrenders-to-its-homesteaders-1.205515); [GeoCities Research Institute](https://blog.geocities.institute/?p=6144); [paper record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-haunting-of-geocities-and-the-politics-of-access-control-on-t-2/)
- **How well told.** One academic article and scattered 1999 news items. Almost never mentioned in GeoCities nostalgia, which skips from 1998 to the 2009 shutdown.
- **Weak points.** I could not open Wired (blocked) or the Wayback Machine, so no haunted page was seen first-hand; an essay needs at least one archived example and the exact clause. Sources differ on whether the reversal took about a week or about ten days and on how complete it was (a further revision around 7 July). Protagonists beyond Townsend are unnamed. More culture than JavaScript; topic would be `people`.

#### 10. Erwise: the browser Finland could not afford (1991 to 1992)
- **Two sentences.** Four Helsinki University of Technology students wanted to finish a course project and built a graphical, multi-window web browser for X a year before Mosaic, but Finland was in a depression with no venture capital and nobody would pay them to continue, so they graduated and the code stopped. The reader learns that features Mosaic users waited for were shipped in April 1992 by students whose own country could not fund a job to maintain them.
- OSnews (Thom Holwerda, 3 March 2009), summarising Juha-Pekka Tikka's Xconomy interview: developers Kim Nyberg, Kari Sydänmaanlakka, Teemu Rantanen and Kati Borgers; instructor Ari Lemmke offered the browser "or something Linux-related"; finished April 1992; it could search for a word across loaded pages and open links in new windows. Nyberg: "At first they did a lot of things quite wrong with Mosaic. There were problems we had already solved," and (speaker not named in the summary) "We could not have created a business around Erwise in Finland then." — [OSnews](https://www.osnews.com/story/21076/the-worlds-first-graphical-browser-erwise/)
- Search summaries of Wikipedia and Xconomy say Tim Berners-Lee travelled to Finland to encourage the group to continue, that first release was 15 April 1992, and that Berners-Lee's guess at the name ("OTH + Erwise = Otherwise") differs from the students' own explanation (the course was "OHT"). — [Wikipedia pointer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwise); [Xconomy original](https://legacy.xconomy.com/2009/03/03/the-greatest-internet-pioneers-you-never-heard-of-the-story-of-erwise-and-four-finns-who-showed-the-way-to-the-web-browser/) (503 on fetch, twice)
- **How well told.** One good 2009 magazine piece (Xconomy), echoed by OSnews, Slashdot and Cybercultural's 1992 chapter. Unknown to most developers, and the Linux connection (Lemmke is the person usually credited with naming Linux's FTP directory; UNVERIFIED here) is a dot few have joined.
- **Weak points.** The primary article would not load, so the Berners-Lee visit, its date and what was proposed are UNVERIFIED. Sources name the fourth student as Kati Borgers or Kati Suominen; do not guess which is current. Start date is given as 1991 or March 1992. "First graphical browser" is disputed (WorldWideWeb on NeXT was graphical) and should be claimed only as "first for X". It is pre-JavaScript and the ending is quiet; it may work better as a section in a wider "browsers before Mosaic" essay, and ViolaWWW is excluded, so keep clear of that.

#### 11. The day the web went black (8 February 1996)
- **Two sentences.** Shabbir Safdar of Voters Telecommunications Watch wanted Congress to see that the people who built web pages opposed the Communications Decency Act, but it passed on 1 February 1996 anyway, so Safdar emailed webmasters asking them to turn their backgrounds black for 48 hours from the moment Clinton signed. The reader learns that the 2012 SOPA blackout had a 1996 original done with one `BGCOLOR` attribute, that Yahoo and Netscape joined, and that the only part of the CDA left standing is Section 230.
- EFF (Parker Higgins, 23 February 2016): Congress passed the CDA on 1 February 1996; Clinton signed on 8 February; sites went black for the following 48 hours; estimated at more than 5% of sites then online; a protester's phrase, "a thousand points of darkness"; Rep. Jerrold Nadler blackened his own site and called the law "the cyberspace equivalent of book burning."; some pages read "This is what censorship looks like."; EFF's Blue Ribbon page became the 4th most linked-to site; on the ribbon, "not everyone has a color monitor, so the verbal name of the campaign is important to pass around." Most of the CDA was later struck down, Section 230 excepted. — [EFF Deeplinks](https://www.eff.org/node/90527)
- The History of the Web: organiser Shabbir J. Safdar founded VTW and emailed developers and webmasters; over 1,500 sites took part, including Netscape and Yahoo; it calls the day "Black Thursday". Its sources include Peter H. Lewis in the New York Times (Feb 1996) and Wired's "Remembering the Great Web Blackout". — [The History of the Web, "Great Web Blackout"](https://thehistoryoftheweb.com/great-web-blackout/)
- A 15 February 1996 newspaper report is headlined "Hundreds More Internet Sites Go Dark In Protest". — [Spokesman-Review](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/1996/feb/15/hundreds-more-internet-sites-go-dark-in-protest/) (headline from search only)
- **How well told.** Told by EFF on anniversaries and by The History of the Web; resurfaced briefly in 2012. Readers aged 40 to 60 may dimly recall the blue ribbon GIF without the story.
- **Weak points.** Participation figures differ (1,500 sites versus "5% of sites"). Dave Winer's "24 Hours of Democracy" essay project (22 February 1996), which I expected to connect, did not appear in any source I opened and is UNVERIFIED. The original VTW alert, with its instructions to webmasters, is the artifact the essay needs and I did not find it. Court dates (the June 1996 district ruling and June 1997 Supreme Court decision) need a primary citation. Policy-heavy; needs the webmaster's-eye view to fit this collection, and "the webmaster job" is an excluded (existing) essay to link to, not repeat.

#### 12. The DOCTYPE switch: a comment at the top of the file that changes the layout engine (1998 to 2001)
- **Two sentences.** Tantek Çelik, building Internet Explorer 5 for the Mac, wanted to implement the CSS box model correctly, but the web was built for the wrong one, so the team shipped two engines' worth of behaviour and let the page's DOCTYPE choose, an idea Çelik credits to Todd Fahrner. The reader learns why `<!DOCTYPE html>` exists at all, that the idea's owner and its implementer are different people, and that there was an argument about who copied whom.
- Tantek Çelik on www-html, 26 June 2003: "I'm certainly not taking credit for the "idea" of DOCTYPE switching" (crediting Todd Fahrner); IE5/Mac "shipped the first DOCTYPE switch"; "we certainly had beta versions shipped to beta testers in early to mid 1999 with it"; "IE5/Mac shipped in March of 2000."; "I'm claiming that Mozilla copied the DOCTYPE switch in IE5/Mac". — [www-html archive](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-html/2003Jun/0131.html)
- Pointers not opened: Çelik's own notes page; the W3C HTML WG wiki on doctype switching (IE6 for Windows, August 2001); Henri Sivonen's "Activating Browser Modes with Doctype" and "Almost Precedent"; The History of the Web's milestone page. — [tantek.com notes](https://tantek.com/notes/doctypeswitching.html); [W3C wiki](https://www.w3.org/html/wg/wiki/DoctypeSwitching); [hsivonen.fi](https://hsivonen.fi/doctype/); [History of the Web](https://thehistoryoftheweb.com/milestones/doctype-switching-box-model-hack/)
- **How well told.** The mechanism is thoroughly documented (Sivonen); the people and the priority dispute are not. Eric Meyer's and Aaron Gustafson's A List Apart pieces of 2008 cover the sequel.
- **Weak points.** Weakest on trouble and turn of the twelve: it is closer to an origin story. Fahrner's 1998 proposal has not been located in a primary source. It sits beside the excluded "IE8 version targeting" essay and must not duplicate it; it may be better as that essay's "Setup" than as its own piece. Lowest priority.

### Inferences
- The best candidates share a shape: one person, one small artifact, one deliberate act that others had to live with (Fielding's ten lines, Shyam's check-in, McLain's signed control, Goldberg's post). These need the least rescue.
- Candidates 2 and 6 are the same underlying story at two layers (deployed code that nobody will update outvotes the standards body). They should not both run close together; 2 is the JavaScript one and has the better artifacts.
- Candidates 9, 10 and 11 are culture and platform stories with little JavaScript; they suit the `people` topic and should be judged against how many such essays the collection wants.
- Candidate 12 is probably a parked topic or a section of an existing essay, not a new essay.

### Gaps
- The Wayback Machine, wired.com, the O'Reilly library and the venona cypherpunks mirror could not be opened from this environment, which cost the primary sources for candidates 5, 9 and part of 3. An author will need them before writing.
- I did not get to several seams named in the brief: www-talk and www-style archives, the HOPL IV paper, Computer History Museum oral histories and US v. Microsoft exhibits. No candidate here is drawn from them, so they remain unmined rather than exhausted.
- Leads considered but not researched, so UNVERIFIED and listed only as pointers: British Telecom's hyperlink patent suit against Prodigy (2000 to 2002); Microsoft's "Smart Tags" in the IE6 beta (2001); the web fonts gap between EOT/TrueDoc (1997) and WOFF (2009); Web SQL Database's abandonment for IndexedDB (2010); the `<picture>` versus `srcset` fight and the crowdfunded Blink implementation (2012 to 2014); David Siegel's spacer GIF and later recantation; the IE9 SunSpider `math-cordic` dead-code accusation (2010); undetectable `document.all`; the Gopher licensing announcement of 1993; SLAC's first North American web server; Mitchell Baker continuing at mozilla.org as a volunteer after an AOL layoff.

## For each candidate, what would a developer who was working at the time be surprised to learn?

### Takeaway
Each candidate has at least one documented surprise; the best are small, checkable and slightly absurd.

### Cited Findings
- DNT: the standard's co-author, not an ad company, was the one who switched the signal off, in a config file, with a commit message that reads like a protest sign. — [apache/httpd commit](https://github.com/apache/httpd/commit/a381ff35fa4d50a5f7b9f64300dfd98859dee8d0)
- `includes`: Firefox shipped `String.prototype.contains` from version 18 until the rename landed for version 40, and the array break was caused by enumerability, not by a naming clash as usually said. — [Bugzilla 1102219](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1102219); [Bugzilla 1075059](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1075059)
- Netscape RNG: the unknown part of the seed could be searched in "about 25 seconds on an HP 712/80", and RSA's offer to review the code beforehand had been declined. — [Dr. Dobb's article](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~daw/papers/ddj-netscape.html)
- favicon: the program manager who approved it was scolded, and Yahoo noticed the feature as unexplained requests in its logs. — [Ray Sun](https://ruthlessray.wordpress.com/2013/09/02/inventing-favicon-ico/)
- Exploder: Microsoft answered with a technote conceding that "Authenticode doesn't verify that software is free of bugs and malicious intent." — [MSDN technote mirror](https://techshelps.github.io/MSDN/TECHART/html/msdn_explode.htm)
- Prefixes: Opera chose which WebKit properties to copy by "a subjective judgment" over the Alexa top 10,000, and the list included a JavaScript event name, `webkitTransitionEnd`. — [Dev.Opera backup](https://raw.githack.com/operasoftware/devopera-static-backup/master/http/dev.opera.com/articles/view/opera-mobile-emulator-experimental-webkit-prefix-support/index.html)
- Promises: Node had them and deleted them in release 0.1.30; "fantasy land" was meant as a put-down. — [nodejs group](https://groups.google.com/g/nodejs/c/5sxvBM0N3xQ); [promises-spec issue 94](https://github.com/promises-aplus/promises-spec/issues/94)
- PNG: the format was first posted as "PBF", the name PNG came two days later from Oliver Fromme, and the whole specification was frozen in about ten weeks. — [Roelofs](http://www.libpng.org/pub/png/pnghist.html)
- MNG: the bug asking Mozilla to restore it passed 400 votes within nine days of the removal. — [MNG news 2003](https://libpng.org/pub/mng/mng2003.html)
- GeoCities: members could not even delete their pages without first accepting the terms they objected to. — [InternetNews](https://www.internetnews.com/developer/yahoo-revises-geocities-terms-many-call-for-more)
- Erwise: it opened links in new windows and searched across loaded pages in April 1992; Nyberg says of Mosaic, "There were problems we had already solved,". — [OSnews](https://www.osnews.com/story/21076/the-worlds-first-graphical-browser-erwise/)
- 1996 blackout: a sitting member of Congress, Jerrold Nadler, blacked out an official site in protest of a law Congress had just passed. — [EFF Deeplinks](https://www.eff.org/node/90527)
- DOCTYPE switch: the person who shipped it publicly declined credit for the idea and named Todd Fahrner. — [www-html, June 2003](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-html/2003Jun/0131.html)
- A spare fact for any CSS essay: Netscape's JavaScript-Based Style Sheets proposal (W3C submission dated 19 and 22 August 1996) lists Lou Montulli, Brendan Eich, Scott Furman, Donna Converse and Troy Chevalier as authors and styles a heading with `tags.H1.color = "blue"`. — [W3C submission](https://www.w3.org/Submission/1996/1/WD-jsss-960822)

### Inferences
- For this audience the JavaScript-facing surprises (candidates 2 and 7) and the server-config one (candidate 1) will land hardest, because readers can check them against code they have typed.
- JSSS versus CSS was named in the brief as an example; I confirmed only the submission's authors, dates and syntax. It has protagonists (Håkon Wium Lie and Bert Bos on one side, the Netscape authors on the other) but I did not establish a turn or an ending from primary sources, so it is a lead, not a vetted candidate.
