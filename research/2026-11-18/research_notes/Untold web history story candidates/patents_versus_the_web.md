# Patents versus the web: (A) Pei-Yuan Wei, ViolaWWW and Eolas; (B) the W3C patent policy fight, 2001 to 2004

Research date: 2026-11-18. Every quotation below was copied from a page I opened. Access notes: web.archive.org, arstechnica.com, viola.org and xcf.berkeley.edu were unreachable from this environment, so Wei's own "Personal Comments on Eolas vs Microsoft" page and Mullin's Ars Technica originals were NOT read directly (two Mullin pieces were read on wired.com, one via a mailing-list repost). Third-party sources (Wired, Wikipedia, list posts) call Wei "he"; I found no self-stated pronouns, so these notes use the name or "they".

## 1. Viola/Eolas: who, what, when, and how it ended

### Takeaway
This is a complete story with a named protagonist (Pei-Yuan Wei), an antagonist (Michael Doyle / Eolas, with the University of California as co-plaintiff), a documented public confrontation on www-talk on 21 August 1995 (three years before the patent issued), two trials nine years apart that turned on the same 7 May 1993 demo to two Sun engineers, and a clean ending (jury invalidation 9 Feb 2012, affirmed 22/23 July 2013). The primary record is unusually rich and online: the 1995 list thread, the 2005 Federal Circuit opinion, W3C's 2003 documents, and courtroom reporting by Joe Mullin.

### Cited Findings

**Wei and Viola**
- Tim Berners-Lee's own FAQ: "Pei Wei, student at U.C. Berkeley ... then wrote ViolaWWW for unix, based on his Viola language"; "Pei Wei has passed though history unnoticed among others whose work is not mentioned in the histories, even though there was a year or so when Viola was the best way to browse the web, was the engine driving the installation of new servers, and the recommended browser at CERN". Also: "Viola, for example, was more advanced in many ways, with downloaded applets and animations way back then - very like HotJava was later." — [Berners-Lee FAQ](https://www.w3.org/People/Berners-Lee/FAQ.html)
- Same FAQ on Mosaic's debt: "As I understand it, Marc Andreessen at NCSA was shown ViolaWWW by a colleague (David Thompson?) at NCSA." — [Berners-Lee FAQ](https://www.w3.org/People/Berners-Lee/FAQ.html)
- W3C's historical status page: "Author: Pei Wei", "Demod at JENC3 (10 May 1992)", "Binaries available for sun4, decstation, SGI (15-Jul-92). Now ('94) available for Motif or non-Motif X." — [W3C History: Status of ViolaWWW](https://www.w3.org/History/1995/WWW/Viola/)
- Wikipedia (pointer only, not verified against primaries): Wei first met HyperCard in 1989, wrote Viola for X in 1990, released Viola 0.8 in 1991, ViolaWWW released 9 March 1992; "first browser to support scripting and stylesheets"; embedded scriptable objects, tables, client-side document insertion; never ran on Windows; development later funded by O'Reilly. — [Wikipedia: ViolaWWW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ViolaWWW)
- December 1991 email from Wei to Berners-Lee, shown to the 2012 jury: "One thing I'd like to do soon, if I have time, is to teach the parser about Viola object descriptions and basically embed Viola objects (GUIs and programmability) into HTML files." — [Wired, Mullin, 8 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/tim-berners-lee-patent/)
- Wei in 1995, on what Viola was for: "even the early Viola (started around 88, relased 1991) had a viola-app net transfer tool (the idea is to have something like a Hypercard like environment on the scale of the net)." Note "started around 88" versus Wikipedia's 1989/1990. — [www-talk, Wei, 21 Aug 1995](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-talk/1995JulAug/0446.html)

**The May 1993 demo (the hinge of both trials)**
- Federal Circuit findings of fact: "Wei testified at trial that he had written code for Viola so that the browser could handle embedded automatically-launched interactive programs in May 1993"; "Microsoft corroborated existence of this code at trial through a computer demonstration of an archived file, dated May 12, 1993. The parties refer to this version of Viola as DX34. Wei demonstrated DX34 to two Sun Microsystems engineers on May 7, 1993." — [Eolas v. Microsoft, 399 F.3d 1325 (Fed. Cir. 2 Mar 2005)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html)
- Same opinion: Wei "only twenty-four days later (on May 31, 1993) posted DX37 on a publicly-accessible Internet site and notified a Sun Microsystems engineer that DX37 was available for downloading"; "the Sun engineers informed Wei that they intended to share information gleaned from Wei with other people at Sun"; "Wei made no attempt to maintain confidentiality". — [399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html)
- In the Chicago courtroom the later build would not run: "Apparently due to a bug in the program, Wei could not make the DX37 version function in the courtroom in conjunction with the Internet." — [399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html)
- Wei's own 1995 account (quoting an earlier post to the VRML list): "Definitely by May 8, 1993 we had demonstrated that plotting demo (the very one shown in the viola paper) to visitors from a certain computer manufacturer... This demo was memorable because someone and I at ORA had lost sleep the night before the meeting, in order to cook up that particular plotting demo :) We had to show something cool." Date differs by one day from the court's May 7. — [www-talk, Wei, 21 Aug 1995](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-talk/1995JulAug/0446.html)
- 2012 testimony: Wei and Scott Silvey (both UC Berkeley students in the 1990s) "combined Wei's Viola browser with a program called V-Plot that allowed users to rotate an image of an airplane." — [Wired, Mullin, 9 Feb 2012 (deliberation story)](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/texas-jury/)
- Second public showing: Simon Spero corrected Wei's "around August 93": "World Wide Web Wizards WOrkshop was at the ORA offices in Cambridge starting July 28th (I remember, cos my birthday was the 27th)". — [www-talk, Spero, 21 Aug 1995](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-talk/1995JulAug/0458.html)

**Doyle, Eolas and the patent**
- Doyle's team (Doyle, David Martin, Cheong Ang) testified in 2012 that they conceived the idea at UCSF by September 1993 and began demonstrating in November 1993; the system was the "Visible Embryo Project". Doyle said he owned 40 percent of Eolas, Martin about 8 percent. UC's cut was "reported some years ago to be 25 percent". — [Wired, Mullin, 9 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/texas-jury/)
- Doyle on the stand: "We were excited to use Mosaic and Viola as building blocks". Eric Bina testified the UCSF team "had added only 334 lines of code added to about 18,000 lines total" of Mosaic. — [Wired, Mullin, 9 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/texas-jury/)
- US 5,838,906, "Distributed hypermedia method for automatically invoking external application providing interaction and display of embedded objects within a hypermedia document"; "filed in October 1994 and granted in November 1998. The patent is owned by the University of California with licensing handled through Eolas Technologies." — [W3C FAQ on US Patent 5,838,906](https://www.w3.org/2003/09/public-faq.html). Exact dates per the court and Wikipedia: filed 17 Oct 1994, granted 17 Nov 1998. — [399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html); [Wikipedia: Eolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eolas)
- Doyle knew of Viola before filing. Court: "On August 31, 1994, Doyle issued a press release to an e-mail list indicating that researchers at the University of California had 'created software for embedding interactive program objects within hypermedia documents.' That same day, Wei contacted Doyle via e-mail"; "Doyle downloaded and read the paper. In a later email exchange, Doyle attempted to get Wei to concede that he was not the first to invent." In 1998 Doyle "made a folder labeled 'Viola stuff.'"; "Although available on the Internet, Doyle never obtained the Viola code." Doyle "did not disclose any information regarding that reference" to the PTO. — [399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html)
- The public 1995 confrontation, all on 21 Aug 1995, the day Eolas announced its exclusive licence from UC. Wei: "I sincerely hope this patent isn't going to stick, for the good of the web as a whole... And for the record, I just want to point out that the ``technology which enabled Web documents to contain fully-interactive "inline" program objects'' was existing in ViolaWWW and was *released* to the public, and in full source code form, even back in 1993". — [www-talk, Wei](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-talk/1995JulAug/0429.html)
- Doyle's reply 20 minutes later: "Pei, We've had this discussion before (last September, remember?). You admitted then that you did NOT release or publish anything like this before the Eolas demonstrations." (Doyle says "last September"; the court dates first contact to 31 Aug 1994.) — [www-talk, Doyle](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-talk/1995JulAug/0431.html)
- Wei's answer: "Please carefully re-read my letter to you... I said Viola was demonstrated in smaller settings, but before your demo." and "Wish I had kept better records and wrote papers about things as they happened!" — [www-talk, Wei](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-talk/1995JulAug/0446.html)
- Wei was posting from pei@gnn.com (O'Reilly's Global Network Navigator); the mail host in the Message-Id is literally named "ebay" (`199508211954.MAA26305@ebay`). — [www-talk, Wei](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-talk/1995JulAug/0429.html)

**Eolas v. Microsoft (N.D. Illinois)**
- Jury: "a royalty of $1.47 per unit of infringing product, which amounted to a total award of $520,562,280", plus a permanent injunction (stayed) and pre-judgment interest. — [399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html)
- Why the first jury never weighed Viola: the district court ruled as a matter of law that Wei "abandoned, suppressed or concealed" DX34 "because Wei disclosed that version only to the Sun engineers and then changed it", and "prevented Microsoft from presenting any evidence of DX34 to the jury". — [399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html)
- Appeal, 2 March 2005, Judge Rader writing: "Because creating an improved version of an invention does not in any sense abandon the original invention, the district court erroneously excluded DX34 as prior art." The court vacated and remanded for a new trial on anticipation, obviousness and inequitable conduct, while affirming claim construction and the foreign-sales (golden master disk) holding. — [399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html)
- Settlement: reported 27 Aug 2007 (Wikipedia, citing Seattle P-I), terms confidential; Mullin: "the case ultimately settled out for more than $100 million, with just over $30 million going to Eolas' co-plaintiff, the University of California". Wikipedia gives UC's share as $30.4 million. — [Mullin, Ars Technica 22 Jul 2013, as reposted](https://attrition.org/pipermail/infowarrior/2013-July/010311.html); [Wikipedia: Eolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eolas)
- **Disagreement on the verdict figure:** $520,562,280 (Federal Circuit); "$521 million" (Wikipedia/BBC); "$540 million jury verdict" (Mullin 2013); $565,894,868 with interest (seen only in a search-result summary of a law-firm page, not opened). Use the court's figure. — [399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html); [Mullin 2013 repost](https://attrition.org/pipermail/infowarrior/2013-July/010311.html)
- Wikipedia summary of the appeal ("infringement and damages were upheld") is misleading next to the opinion, which vacated the judgment. — [Wikipedia: Eolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eolas); [399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html)

**W3C and Berners-Lee, 2003**
- 19 Aug 2003: W3C ad hoc meeting "hosted by Macromedia ... in San Francisco"; "Microsoft has indicated to W3C that they will very soon be making changes to its Internet Explorer browser software"; "W3C has made efforts to contact the patent holder to determine their future intentions, but has not received any reply." Signed by Steven R Bratt, COO. — [W3C ad hoc meeting report](https://www.w3.org/2003/08/patent)
- 23 Sep 2003: W3C launched the HTML Patent Advisory Group, chaired by Alan Kotok. — [HTML PAG home page](https://www.w3.org/2003/09/pag)
- Letter from Berners-Lee to USPTO Director James E. Rogan, dated 28 Oct 2003, asking for a Director-initiated re-examination: "The '906 patent will cause cascades of incompatibility to ripple through the Web." It warned of "permanent impact on millions of Web pages that may have historical importance." Contact named: Daniel Weitzner. — [W3C letter to Rogan](https://www.w3.org/2003/10/27-rogan.html)
- W3C's FAQ calls it "an unprecedented request". Date discrepancies inside W3C's own pages: FAQ says prior art presented "On 23 October 2003" and letter "on 29 October"; the letter itself is dated 28 October and cites a Section 301 filing of 24 October. — [W3C FAQ](https://www.w3.org/2003/09/public-faq.html); [W3C letter](https://www.w3.org/2003/10/27-rogan.html)
- USPTO response: re-examination order "signed by Stephen G. Kunin, Deputy Commissioner of the US PTO was issued on 30 October 2003", citing "a substantial outcry from a widespread segment of the affected industry." "On 26 February 2004 the USPTO issued a preliminary report rejecting all claims in the patent." — [W3C FAQ](https://www.w3.org/2003/09/public-faq.html)
- Then the patent survived: USPTO upheld validity 27 Sep 2005 (Wikipedia, citing a UC press release; not opened). Mullin: "Eolas somehow emerged unscathed." — [Wikipedia: Eolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eolas); [Mullin 2013 repost](https://attrition.org/pipermail/infowarrior/2013-July/010311.html)

**"Click to activate" (the part developers felt)**
- Microsoft's IE team: "Back in April 2006, we made a change to how Internet Explorer handled embedded controls ... Some sites required users to 'click to activate' before they could interact with the control. Microsoft has now licensed the technologies from Eolas, removing the 'click to activate' requirement". Their table shows controls "Loaded Direct In HTML (<object>, <embed>, <applet>)" needed the click while "Controls Injected Via JavaScript" never did. Preview December 2007; rolled into the IE Cumulative Update in April 2008. — [IE blog, "IE Automatic Component Activation"](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/ie/ie-automatic-component-activation-changes-to-ie-activex-update)
- Wikipedia dates the change February 2006 and says Opera 9 (June 2006) did the same; the IE blog says April 2006 (likely optional update versus cumulative update; unverified). — [Wikipedia: Eolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eolas)

**Tyler, Texas, February 2012 (Eolas v. Adobe et al., E.D. Tex. 6:09-cv-446, Judge Leonard Davis)**
- Suit filed October 2009 over the '906 and the new '985 patent (US 7,599,985, granted Oct 2009). Defendant count varies: Wikipedia says 22 but lists 23; Mullin says "20 big companies". — [Wikipedia: Eolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eolas); [Mullin 2013 repost](https://attrition.org/pipermail/infowarrior/2013-July/010311.html)
- Berners-Lee testified Tuesday 7 Feb 2012, "for the first time in his life", before "a jury of two men and six women". Asked why he came: "I am here because I want to help get some clarity over what was obvious, and what was the feeling of computing [in the early 1990s].... The tools I had in my knapsack, so to speak". On Viola: "an important part of the development of the web"; "It was ahead of its time ... The things Pei was doing would later be done in Java." — [Wired, Mullin, 8 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/tim-berners-lee-patent/)
- Cross-examination by Mike McKool used Berners-Lee's 2004 slides against him: "What you say ... is that Europe should stay clear of the mess the U.S.A. is in." Berners-Lee: "I had concerns about the software patent system in the U.S., and this particular patent is key in raising those concerns". Afterwards, to the reporter: "Oh.... I can't, I just can't". — [Wired, Mullin, 8 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/tim-berners-lee-patent/)
- Other witnesses: Eric Bina (Wei's demonstration "inspired me"), Dave Raggett ("People were still interested in embedded objects ... But it was just one of many features … it was de-prioritized."), Scott Silvey, Wei; a court filing also lists Karl Jacob (search-result summary only, not opened). — [Wired, Mullin, 9 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/texas-jury/)
- Evidence fight: "Eolas lawyers attacked that testimony because the code couldn't be found any longer." The defence found near-identical code dated 12 May 1993 after trawling "through almost 100 gigabytes of backup tapes"; Judge Davis "ruled that the five-day-late code ... wasn't good enough. The jury never saw the V-Plot video." (Wired's sentence says the code dated from "less than a week after Doyle's team filed its patent", which is garbled; five days after the 7 May demo is what the court record supports.) — [Wired, Mullin, 9 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/texas-jury/); [399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html)
- Closing arguments: Eolas's Doug Cawley: "More than 25 different patent examiners spent a decade and a half studying the patent". Defence lawyer Jennifer Doan: "You know how many times the patent office considered the patent [during one reexam]? Two days. That's 1.7 minutes per reference." — [Wired, Mullin, 9 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/texas-jury/)
- Verdict, Thursday 9 Feb 2012: the jury "deliberated Thursday for just a few hours before concluding that all of Eolas' asserted claims ... were invalid"; three scheduled infringement and damages trials were cancelled; Eolas would have sought "more than $600 million". JCPenney's spokeswoman: "We also want to thank the true inventors who traveled from all over the world to testify". Settled earlier: Apple, Argosy Publishing, Blockbuster, Citigroup, eBay, Frito-Lay, JP Morgan Chase, New Frontier Media, Office Depot, Perot Systems, Playboy, Rent-A-Center, Sun Microsystems, Texas Instruments. — [Wired, Mullin, 9 Feb 2012 (verdict story)](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/interactive-web-patent/)
- **Disagreement on who held out:** the 2012 verdict story says "eight defendant companies" resisted and names Google, YouTube, Yahoo, Amazon, JC Penney, CDW and Staples; Mullin in 2013 wrote "By the end of the 2012 jury trial, only Google, Yahoo and JC Penney had not struck deals". One search-result summary dated the verdict 8 Feb; Wired's on-the-scene reporting says Thursday (9 Feb). — [Wired verdict story](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/interactive-web-patent/); [Mullin 2013 repost](https://attrition.org/pipermail/infowarrior/2013-July/010311.html)
- Appeal: "this morning, a three-judge appeals panel affirmed the jury's verdict without comment" (article dated 22 July 2013; Wikipedia says 23 July 2013). Pending suits against Disney, ESPN, ABC, Facebook and Wal-Mart were "all but doomed". — [Mullin 2013 repost](https://attrition.org/pipermail/infowarrior/2013-July/010311.html)
- The UC irony, in Mullin's words: UC's licensing lawyer said the university "stands by its licensees", while "two UC Berkeley-trained computer scientists were key witnesses in the effort to demolish the Eolas patents." — [Mullin 2013 repost](https://attrition.org/pipermail/infowarrior/2013-July/010311.html)
- Afterlife of Eolas (Wikipedia only, not checked against primaries): a third patent, US 9,195,507, issued Nov 2015; suits against Google, Amazon and Walmart; invalidated in 2022 as abstract; Supreme Court declined review October 2024; all patents expired by September 2017. — [Wikipedia: Eolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eolas)

**What became of Wei**
- Wikipedia stub: born in Pingtung County, Taiwan; Berkeley High School 1986 (flagged "citation needed"); UC Berkeley, XCF member; "a founding employee of Global Network Navigator"; later "various Palm OS-related businesses"; from 2008 lived in Taiwan and the US "devoting most of his time to taking care of his ill family member"; "d. 2023". — [Wikipedia: Pei-Yuan Wei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pei-Yuan_Wei)
- The death passed almost unnoticed. Colby Russell, 15 Sep 2023: "I came across a post by the Computer History Museum from some months ago announcing that Pei-Yuan Wei ... has died ... I'm subscribed to the list and didn't see it mentioned (nor anywhere else at the time of the announcement)". — [public-webhistory, Sep 2023](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-webhistory/2023Sep/0001.html)

### Inferences
- Two-sentence test passes: Wei wanted a HyperCard-like programmable web and gave the code away; Doyle patented the same idea, Wei objected in public in 1995, and it took 17 years, two trials and a half-billion-dollar verdict before a jury was allowed to hear Wei's evidence and agree.
- The turn has a date: 2 March 2005 (Federal Circuit says Wei's improved version did not "abandon" the first). A second turn: 9 Feb 2012.
- The 2003 jury never saw the Sun demo evidence; the 2012 jury never saw the V-Plot video. Both times the same five-day gap (demo 7 May, surviving file 12 May 1993) was the weak point. Wei had named that weakness in 1995: "Wish I had kept better records".
- JavaScript angle for the audience: IE's click-to-activate applied only to markup-loaded controls, not script-injected ones, which is why a generation of pages embedded Flash through an external script. That is a concrete artifact the readers typed. (The SWFObject connection is my inference; I did not open a source tying SWFObject's adoption to Eolas.)
- Berners-Lee's FAQ line that Wei "has passed though history unnoticed" was borne out at the end: a museum LinkedIn post and a late list message appear to be the public record of the death.

## 2. W3C patent policy, 2001 to 2004: trigger, draft, backlash, reversal

### Takeaway
The record supports a sharp story with a nameable turn: a draft published quietly on 16 August 2001 drew about two public comments in six weeks, then 617 in a single day (Sunday 30 September 2001, the deadline) after Adam Warner's alarm circulated; W3C extended the deadline, seated Eben Moglen and Bruce Perens within two weeks, and by 26 February 2002 the RAND track was gone. The weaker elements are the protagonist (it is a crowd plus a chairman, Daniel Weitzner) and attribution of which companies argued for RAND, which W3C's public summaries deliberately leave unnamed.

### Cited Findings

**Trigger**
- W3C's own explanation: "W3C has already found itself in a situation where implementers of a W3C Working Draft (the P3P specification) faced possible patent infringement litigation by a patent holder who had participated in the Working Group developing P3P. The immediate result was to bring P3P development to a halt. W3C then made a call to the developer community for prior art, and conducted a patent analysis." — [W3C Response to Public Comments, Oct 2001](https://www.w3.org/2001/10/patent-response)
- The P3P analysis: W3C Note of 27 Oct 1999 on "Intermind's U.S. Patent No. 5,862,325", written by Barry D. Rein and colleagues at Pennie & Edmonds, edited by Joseph Reagle and Daniel Weitzner. — [Analysis of P3P and US Patent 5,862,325](https://www.w3.org/TR/P3P-analysis)
- The draft's own background cites the State Street decision, Amazon v. Barnesandnoble.com, and licensing trouble at W3C, IETF and the WAP Forum; it does not name Intermind. (Summarised by a fetch tool, not read line by line.) — [W3C Patent Policy Framework WD, 16 Aug 2001](https://www.w3.org/TR/2001/WD-patent-policy-20010816/)
- Berners-Lee's 1999 view, which W3C posted during the fight: patents "are a great stumbling block for Web development"; "it would have been unthinkable for a player to ask fees just for implementing a standard protocol such as HTTP"; "like patenting going shopping in a striped automobile on a Thursday." — [Weaving the Web excerpt, ch. 13](https://www.w3.org/2001/10/Patents-wtw-excerpt.html)

**The 16 August 2001 Last Call draft**
- Editor Daniel J. Weitzner (W3C/MIT). Authors: Michele Herman (Microsoft), Scott Peterson (Hewlett-Packard), Tony Piotrowski (Philips), Barry Rein (Pennie & Edmonds, for W3C), Weitzner, Helene Plotka Workman (Apple). Working group members also from IBM, Reuters, Sun, ILOG, Nortel, The Open Group. Last Call to close 30 September 2001. It defined two licensing modes per working group charter, RF or RAND, and a membership-wide commitment to "license all Essential Claims needed to implement W3C Recommendations on RAND terms". — [WD-patent-policy-20010816](https://www.w3.org/TR/2001/WD-patent-policy-20010816/)
- W3C's own gloss: "The policy defines two licensing modes for a Working Group: Royalty-Free and RAND." — [W3C Response to Public Comments](https://www.w3.org/2001/10/patent-response)

**How the public found out, and the numbers**
- Adam Warner (New Zealand, adam@consulting.net.nz) to LWN, dated 29 Sep 2001 09:36 +1200: "The last call review period closes on 30 September 2001 (two days from the time I am writing this abstract)"; "as evidence of how well this issue has been publicised, only two relevant public comments have been made to the W3C archive to date." Title: "W3C and the Promotion of Fee-based Standards for the Web". — [LWN, Warner letter](https://lwn.net/2001/1004/a/w3c-patents.php3)
- My count from the archive's by-date index for September 2001: 24 Sep, 1 message; 27 Sep, 1; 28 Sep, 2 (one is Warner's); 29 Sep, 15; 30 Sep, 617; filed under 1 Oct in the September index, 114. Page total "750 messages" (the list's top index says 755). — [www-patentpolicy-comment, September 2001](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Sep/)
- Monthly totals: August 2001, 7; September, 755; October, 1,683; November, 192; December, 79; January 2002, 19; second Last Call: November 2002, 120; December 2002, 95; January 2003, 154. — [www-patentpolicy-comment archive index](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/)
- Weitzner on 12 Oct 2001: "we have a huge (2200+) number of public comments to respond to." — [Weitzner, "Next steps in W3C Patent Policy process"](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Oct/1559.html)
- W3C on the accusation of burying it: "The document was made public six weeks ago, and W3C invited public comment at that time." and "due to the large number of comments received in the past two days". — [W3C Response to Public Comments](https://www.w3.org/2001/10/patent-response)

**Named objectors (all on the public list)**
- Subject lines on 30 Sep and 1 Oct 2001 include posts by Russell Nelson, John Gilmore, Jeremy Allison ("Why Tim Berners-Lee is against the RAND"), Branden Robinson, and "I concur with Alan Cox". (Subjects seen in the index; bodies not opened.) — [September 2001 index](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Sep/)
- Eric S. Raymond for the Open Source Initiative, 9 Oct 2001: "The recent public disclosure of these plans to endorse patented Web standards has ignited a major public controversy"; clause 5 ("may be conditioned on payment of reasonable, non-discriminatory royalties or fees") "is unacceptable as written"; "there can be no question but that it is discriminatory." OSI endorsed the disclosure rules. — [OSI letter](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Oct/1310.html)
- Jeffrey Zeldman, 9 Oct 2001: "lack of clarity in the language has led thousands of developers to fear that they'll have to 'pay royalties to use HTML.'" Also names "Intermind's recent attempt to undermine P3P", and warns that some are "threatening to fork the web". — [Zeldman comment](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Oct/1337.html)
- Håkon Wium Lie for Opera, 10 Oct 2001: "we do not think W3C should describe procedures for RAND licensing. Doing so would help legitimize software patents which we think are harmful to the development of the Web." — [Opera's position](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Oct/1415.html)
- Also in the October index: Mozilla.org response (Christopher Blizzard), EFF comments (Seth David Schoen, 11 Oct), Tim Bray, Dave Winer ("What to do about the W3C?"), a Web Standards Project statement by Steven Champeon (8 Oct, per search result). Bodies not opened. — [October 2001 index](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Oct/)

**A named party that moved: Apple**
- Apple's Helene Plotka Workman was a listed author of the RAND-permitting draft. On 11 Oct 2001 Wanda Cox posted "Apple Computer's Statement on the Draft W3C Patent Policy": "Apple supports a W3C patent policy with an immutable commitment to royalty-free licensing for fundamental Web standards"; "the ready availability of a RAND option presents too easy an alternative for owners of intellectual property who may seek to use the standardization process to control access to fundamental Web standards. A mandatory royalty-free requirement for all adopted standards will avoid this result." Apple also proposed the per-patent opt-out that ended up in the final policy's exclusion mechanism. — [Apple statement](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Oct/1488.html); [WD-patent-policy-20010816](https://www.w3.org/TR/2001/WD-patent-policy-20010816/)
- Context that complicates Apple as hero: the October list has long threads titled "Apple's SVG patent" and "Is Apple's patent valid?" (Chris Lilley replying), suggesting Apple was itself under fire over a RAND claim on SVG. Thread bodies not opened. — [October 2001 index](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Oct/)

**W3C's response and the reversal**
- Deadline extended "until 11 October 2001". — [W3C Response to Public Comments](https://www.w3.org/2001/10/patent-response)
- 12 Oct 2001, Weitzner: "Eben Moglen (General Counsel, Free Software Foundation) and Bruce Perens (Co-Founder of the Open Source Initiative) are joining the Patent Policy Working Group as 'invited experts.' They will participate equally with the W3C Members"; a second Last Call promised; public home page and public meeting summaries; "I'll be doing a Slashdot interview". Larry Rosen (for OSI) was added later as a third invited expert. — [Weitzner, "Next steps"](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Oct/1559.html); [PPWG public home page](https://www.w3.org/2001/ppwg/)
- 15 to 17 Oct 2001, Cupertino face-to-face (Perens present, Moglen and Berners-Lee by phone): "The Working Group did not reach a unanimous position on the correct role for RAND or RF technology choices at W3C." Arguments for and against RAND are recorded without attribution. — [Cupertino meeting summary](https://www.w3.org/2001/10/ppwg-cupertino-ftf-summary.html)
- 24 Oct 2001, Berners-Lee, "Why I have not spoken personally about the patent policy issue": "There have been several strong calls for me to break my silence"; "I am not (contrary to what some of the pundits might suggest! ;-) a dictator by role or nature"; "I wouldn't want people to feel, afterward, that the result was rather less valid because people had been swayed by the over-weighted opinion of the Director." The footnote points to the Weaving the Web patent excerpt. — [Berners-Lee to the list](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Oct/1642.html)
- 24 Jan 2002: "Current Patent Practice" Note sets "Royalty-Free implementation as a goal" and introduces Patent Advisory Groups; the Advisory Board had recommended publication on 22 Jan 2002. — [Current Patent Practice](https://www.w3.org/TR/2002/NOTE-patent-practice-20020124)
- 26 Feb 2002 working draft: "RAND track removed: There is no process in this policy for developing RAND specifications"; "Both public and Member comments had a significant impact on the direction of the policy"; but "many W3C Members feel that there should be a way a deadling with technologies only available on RAND terms". — [Backgrounder, 26 Feb 2002](https://www.w3.org/2002/02/25-pwd-summary)
- 14 Nov 2002 second Last Call, now titled "Royalty-Free Patent Policy": "W3C will not approve a Recommendation if it is aware that Essential Claims exist which are not available on Royalty-Free terms." Resolved "without any formal objection, though one participant disagrees with the direction taken by this policy." Comments until 31 Dec 2002. It carries an addendum: "Text of RAND Exception Process Considered and Rejected by the Patent Policy Working Group". — [WD-patent-policy-20021114](https://www.w3.org/TR/2002/WD-patent-policy-20021114/)
- Approval announced 21 May 2003: "W3C Members, Director Establish Policy for Encouraging Royalty-Free Web Standards". Berners-Lee: "W3C now sets the benchmark for the pragmatic way to successfully develop royalty-free Web Standards in the current patent environment." Weitzner on the surviving exception: "nearly unanimous support is required for such exceptions." InternetNews teaser says the policy was ratified "in a meeting in Budapest Tuesday" (20 May). — [W3C press release, 21 May 2003](https://www.w3.org/2003/05/patentpolicy-pressrelease.html.en); [InternetNews](https://www.internetnews.com/developer/the-royalty-frees-have-it-at-w3c)
- The policy became "operative as of 5 February 2004"; the working group closed 17 December 2004. — [PPWG public home page](https://www.w3.org/2001/ppwg/)

**Link to the Eolas case**
- Same machinery: the HTML PAG was launched "Under W3C Current Patent Practice" on 23 Sep 2003, four months after the policy was approved, because of "specific patent claims likely to be essential to W3C work that are not available on royalty free terms". — [HTML PAG](https://www.w3.org/2003/09/pag)
- Same people: Daniel Weitzner (chair of the Patent Policy WG; contact on the Rogan letter), Alan Kotok (PPWG member; HTML PAG chair), Michele Herman of Microsoft (co-author of the 2001 RAND draft; HTML PAG member), Barry Rein of Pennie & Edmonds (P3P analysis 1999; 2001 draft author; HTML PAG). — [WD-patent-policy-20010816](https://www.w3.org/TR/2001/WD-patent-policy-20010816/); [HTML PAG](https://www.w3.org/2003/09/pag); [P3P analysis](https://www.w3.org/TR/P3P-analysis); [W3C letter](https://www.w3.org/2003/10/27-rogan.html)
- Same playbook: the Eolas FAQ notes a prior-art call "was successfully used in the context of W3C work on P3P patent infringement allegations." — [W3C FAQ on '906](https://www.w3.org/2003/09/public-faq.html)

### Inferences
- Two-sentence test: a working group of patent lawyers from member companies wanted a formal policy that permitted royalty-bearing standards; the draft sat unread for six weeks until one reader raised the alarm two days before the deadline, 617 objections arrived in a day, and W3C ended with a royalty-free rule. It passes, but the "someone who wanted something" is diffuse.
- Best protagonist candidates: Adam Warner (an outsider whose letter appears to have lit the fuse; I could not confirm how it reached Slashdot or Linux Today) or Daniel Weitzner (chaired the group that wrote the RAND draft, then steered it to RF and wrote the Eolas response). Berners-Lee is a striking non-actor: the Director explained on the list why they were staying silent.
- The 2001 fight is the reason W3C had both the standing and the mechanism (PAG) to act against Eolas in 2003. Without an RF commitment, "not available on royalty free terms" would not have been a trigger. This is my connection; no opened source states it.
- Microsoft's arc is the irony that joins the two topics: a Microsoft lawyer co-wrote the draft that would have allowed royalty-bearing web standards (2001), and Microsoft was then the defendant ordered to pay $520.6 million over a web patent (2003), with the royalty-free W3C lobbying the USPTO on the web's behalf. (Whether Microsoft argued for RAND inside the group is NOT documented in anything I opened.)

### Gaps
- Which companies favoured RAND: W3C's public summaries do not attribute positions; member-only archives are closed. Press of the time (not opened) commonly named IBM and Microsoft; treat as unverified. Hewlett-Packard's reported pro-RF statement was not found or opened.
- How Warner's letter spread (Slashdot, Linux Today, LWN front page of 4 Oct 2001) and the exact trigger for the 30 September flood: not traced. The Slashdot story Weitzner links (sid=01/10/08/1459240) was not opened.
- The Director's Decision of May 2003 (www.w3.org/2003/05/12-director-patent-decision.html) now returns a login page; Berners-Lee's reasoning and the Advisory Committee vote counts were not read.
- November 2001 Advisory Committee meeting and its "Action Item to develop an RF Patent Policy": referenced by the Feb 2002 backgrounder but the summary page was not found (404).
- Identity of the "one participant" who disagreed in November 2002: not public.
- Whether Moglen, Perens or Rosen made a specific decisive intervention: not established.

## 3. How well covered is each story, and is there an unwritten angle?

### Takeaway
Eolas is well covered as a patent-troll news story (2003 and 2012) but thin as a story about Wei; the W3C policy fight is barely told outside its own archives. The connection between the two (same people, same mechanism, 2001 leading to 2003) does not appear in anything I found.

### Cited Findings
- Eolas/Viola existing tellings found: Joe Mullin's courtroom series (Wired/Ars, Feb 2012) and "The Web's longest nightmare ends" (Ars, 22 Jul 2013); EFF's "Why the Patent System Doesn't Play Well with Software" (2012); Gillies and Cailliau, *How the Web Was Born* (Viola pages); Berners-Lee, *Weaving the Web*; Ars Technica "Before Netscape: the forgotten Web browsers of the early 1990s" (2019); The History of the Web's ViolaWWW milestone; a 2022 Miriam Suzanne post on ancient browsers and stylesheets; Wikipedia articles. Only the first two were opened. — [Wired, Mullin](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/tim-berners-lee-patent/); [Mullin 2013 repost](https://attrition.org/pipermail/infowarrior/2013-July/010311.html); [Wikipedia: ViolaWWW references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ViolaWWW)
- Mullin's framing is the troll and the trial; Wei is a witness in it, paraphrased, never quoted from the stand. — [Wired, Mullin, 9 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/texas-jury/)
- W3C policy fight: searches returned only contemporary items (LWN, Linux Today, InternetNews, OSI press release, list posts) and one 2013 list summary by Karl Dubost; no long-form retrospective, book chapter or podcast surfaced. — [LWN](https://lwn.net/2001/1004/a/w3c-patents.php3); [InternetNews](https://www.internetnews.com/developer/the-royalty-frees-have-it-at-w3c)
- Wei's death in 2023 drew almost no notice, per the public-webhistory post. — [public-webhistory](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-webhistory/2023Sep/0001.html)

### Inferences
- Under-reported angles for (A): (1) the 21 August 1995 www-talk exchange read as the opening scene, with everything after as its consequence; (2) the same five-day evidence gap deciding what two juries could see; (3) click-to-activate as the developer-facing artifact, and why script-injected embeds were exempt; (4) UC on both sides; (5) Wei's quiet exit and unremarked death against Berners-Lee's "passed though history unnoticed".
- Under-reported angle for (B): the day-by-day comment counts (2, then 15, then 617) have not, as far as I can find, been published anywhere; they come straight from the archive index.
- Strongest unwritten piece may be a single essay with (A) as the story and (B) as its "Why" section: the web had just decided, by public revolt, that its standards must be royalty-free, when a court said one patent covered every plugin. That would use (B)'s best facts without needing (B) to carry a protagonist.
- Verdict on strength: (A) strong story, moderately known in outline, little known in detail. (B) strong turn and numbers, weak protagonist, very little known; risk of reading as a report unless anchored on Warner or Weitzner.

### Gaps
- "No long-form retelling exists" is an absence-of-evidence claim from three searches; books on W3C or standards history (and Weitzner's or Rosen's own later writing, for example Rosen's *Open Source Licensing*, 2004) were not checked.
- Did not check podcasts or conference talks.

## 4. The single most surprising documented fact for each

### Takeaway
(A): Wei told Doyle, in public on www-talk on the day Eolas announced its licence in 1995, that Viola had done this first, and Doyle had read Wei's paper before filing; yet the first jury was barred from hearing it and awarded $520,562,280. (B): the proposal to allow royalties on web standards drew about two comments in six weeks and 617 on its final day.

### Cited Findings
- (A) "I sincerely hope this patent isn't going to stick, for the good of the web as a whole..." (Wei, 21 Aug 1995). — [www-talk](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-talk/1995JulAug/0429.html)
- (A) Doyle "downloaded and read the paper" in 1994, kept a "Viola stuff" folder in 1998, and told the PTO nothing; the trial judge excluded the demo because Wei "then changed it". — [399 F.3d 1325](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/399/399.F3d.1325.04-1234.html)
- (A) runner-up: Doyle under oath in 2012: "We were excited to use Mosaic and Viola as building blocks"; Bina counted 334 added lines. — [Wired, Mullin, 9 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/texas-jury/)
- (A) runner-up: Berners-Lee's first time ever testifying in court was for this, and the cross-examination turned their own anti-software-patent slides against them. — [Wired, Mullin, 8 Feb 2012](https://www.wired.com/2012/02/tim-berners-lee-patent/)
- (B) "only two relevant public comments have been made to the W3C archive to date" (Warner, 28/29 Sep 2001); archive shows 617 messages dated 30 Sep 2001 and 1,683 in October. — [LWN](https://lwn.net/2001/1004/a/w3c-patents.php3); [September 2001 index](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Sep/); [archive index](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/)
- (B) runner-up: Apple co-authored the RAND draft and eight weeks later demanded "A mandatory royalty-free requirement for all adopted standards". — [Apple statement](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Oct/1488.html)
- (B) runner-up: the Director of W3C wrote to the list to explain a deliberate silence. — [Berners-Lee to the list](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-patentpolicy-comment/2001Oct/1642.html)

### Inferences
- Both surprises are checkable by a reader in one click, which suits the essay's "link your sources" rule.
- The 617 figure is my own count of index entries under the heading "Sunday, 30 September 2001" (UTC received dates); an essayist should recount before printing it, and should say "about 600" if in doubt.

### Gaps
- Whether Wei's 1994 email to Doyle survives in public (it would be in trial exhibits) is unknown.
- No source opened says how Wei felt about the 2012 outcome.
